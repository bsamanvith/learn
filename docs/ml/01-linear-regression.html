Lesson 01 · Stage 1: learning from data

Linear regression

The simplest model that learns from data, and the one every other model is measured against. We build it three ways: as geometry, as calculus, and as probability. All three give the same answer, and that agreement is the first big idea of machine learning.

The problem

We have $n$ examples. Each has an input $x_i$ (a house's floor area) and a real-valued output $y_i$ (its price). We want a rule $f$ that predicts $y$ for an $x$ it has never seen. Written as a dataset:

$$\mathcal{D} = \{(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\}, \qquad x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d,\; y_i \in \mathbb{R}.$$

Predicting a number is called regression. Predicting a category is classification (lesson 03). Everything in this lesson works for $d$ inputs at once, but we draw $d = 1$ so you can see it.

The model

The simplest rule that can possibly work is a straight line:

$$\hat y = w x + b.$$
  • $w$, the weight, is the slope: how much $\hat y$ rises per unit of $x$.
  • $b$, the bias or intercept, is the prediction when $x = 0$.
  • $\hat y$ ("y-hat") is the model's guess. $y$ is the truth.

The numbers $w$ and $b$ are the model's parameters. Learning means choosing them well. With $d$ inputs the model is a weighted sum, written as a dot product:

$$\hat y = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_d x_d + b = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b.$$
Math detour · dot product and matrix form

$\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} = \sum_j w_j x_j$: multiply matching entries and add. It is the most common operation in deep learning; a neuron is a dot product plus a bias, passed through a squashing function.

Stack the inputs as rows of a design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ and every prediction at once is a matrix-vector product, $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{w} + b\mathbf{1}$. The usual trick is to absorb the bias: add a column of ones to $X$ and append $b$ to $\mathbf{w}$. Then simply

$$\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{w}, \qquad X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (d+1)},\; \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}.$$

From here on, "$X\mathbf{w}$" includes the bias.

Measuring error: the loss

For one example, the residual is $r_i = \hat y_i - y_i$. To score the whole line with one number, square each residual and average:

$$J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\hat y_i - y_i\right)^2 = \frac{1}{n}\,\lVert X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\rVert^2 .$$

This is the mean squared error (MSE). Squaring does three jobs. Positive and negative errors can't cancel. Big errors cost far more than small ones (an error of 3 costs 9). And the result is a smooth quadratic, which we can minimise exactly. There is also a deeper reason, which section 7 derives: squared error is what you get if you assume the noise is Gaussian.

Squared error, made literal: each orange square has side $|r_i|$, so its area is $r_i^2$, and the MSE is the average area. Drag $w$ and $b$ and watch big squares dominate. That is why a single outlier can drag a least-squares line toward it.

The loss is a bowl

Each choice of parameters gives one line and one loss value. So $J$ is a surface over parameter space, and the best line is its lowest point. Expanding the square shows its shape:

$$J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbf{w}^\top X^\top X\,\mathbf{w} - 2\,\mathbf{y}^\top X\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{y}\right).$$

That is a quadratic in $\mathbf{w}$. Its curvature is the Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives:

$$\nabla^2 J = \frac{2}{n} X^\top X.$$

For any vector $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{v}^\top X^\top X\,\mathbf{v} = \lVert X\mathbf{v}\rVert^2 \ge 0$. So the Hessian is positive semidefinite, the surface curves upward in every direction, and $J$ is convex: a bowl with no false bottoms. Any point where the slope is zero is the global minimum. The gradient descent lab draws this bowl for $(w, b)$; click anywhere on its map to see the line that point stands for.

Solving it exactly: the normal equations

At the bottom of a convex bowl the gradient is zero. Differentiate $J$ (lesson 02 explains gradients in full; here we only need the result):

$$\nabla J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{2}{n}\,X^\top\!\left(X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\right).$$

Set it to zero and rearrange:

The normal equations
$$X^\top X\,\mathbf{w}^* = X^\top \mathbf{y} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{w}^* = \left(X^\top X\right)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}\quad \text{(when } X^\top X \text{ is invertible).}$$

The one-input case, by hand

With a single input the two equations $\partial J/\partial b = 0$ and $\partial J/\partial w = 0$ read

$$\sum_i (w x_i + b - y_i) = 0, \qquad \sum_i (w x_i + b - y_i)\,x_i = 0.$$

The first gives $b = \bar y - w\bar x$: the best line always passes through the point of means $(\bar x, \bar y)$. Substituting into the second and dividing by $n$:

$$w^* = \frac{\tfrac1n\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\tfrac1n\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x, y)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)}, \qquad b^* = \bar y - w^*\bar x.$$

Read the slope as "how much $x$ and $y$ move together, divided by how much $x$ moves on its own".

Math detour · mean, variance, covariance

Mean $\bar x = \frac1n\sum x_i$. Variance $\operatorname{Var}(x) = \frac1n\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2$, the average squared distance from the mean. Covariance $\operatorname{Cov}(x,y) = \frac1n\sum (x_i-\bar x)(y_i - \bar y)$: positive when $x$ and $y$ tend to be above their means together. Dividing by $n$ or $n-1$ changes nothing here, because the factor cancels in the ratio.

The geometry: projection

Rewrite the normal equations as $X^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}^*) = \mathbf{0}$. In words: the residual vector is orthogonal to every column of $X$.

Think of $\mathbf{y}$ as one point in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Every possible prediction $X\mathbf{w}$ lives in the column space of $X$, a flat subspace. The closest point of that subspace to $\mathbf{y}$ is the foot of the perpendicular, the orthogonal projection:

$$\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\left(X^\top X\right)^{-1}X^\top\,\mathbf{y} = H\mathbf{y}.$$

$H$ is called the hat matrix, because it puts the hat on $\mathbf{y}$. Two consequences you can check on any least-squares fit with a bias term:

  • The residuals sum to zero (orthogonal to the column of ones).
  • The residuals have zero correlation with every input (orthogonal to each feature column).

So least squares is not an arbitrary recipe. It is "drop a perpendicular", the same idea as finding the shortest distance from a point to a plane.

The probability: maximum likelihood

Now assume the data really were made by a line plus random noise:

$$y_i = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\ \text{independent}.$$
Math detour · the Gaussian and likelihood

The Gaussian density is $\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon; 0, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$. The likelihood of parameters is the probability (density) the model assigns to the data we actually saw. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) picks the parameters that make the observed data most probable.

Independence means the likelihood is a product. Products are awkward, so take logs, which turns the product into a sum:

$$\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid X, \mathbf{w}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log \mathcal{N}(y_i;\, \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_i - \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i\right)^2 .$$

The first term does not depend on $\mathbf{w}$. So maximising the log-likelihood over $\mathbf{w}$ is exactly minimising the sum of squared errors. Setting the derivative with respect to $\sigma^2$ to zero also gives the noise estimate: $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n\sum_i r_i^2$, which is just the MSE.

The pattern to remember

Choose a noise model, write the likelihood, take $-\log$: out falls the loss. Gaussian noise gives squared error. Lesson 03 does the same with coin flips and gets log-loss. Almost every loss in deep learning, including the one that trains LLMs, comes from this recipe.

How good is the fit?

A model that ignores $x$ and always predicts $\bar y$ has MSE equal to $\operatorname{Var}(y)$. That is the bar to beat. The coefficient of determination measures the fraction of variance the model explains:

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2} = 1 - \frac{\text{MSE}}{\operatorname{Var}(y)}.$$

$R^2 = 1$ is a perfect fit; $R^2 = 0$ is no better than the mean. On training data with a bias term, $R^2 \in [0, 1]$. On new data it can go negative, which is your first warning about overfitting (lesson 04).

When the formula breaks

  • $X^\top X$ is not invertible. This happens when features are collinear (one is a combination of others) or when there are more features than examples ($d \ge n$). There are then infinitely many minimisers. The pseudo-inverse picks the smallest one, and ridge regression fixes it by adding a penalty: $\mathbf{w}^* = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}$ (lesson 05).
  • It is expensive. Forming $X^\top X$ costs $O(nd^2)$ and solving costs $O(d^3)$. With a million features that is hopeless.
  • It only exists for this model. Change the loss (lesson 03) or add a nonlinearity (neural nets) and no closed form exists.

All three point to the same tool: start somewhere, and walk downhill. That is lesson 02, gradient descent.

Code from scratch

import numpy as np

x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
y = np.array([2.0, 4.0, 5.0])

# One input: the covariance formula.
w = np.mean((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean())) / np.mean((x - x.mean()) ** 2)
b = y.mean() - w * x.mean()
print(w, b)                      # 1.5 0.6667

# Any number of inputs: the normal equations, bias absorbed as a column of ones.
X = np.column_stack([x, np.ones_like(x)])
w_vec = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)   # never form the inverse explicitly
print(w_vec)                     # [1.5 0.6667]

r = X @ w_vec - y
print(r.sum(), (r * x).sum())    # both ~0: residuals are orthogonal to the columns
print(1 - np.mean(r**2) / np.var(y))         # R^2 = 0.964

In practice use np.linalg.lstsq(X, y), which solves the same problem through a QR or SVD factorisation and stays accurate when $X^\top X$ is nearly singular.

Exercises

Use this dataset: $x = (1, 2, 3)$, $y = (2, 4, 5)$.

  1. Compute the MSE of the line $\hat y = x + 1$.
  2. Use $w^* = \operatorname{Cov}(x,y)/\operatorname{Var}(x)$ and $b^* = \bar y - w^*\bar x$ to find the best line, then its MSE and $R^2$.
  3. Derive the normal equations: expand $\lVert X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\rVert^2$, differentiate with respect to $\mathbf{w}$, and set the result to zero.
  4. Prove that with a bias term the residuals of the least-squares fit sum to zero.
  5. Show that $H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ satisfies $H^2 = H$ and $H^\top = H$. Why does a projection have to be idempotent?
  6. Assume Laplace noise, $p(\varepsilon) \propto e^{-|\varepsilon|/s}$, instead of Gaussian. Which loss does maximum likelihood give? What does it do to outliers compared with squared error?
  7. Duplicate a feature column in $X$. What happens to $X^\top X$, and to the set of solutions?
Answers

1. Predictions 2, 3, 4; residuals 0, −1, −1; MSE $= 2/3$.

2. $\bar x = 2$, $\bar y = 11/3$, $\operatorname{Cov} = 1$, $\operatorname{Var}(x) = 2/3$, so $w^* = 1.5$, $b^* = 2/3$. Residuals $\tfrac16, -\tfrac13, \tfrac16$, MSE $= 1/18 \approx 0.056$. $\operatorname{Var}(y) = 14/9$, so $R^2 = 1 - \tfrac{1}{28} \approx 0.964$.

3. $\lVert X\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}\rVert^2 = \mathbf{w}^\top X^\top X\mathbf{w} - 2\mathbf{y}^\top X\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{y}$. Using $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w}^\top A\mathbf{w}) = 2A\mathbf{w}$ for symmetric $A$ and $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{c}^\top\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{c}$, the gradient is $2X^\top X\mathbf{w} - 2X^\top\mathbf{y}$. Setting it to zero gives $X^\top X\mathbf{w} = X^\top\mathbf{y}$.

4. The normal equations say $X^\top \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{0}$. The row of $X^\top$ that comes from the column of ones gives $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{r} = \sum_i r_i = 0$.

5. $H^2 = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top = H$. Symmetry follows from $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ being symmetric. Projecting something that is already in the subspace leaves it where it is, so projecting twice equals projecting once.

6. $-\log p$ gives $\sum_i |r_i|$, the mean absolute error. Its penalty grows linearly, not quadratically, so outliers pull the fit far less. The minimiser of absolute error is a median, not a mean.

7. Two identical columns make $X^\top X$ singular (its determinant is zero). Any split of the weight between the two copies gives the same predictions, so there are infinitely many minimisers.
