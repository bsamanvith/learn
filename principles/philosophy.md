# Philosophy

## The one-line version

> **Fun to read, deep enough to trust, and you can't finish a page without doing something.**

## What we believe

1. **Start with a puzzle, not a definition.** Every idea was invented to solve a problem.
   Show the problem first (a paradox, a bet, a surprising number) so the definition
   arrives as a relief, not a rule.

2. **Intuition first, rigour right after. Never only one.** A picture without a proof is a
   story; a proof without a picture is a password. We want both, in that order.

3. **Doing beats reading.** Understanding is tested by producing an answer, not by
   recognising one. Every concept has a problem on the same page.

4. **Seeing is a tool, not decoration.** A visual must answer a question you could state
   in words. If it doesn't, cut it. Interactive is better than static when there is a
   parameter worth wiggling.

5. **Go deep, but build the ladder.** "Deep" means we eventually reach the real thing
   (measure theory, martingales, proofs) — but every rung is small enough to climb.
   Nothing appears before its prerequisites; everything links back to them.

6. **Simulate when in doubt.** For anything random, a 10-line simulation is a second
   opinion that never lies. Theory predicts, simulation checks.

7. **Mistakes are content.** The wrong answer most people give, and *why* it's tempting,
   is often the most valuable paragraph on the page.

8. **Connections over coverage.** A concept is only learned once it's linked to two
   others. Every chapter says where it came from and where it goes next.

9. **Revisit on purpose.** Old ideas come back in new chapters' problems. Forgetting is
   expected; we design against it (see [learning-science.md](learning-science.md)).

10. **It changes.** Subjects differ (see [subject-modes.md](subject-modes.md)), and these
    principles get revised as we learn what works for us.

## What "done" looks like for a chapter

- A reader who skipped the text could solve the core problems after only playing with
  the visuals and reading the worked examples — and a reader who skipped the visuals
  could still follow every proof.
- Every problem has a full solution.
- At least one thing on the page made you go "oh, *that's* why."
