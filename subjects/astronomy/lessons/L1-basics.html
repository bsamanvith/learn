Level 1: basics

Twenty-two first problems about the sky and the things in it: how far, how big, how bright, how heavy, how hot. Each needs only arithmetic, a little algebra and a formula given in the problem or the solution. The aim is to build a feeling for astronomical numbers, which are so large that the only way to grasp them is to compare them with something you know. Work them in order; the solutions explain every step.

Distance and size

  1. keywarm-up (Looking back in time.) Light travels at $3.00\times10^8$ m/s. How long does it take to reach us from the Moon ($3.84\times10^8$ m away) and from the Sun ($1.50\times10^{11}$ m)? The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is 4.24 light-years away: what does that mean?

    Solution

    Time is distance over speed: from the Moon, $3.84\times10^8/3.00\times10^8 = 1.3$ s; from the Sun, $1.50\times10^{11}/3.00\times10^8 = 500$ s, or 8.3 minutes. If the Sun vanished, we would not know for eight minutes. A light-year is a distance, the distance light travels in a year, so Proxima's light set out 4.24 years ago: we see it as it was then. Every look into the sky is a look into the past, and the deeper we look, the further back we see.

  2. warm-up (A scale model.) Shrink the Sun (diameter $1.39\times10^9$ m) to a 30 cm football. How big is the Earth (diameter 12 742 km), and how far from the ball is it (1 AU $= 1.50\times10^{11}$ m)? Where is Neptune (30 AU)? Where is Proxima Centauri ($4.02\times10^{16}$ m)?

    Solution

    The scale factor is $0.30/1.39\times10^9 = 2.2\times10^{-10}$. Multiply every real length by it. The Earth becomes 2.7 mm, a peppercorn, 32 m from the ball. Neptune is almost a kilometre away, and Proxima Centauri, another football, is 8700 km away: in another continent. Space is mostly empty, and the stars are much further from each other than the planets are from their stars. That is why stars almost never collide, even when whole galaxies do.

  3. warm-up (How big does the Moon look?) The Moon's diameter is 3474 km and its distance 384 400 km. What angle does it cover in the sky? For a small angle, $\theta \approx \text{size}/\text{distance}$ in radians, and $1\text{ rad} = 57.3^\circ$.

    Solution

    $\theta = 3474/384\,400 = 0.00904$ rad $= 0.52^\circ$. A fingernail held at arm's length is about $1^\circ$ across, so it covers the Moon twice over. The Sun is 400 times larger and 400 times further, so it looks the same size, which is why total solar eclipses are possible (problem 4 of P1 looks at that coincidence). The Moon low on the horizon looks bigger, but measure it and it is the same: that is an illusion of the brain, not the sky.

  4. warm-up (How far is the horizon?) Standing with your eyes 1.7 m above a calm sea, how far away is the horizon? The Earth's radius is 6371 km. What about from a 100 m cliff?

    Solution

    Your line of sight just touches the Earth at the horizon, so it is at right angles to the radius there. Pythagoras, with the centre of the Earth, the horizon and your eye: $d^2 + R^2 = (R + h)^2$, so $d^2 = 2Rh + h^2 \approx 2Rh$, since $h \ll R$. From 1.7 m, $d = \sqrt{2\times6.371\times10^6\times1.7} = 4.7$ km. From 100 m, 36 km. Distance grows only as $\sqrt h$, which is why ships' lookouts sat at the top of the mast and lighthouses are tall.

  5. keywarm-up (Eratosthenes measures the Earth.) Around 240 BC, Eratosthenes learned that at noon on midsummer's day the Sun shone straight down a well at Syene, while at Alexandria, about 800 km to the north, a vertical stick cast a shadow showing the Sun $7.2^\circ$ from overhead. What circumference did he deduce, and what did he assume?

    Solution

    He assumed the Sun is so far away that its rays arrive parallel, and that the Earth is a sphere. Then the $7.2^\circ$ difference in the Sun's angle is the angle between the two places, measured at the centre of the Earth. $7.2^\circ$ is $1/50$ of a full circle, so the circumference is $50\times800 = 40\,000$ km. The true value is 40 008 km round the poles. With one stick, one angle and one distance he measured a planet, which is what astronomy has done ever since: measure what you can reach, then use geometry for the rest.

  6. warm-up (The seasons.) The Earth's axis is tilted $23.4^\circ$. At latitude $52^\circ$ N (London, Berlin), how high is the Sun at noon on the summer solstice, and on the winter solstice? Why is summer warmer, given that the Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January?

    Solution

    At the equinoxes the noon Sun stands $90^\circ - 52^\circ = 38^\circ$ high. At the solstices it is $23.4^\circ$ higher or lower: $61.4^\circ$ in June and $14.6^\circ$ in December. Sunlight hitting the ground at a low angle spreads over a larger area, so the energy per square metre goes as $\sin(\text{altitude})$: in winter it is $\sin14.6^\circ/\sin61.4^\circ = 0.29$ of summer's. Add short days and a long path through the air, and winter is cold. The Earth's distance changes by only 3%, worth about 7% in sunlight, much less than the effect of the tilt, which is also why the southern hemisphere has winter in July.

  7. warm-up (Finding your latitude.) Explain why the height of the Pole Star (Polaris) above the horizon equals your latitude. Where would you have to be to see it overhead, and where does it disappear?

    Solution

    Polaris lies almost exactly along the Earth's axis, and is so far away that its direction is the same from everywhere on Earth. At the North Pole the axis points straight up, so Polaris is overhead. At latitude $\phi$, your "up" is tilted by $90^\circ - \phi$ from the axis, so Polaris stands $\phi$ above the horizon. At the equator it sits on the horizon, and south of the equator it is hidden. Sailors used this for centuries to find their latitude; longitude was much harder, and needed an accurate clock.

The Moon and the planets

  1. warm-up (Moonrise.) The Moon goes once round the Earth relative to the stars in 27.3 days, in the same direction as the Earth turns. Show that it rises about 50 minutes later each day. At roughly what time does the full Moon rise, and the first-quarter Moon?

    Solution

    The Moon moves east among the stars by $360^\circ/27.3 = 13.2^\circ$ a day, and the Sun moves east by about $1^\circ$ a day, so the Moon gains $12.2^\circ$ a day on the Sun. The Earth turns $15^\circ$ an hour, so the Earth needs an extra $12.2/15$ h, about 49 minutes, to bring the Moon back over the horizon. (The actual delay varies from under 20 minutes to over an hour with the season and latitude.) A full Moon is opposite the Sun, so it rises at sunset; a first-quarter Moon is $90^\circ$ east of the Sun, so it rises around noon and is highest at sunset.

  2. keywarm-up (Kepler's third law.) For planets going round the Sun, $T^2 = a^3$ when the period $T$ is in years and the orbit's size $a$ is in astronomical units. Find the periods of Mars ($a = 1.524$ AU) and Jupiter ($a = 5.20$ AU). How far out is an asteroid with a 4-year period?

    Solution

    $T = a^{3/2}$: Mars takes $1.524^{1.5} = 1.88$ years and Jupiter $5.20^{1.5} = 11.9$ years. For the asteroid, $a = T^{2/3} = 4^{2/3} = 2.52$ AU, in the asteroid belt. This law, found by Kepler from Tycho's observations in 1619, was the clue that led Newton to the inverse-square law of gravity: it holds exactly in that form only for gravity that falls as $1/r^2$. Level 2 derives it.

  3. warm-up (How fast are we moving?) The Earth goes round the Sun in a nearly circular orbit of radius $1.50\times10^{11}$ m once a year ($3.16\times10^7$ s). How fast is it moving? How does that compare with the speed of a point on the equator due to the Earth's spin?

    Solution

    The circumference is $2\pi\times1.50\times10^{11} = 9.4\times10^{11}$ m, so the speed is $9.4\times10^{11}/3.16\times10^7 = 29.8$ km/s, over 100 000 km/h. The equator moves $2\pi\times6371$ km per day, which is 0.46 km/s. We don't feel either because both motions are steady: only changes in velocity, accelerations, can be felt, and both of these are tiny. The Sun in turn carries us round the Galaxy at about 230 km/s.

  4. warm-up (Weight on other worlds.) Surface gravity is $g = GM/R^2$, so it scales as mass over radius squared. Mars has 0.107 times the Earth's mass and 0.532 times its radius; Jupiter has 318 times the mass and 11.2 times the radius. Find $g$ on each, given 9.81 m/s² on Earth.

    Solution

    $g_{\text{Mars}} = 9.81\times0.107/0.532^2 = 3.71$ m/s², just over a third of Earth's. $g_{\text{Jupiter}} = 9.81\times318/11.2^2 = 24.8$ m/s², about two and a half times. Jupiter is over 300 times heavier, but its large size spreads the effect out. A 70 kg person keeps a mass of 70 kg everywhere; what changes is the weight, the force of gravity on them.

  5. warm-up (Why the Moon has no air.) The escape speed from a body is $\sqrt{2GM/R}$. Find it for the Moon ($M = 7.34\times10^{22}$ kg, $R = 1737$ km, $G = 6.67\times10^{-11}$ N m² kg⁻²). Molecules in a gas at 400 K move at around 0.5 km/s, and some much faster. What does this tell you?

    Solution
    $$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\times6.67\times10^{-11}\times7.34\times10^{22}}{1.737\times10^6}} = 2.4\text{ km/s}.$$

    The typical molecule is slower, but a gas has a spread of speeds, and the fastest few percent leak away, to be replaced from the rest. Over billions of years in the sunlit lunar day, any atmosphere is lost. A rough rule is that a planet keeps a gas for the age of the Solar System only if the escape speed is at least six times the gas's typical speed. The Earth (11.2 km/s) keeps nitrogen and oxygen but not hydrogen or helium.

  6. warm-up (The planet that would float.) Saturn has mass $5.68\times10^{26}$ kg and radius 58 200 km. Find its average density and compare it with water (1000 kg/m³). How many Earths would fit inside the Sun, whose radius is 109 times the Earth's?

    Solution

    The volume of a sphere is $\frac43\pi R^3 = \frac43\pi(5.82\times10^7)^3 = 8.26\times10^{23}$ m³, so the density is $5.68\times10^{26}/8.26\times10^{23} = 690$ kg/m³. Saturn is less dense than water: in a large enough bath it would float. It is mostly hydrogen and helium. Volume goes as radius cubed, so the Sun could hold $109^3 = 1.3$ million Earths. Cubing turns modest ratios of size into huge ratios of volume.

Light from the sky

  1. warm-up (Sunlight on other planets.) At the Earth's distance, sunlight delivers 1361 W per square metre. Light spreads out over a sphere, so its intensity falls as $1/r^2$. How much does Mars get (1.52 AU)? Jupiter (5.20 AU)?

    Solution

    The same total power passes through every sphere around the Sun, and a sphere's area grows as $r^2$. So Mars gets $1361/1.52^2 = 590$ W/m², less than half, and Jupiter $1361/5.20^2 = 50$ W/m², about a twenty-seventh. Spacecraft beyond Jupiter can't use solar panels efficiently, which is why Voyager, Cassini and New Horizons carried nuclear power sources instead (though Juno, with enormous panels, manages at Jupiter).

  2. keywarm-up (The colours of stars.) A hot body glows most strongly at the wavelength $\lambda_{\max} = b/T$, with $b = 2.90\times10^{-3}$ m K (Wien's law). Find it for the Sun (5772 K), red Betelgeuse (about 3600 K) and blue-white Rigel (about 12 000 K). Why do we not see green stars?

    Solution

    The Sun peaks at $2.90\times10^{-3}/5772 = 502$ nm, blue-green. Betelgeuse peaks at 805 nm, in the infrared, so we see the red tail of its light. Rigel peaks at 242 nm, in the ultraviolet, and its visible light is dominated by blue. A star whose peak is in the green still emits plenty of red and blue on either side; their mixture looks white to the eye. That is the Sun: its peak is green, but it is white (yellowish only through our atmosphere). Colour is a thermometer that works across thousands of light-years.

  3. warm-up (Is it coming or going?) Hydrogen emits a red line at 656.28 nm in the laboratory. In a star's spectrum the line is at 656.50 nm. Is the star moving towards us or away, and how fast? For speeds much less than light, $\Delta\lambda/\lambda = v/c$.

    Solution

    The wavelength is longer, shifted to the red, so the star is moving away. $v = c\,\Delta\lambda/\lambda = 3.00\times10^5\times0.22/656.28 = 100$ km/s. Only the motion along the line of sight shows up: a star moving across the sky has no shift. Measuring Doppler shifts to a precision of metres per second is how most of the first planets around other stars were found, by the small wobble they cause in their star (problem 1 of P3).

  4. warm-up (What a telescope does.) A telescope has an objective lens of focal length 1000 mm and diameter 200 mm, used with an eyepiece of focal length 25 mm. What is its magnification? How much more light does it collect than the eye, whose pupil is about 7 mm wide at night? Which number matters more?

    Solution

    The magnification is the ratio of focal lengths, $1000/25 = 40$. The light collected goes as the area of the aperture, so as diameter squared: $(200/7)^2 = 816$ times more than the eye. For astronomy, the collecting area matters most. Stars are points at any magnification; what a telescope does is gather enough light to see faint ones, and its aperture also sets the finest detail it can show. Astronomers describe telescopes by their diameter, never their magnification.

Stars, galaxies and the universe

  1. warm-up (Inside the Solar System.) How long does sunlight take to reach Neptune (30.1 AU)? Voyager 1, launched in 1977, is now about 170 AU from the Sun, moving at 17 km/s. How long would it take to cover the distance to Proxima Centauri (268 000 AU)?

    Solution

    Light takes 8.3 minutes per AU, so $30.1\times8.3 = 250$ minutes, 4.2 hours, to Neptune. Voyager covers $17\times3.16\times10^7 = 5.4\times10^8$ km a year, or 3.6 AU. At that speed, 268 000 AU takes about 75 000 years. (It is not heading for Proxima, and won't pass close to any star for about 40 000 years.) Our fastest spacecraft move at a twenty-thousandth of the speed of light; the stars are, for now, far out of reach.

  2. warm-up (Brightness and distance.) Two identical stars are at different distances; one appears 100 times fainter than the other. How many times further away is it? If a star like the Sun were moved to 10 light-years, by what factor would it be dimmer than the Sun now? (1 light-year $= 63\,200$ AU.)

    Solution

    Brightness falls as $1/d^2$, so 100 times fainter means $\sqrt{100} = 10$ times further. At 10 light-years, 632 000 AU, the Sun would be $632\,000^2 = 4\times10^{11}$ times dimmer: about as bright as the Pole Star, one of many ordinary stars in the night sky. If you know how bright a kind of star truly is, its apparent brightness tells you its distance. These "standard candles" are how distances to galaxies are measured.

  3. warm-up (Andromeda.) The Andromeda galaxy, about 2.5 million light-years away, is visible to the naked eye as a faint smudge, and spans about $3^\circ$ of sky in long photographs. Roughly how large is it? When did its light leave?

    Solution

    $3^\circ$ is $3/57.3 = 0.052$ rad, so its size is about $0.052\times2.5\times10^6 = 130\,000$ light-years, a little larger than our own Galaxy. The light left 2.5 million years ago, when the first humans of the genus Homo were appearing in Africa. It is the most distant thing most people can see without a telescope. Andromeda is coming towards us at about 110 km/s, and will merge with the Milky Way in about 4.5 billion years.

  4. warm-up (Hubble's law.) Distant galaxies move away from us at a speed proportional to their distance: $v = H_0d$, with $H_0 \approx 70$ km/s per megaparsec (1 Mpc $= 3.26$ million light-years). How far is a galaxy receding at 7000 km/s? If every galaxy has always moved at its present speed, how long ago were they all together?

    Solution

    $d = v/H_0 = 7000/70 = 100$ Mpc, about 330 million light-years. If galaxies kept constant speeds, a galaxy at distance $d$ left us a time $d/v = 1/H_0$ ago, the same for all of them. Converting units, 1 Mpc $= 3.09\times10^{19}$ km, so $1/H_0 = 3.09\times10^{19}/70$ s $= 4.4\times10^{17}$ s $= 14$ billion years. This "Hubble time" is close to the actual age of the universe, 13.8 billion years. It is not exactly right, because the expansion has slowed and sped up over time (see problems 7 and 8 of P3).

  5. keywarm-up (A year as a day.) Squash the 13.8 billion years since the Big Bang into one calendar year. On what date does the Sun form (4.6 billion years ago)? When do the dinosaurs die out (66 million years ago)? How long before midnight on 31 December does recorded history begin (5000 years ago)?

    Solution

    One day of the calendar is $13.8\times10^9/365 = 3.8\times10^7$ years. The Sun forms $4.6\times10^9/3.8\times10^7 = 122$ days before the end of the year: about 31 August. The dinosaurs die $66\times10^6/3.8\times10^7 = 1.7$ days before the end, early on 30 December. A second of the calendar is $3.8\times10^7/86\,400 = 437$ years, so recorded history starts about 11 seconds before midnight. Everything that has happened to humans fits into the last minutes of the last day.
