Lesson 07 · Stage 2: making models honest

Stochastic optimisation

Gradient descent from lesson 02 reads the whole dataset for every step. With a million examples that is hopeless. Stochastic gradient descent uses a small random batch instead: the gradient becomes noisy but stays right on average. This lesson works out exactly how noisy, what that noise does to the final answer, and the three ideas every deep learning optimiser is built from: momentum, per-coordinate step sizes (Adam), and learning-rate schedules.

Prerequisites: lesson 02, lesson 05Math pulled in: unbiased estimators, the variance of a mean, linear recurrences, exponential moving averages

The idea

Almost every training loss is an average over examples:

$$L(\mathbf{w}) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell_i(\mathbf{w}), \qquad \nabla L(\mathbf{w}) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \nabla\ell_i(\mathbf{w}).$$

The full gradient is a mean of $n$ per-example gradients. You don't need all $n$ terms to estimate a mean well: a random sample of 32 gives a decent estimate at a tiny fraction of the cost. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) draws a random minibatch $\mathcal{B}$ of size $B$ and steps along

$$\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}} = \frac1B\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\nabla\ell_i(\mathbf{w}), \qquad \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}} .$$

One pass through the data in batches is an epoch. In one epoch, full-batch gradient descent takes one step; SGD with $B = 32$ on 2,000 examples takes 62. Early in training, when every example points the same way, those 62 cheap steps are worth far more than one exact one.

How noisy is a minibatch gradient?

Write $\mathbf{g}_i = \nabla\ell_i(\mathbf{w})$ and $\bar{\mathbf{g}} = \nabla L(\mathbf{w})$. Suppose each of the $B$ indices in the batch is drawn uniformly from $\{1,\dots,n\}$, independently. Each draw has mean $\frac1n\sum_i\mathbf{g}_i = \bar{\mathbf{g}}$, so by linearity of expectation

$$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}}] = \bar{\mathbf{g}} .$$

The minibatch gradient is an unbiased estimator of the full gradient. Its spread comes from the covariance of a single draw,

$$\Sigma(\mathbf{w}) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(\mathbf{g}_i - \bar{\mathbf{g}})(\mathbf{g}_i - \bar{\mathbf{g}})^\top .$$

The covariance of a sum of independent vectors is the sum of their covariances, and scaling by $1/B$ scales covariance by $1/B^2$, so

Variance of a minibatch gradient
$$\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}}) = \frac{1}{B^2}\cdot B\,\Sigma = \frac{\Sigma}{B}.$$

The standard deviation of the estimate falls like $1/\sqrt B$. Going from $B = 32$ to $B = 128$ costs four times as much compute per step and only halves the noise. That diminishing return is why moderate batch sizes are so efficient. (Sampling without replacement, as in the shuffled epochs of the code, multiplies the covariance by $(n - B)/(n - 1)$, slightly less noise; the full batch $B = n$ has none.)

What the noise costs: the noise floor

Near a minimum $\mathbf{w}^*$ the loss is close to quadratic, $L(\mathbf{w}) \approx L(\mathbf{w}^*) + \tfrac12\mathbf{e}^\top H\mathbf{e}$ with $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{w}^*$. Lesson 02 showed that in the eigenbasis of the Hessian $H$ each direction evolves on its own. Take one direction with curvature $h$ and write the minibatch gradient as the true gradient plus noise, $h e_t + \xi_t$, with $\mathbb{E}[\xi_t] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\xi_t) = s/B$:

$$e_{t+1} = (1 - \eta h)\,e_t - \eta\,\xi_t .$$

The noise $\xi_t$ is fresh at each step, so it is independent of $e_t$, and the variances add:

$$\operatorname{Var}(e_{t+1}) = (1 - \eta h)^2\operatorname{Var}(e_t) + \eta^2\frac{s}{B}.$$

If $0 < \eta h < 2$ (lesson 02's stability condition) the factor $(1-\eta h)^2$ is below 1, and the variance settles where $V = (1-\eta h)^2V + \eta^2 s/B$:

$$V = \frac{\eta^2 s/B}{1 - (1 - \eta h)^2} = \frac{\eta\, s}{B\,h\,(2 - \eta h)} .$$

The iterate never settles at the minimum. It keeps jittering around it, and that jitter costs $\tfrac12 hV$ in expected loss for this direction. For least squares with noise variance $\sigma^2$, the per-example gradient noise near the optimum is $s_j \approx \sigma^2 h_j$ in direction $j$. Summing over directions:

SGD noise floor on a quadratic (constant step size)
$$\mathbb{E}\big[L(\mathbf{w}_t)\big] - L(\mathbf{w}^*) \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{\eta\,\sigma^2}{2B}\sum_j\frac{h_j}{2 - \eta h_j} \;\approx\; \frac{\eta}{B}\cdot\frac{\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(H)}{4}\quad\text{for small }\eta h_j .$$

The code below checks this: the predicted floors are $1.9\times10^{-2}$ for $\eta = 0.1$ and $1.3\times10^{-3}$ for $\eta = 0.01$, and the measured values are $1.8\times10^{-2}$ and $1.4\times10^{-3}$. Three lessons fall out of the formula:

  • A big step size learns fast but ends noisy. The floor is proportional to $\eta$. That is the trade-off on the left of the figure.
  • Only $\eta/B$ matters for the noise. Doubling the batch and doubling the step size leaves the floor unchanged while halving the number of steps. This is the linear scaling rule used to train ImageNet in an hour (Goyal et al., 2017). It breaks down once $\eta$ gets near the stability limit $2/h_{\max}$.
  • To converge, the step size must shrink. Robbins and Monro (1951) showed that SGD converges to the minimum if $\sum_t\eta_t = \infty$ (you can still travel any distance) and $\sum_t\eta_t^2 < \infty$ (the accumulated noise is finite); $\eta_t \propto 1/t$ satisfies both.

The noise is not only a nuisance. In non-convex deep learning it helps SGD escape saddle points and sharp minima, and lesson 14 returns to that.

Momentum

Lesson 02 showed that gradient descent on an ill-conditioned problem zigzags: the step size has to be small enough for the steepest direction ($\eta < 2/h_{\max}$), so progress along the flattest direction is painfully slow. The number of steps grows with the condition number $\kappa = h_{\max}/h_{\min}$. Heavy-ball momentum (Polyak, 1964) keeps a running sum of past gradients:

$$\mathbf{v}_t = \beta\,\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + \mathbf{g}_t, \qquad \mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t - \eta\,\mathbf{v}_t, \qquad 0 \le \beta < 1 .$$

Across the valley the gradient flips sign every step, so those components cancel in $\mathbf{v}$. Along the valley it keeps pointing the same way, so they add up: with a constant gradient, $\mathbf{v}$ grows to $\mathbf{g}/(1 - \beta)$, a tenfold longer step for the usual $\beta = 0.9$.

How much faster, exactly. Eliminate $\mathbf{v}$: $\mathbf{w}_{t+1} - \mathbf{w}_t = -\eta(\beta\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + \mathbf{g}_t) = \beta(\mathbf{w}_t - \mathbf{w}_{t-1}) - \eta\mathbf{g}_t$. On a quadratic, in a direction with curvature $h$, the error obeys

$$e_{t+1} = (1 + \beta - \eta h)\,e_t - \beta\,e_{t-1}.$$

A linear recurrence like this is solved by $e_t = r^t$, which gives the characteristic equation $r^2 - (1 + \beta - \eta h)\,r + \beta = 0$. The product of its two roots is $\beta$. When the roots are complex they are conjugates with equal size, so $|r| = \sqrt\beta$, and the error shrinks by $\sqrt\beta$ per step regardless of $h$. The roots are complex when $(1 + \beta - \eta h)^2 < 4\beta$, which rearranges to

$$(1 - \sqrt\beta)^2 < \eta h < (1 + \sqrt\beta)^2 .$$

Choose $\eta$ and $\beta$ so that the whole range $[h_{\min}, h_{\max}]$ fits in this window: $\eta h_{\min} = (1-\sqrt\beta)^2$ and $\eta h_{\max} = (1+\sqrt\beta)^2$. Dividing the two gives $\kappa = \big((1+\sqrt\beta)/(1-\sqrt\beta)\big)^2$, and solving for $\sqrt\beta$:

Convergence rate per step on a quadratic with condition number κ
$$\text{gradient descent: } \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}, \qquad \text{heavy-ball momentum: } \frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}.$$

Momentum replaces $\kappa$ by $\sqrt\kappa$. For $\kappa = 100$, plain descent needs about 50 steps to shrink the error by a factor of $e$; momentum needs about 5. On the right of the figure ($\kappa = 25$, both methods tuned optimally) gradient descent takes 44 steps to reduce the loss a thousandfold and momentum takes 15.

With noisy gradients the same $\mathbf{g}/(1-\beta)$ amplification applies to the noise, so momentum with step $\eta$ has roughly the noise floor of SGD with step $\eta/(1-\beta)$. In the code, momentum with $\eta = 0.01$, $\beta = 0.9$ ends near SGD with $\eta = 0.1$. The Nesterov variant evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point $\mathbf{w}_t - \eta\beta\mathbf{v}_{t-1}$, which is slightly more stable and achieves the $\sqrt\kappa$ rate on all smooth convex functions, not just quadratics.

Adaptive step sizes: Adam

Momentum fixes the zigzag but still uses one step size for every coordinate. If one weight's gradients are typically 100 times larger than another's, no single $\eta$ suits both. Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) divides each coordinate by a running estimate of its typical gradient size.

Math detour · exponential moving averages and bias correction

An exponential moving average (EMA) of a sequence $\mathbf{g}_1, \mathbf{g}_2, \dots$ is $\mathbf{m}_t = \beta\mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1-\beta)\mathbf{g}_t$ with $\mathbf{m}_0 = \mathbf{0}$. Unrolling,

$$\mathbf{m}_t = (1-\beta)\sum_{s=1}^t\beta^{t-s}\mathbf{g}_s .$$

Older values are discounted geometrically, and the weights look back over roughly $1/(1-\beta)$ steps: 10 for $\beta = 0.9$, 1,000 for $\beta = 0.999$. Because $\mathbf{m}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, early averages are pulled toward zero. If every $\mathbf{g}_s$ has mean $\boldsymbol\mu$, then $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{m}_t] = (1-\beta)\sum_{s=1}^t\beta^{t-s}\boldsymbol\mu = (1-\beta^t)\,\boldsymbol\mu$. Dividing by $1 - \beta^t$ removes the bias exactly.

Adam keeps two EMAs, of the gradient and of its elementwise square, and corrects both:

Adam (all operations elementwise)
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf{m}_t &= \beta_1\mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_1)\,\mathbf{g}_t, & \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t &= \mathbf{m}_t/(1-\beta_1^t),\\ \mathbf{v}_t &= \beta_2\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_2)\,\mathbf{g}_t^2, & \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t &= \mathbf{v}_t/(1-\beta_2^t),\\ \mathbf{w}_{t+1} &= \mathbf{w}_t - \eta\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t} + \epsilon}. &&\end{aligned}$$

Defaults: $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$. What the update does:

  • $\hat{\mathbf{m}}$ is momentum. $\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}}$ is the root-mean-square gradient size of each coordinate over the last thousand or so steps.
  • The ratio $\hat m_j/\sqrt{\hat v_j}$ is roughly between $-1$ and $1$. When gradients in coordinate $j$ are consistent it is near $\pm1$ and the step is about $\eta$; when they are noise around zero it is small. So $\eta$ sets the step size in parameter units, the same for every coordinate.
  • Multiplying the loss by any constant leaves the update unchanged (apart from $\epsilon$). Adam does not care how the loss or the features are scaled, which is why it is the default for transformers, whose layers have gradients on wildly different scales.

The price is the same noise floor as before: on the right of the figure Adam reaches the bottom of the quadratic quickly but then rattles around at a distance set by $\eta$. A schedule (next section) takes care of that.

AdamW. Adding the ridge penalty $\tfrac\lambda2\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ to the loss puts $\lambda\mathbf{w}$ into $\mathbf{g}$, and Adam then divides it by $\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}}$: weights with large gradients are barely decayed at all. Loshchilov and Hutter (2019) decoupled the two, applying the decay directly to the weights:

$$\mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t - \eta\Big(\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t}+\epsilon} + \lambda\,\mathbf{w}_t\Big).$$

This is lesson 05's weight decay restored to its original meaning, and AdamW is the standard optimiser for training large language models (lesson 27).

Learning-rate schedules

The noise floor says: large steps early to make progress, small steps late to settle. Modern training does both with a schedule. The most common one is linear warm-up then cosine decay. For total steps $T$ and $T_w$ warm-up steps,

$$\eta_t = \begin{cases}\eta_{\max}\,\dfrac{t}{T_w}, & t < T_w,\\[2mm] \eta_{\min} + \tfrac12(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min})\Big(1 + \cos\dfrac{\pi(t - T_w)}{T - T_w}\Big), & t \ge T_w .\end{cases}$$

The cosine half spends a long time near $\eta_{\max}$, then glides to $\eta_{\min}$. In the figure, cosine decay from $\eta = 0.1$ tracks the fast constant-step run for most of training, then ends two to three orders of magnitude below the constant runs, as the floor formula predicts once $\eta$ shrinks.

Why warm-up? At step 1 Adam's $\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ is an average of a single squared gradient, a very noisy denominator, and the network's initial gradients can be large and unrepresentative. A few hundred to a few thousand steps of gradually increasing $\eta$ prevent early updates from throwing the weights somewhere bad. Transformers are notoriously unstable without it.

Code from scratch

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, d, sigma = 2000, 20, 0.5
X = rng.standard_normal((n, d)) * np.linspace(0.2, 3.0, d)   # features on very different scales
y = X @ rng.standard_normal(d) + sigma * rng.standard_normal(n)
w_best = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
L = lambda w: 0.5 * np.mean((X @ w - y) ** 2)
excess = lambda w: L(w) - L(w_best)                # how far above the best possible loss
h = np.linalg.eigvalsh(X.T @ X / n)                # curvatures (Hessian eigenvalues)

def train(update, epochs=30, B=32, seed=1):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    w, state, t = np.zeros(d), {}, 0
    for epoch in range(epochs):
        for idx in np.array_split(rng.permutation(n), n // B):
            g = X[idx].T @ (X[idx] @ w - y[idx]) / len(idx)   # minibatch gradient
            t += 1
            w = update(w, g, state, t)
    return excess(w)

def sgd(lr):
    return lambda w, g, s, t: w - lr * g

def sgd_cosine(lr0, T):                            # learning rate decays from lr0 to 0 over T steps
    return lambda w, g, s, t: w - lr0 * 0.5 * (1 + np.cos(np.pi * t / T)) * g

def momentum(lr, beta=0.9):
    def update(w, g, s, t):
        s["v"] = beta * s.get("v", 0) + g
        return w - lr * s["v"]
    return update

def adam(lr, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    def update(w, g, s, t):
        s["m"] = b1 * s.get("m", 0) + (1 - b1) * g
        s["v"] = b2 * s.get("v", 0) + (1 - b2) * g * g
        m_hat, v_hat = s["m"] / (1 - b1**t), s["v"] / (1 - b2**t)   # bias correction
        return w - lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
    return update

print(f"curvatures from {h[0]:.3f} to {h[-1]:.2f}: condition number {h[-1] / h[0]:.0f}")
for lr in [0.1, 0.03, 0.01]:
    floor = lr * sigma**2 / (2 * 32) * np.sum(h / (2 - lr * h))     # predicted noise floor
    print(f"SGD lr={lr:<5} excess {train(sgd(lr)):.1e}   predicted floor {floor:.1e}")
T = 30 * (n // 32)
print(f"SGD cosine from 0.1   excess {train(sgd_cosine(0.1, T)):.1e}")
print(f"momentum lr=0.01      excess {train(momentum(0.01)):.1e}")
print(f"Adam lr=0.003         excess {train(adam(0.003)):.1e}")

# curvatures from 0.041 to 9.48: condition number 231
# SGD lr=0.1   excess 1.8e-02   predicted floor 1.9e-02
# SGD lr=0.03  excess 7.0e-03   predicted floor 4.3e-03
# SGD lr=0.01  excess 1.4e-03   predicted floor 1.3e-03
# SGD cosine from 0.1   excess 6.2e-06
# momentum lr=0.01      excess 2.9e-02
# Adam lr=0.003         excess 2.2e-04

Each line is a single run, so each excess loss is one random draw from the stationary jitter; the prediction is its average (the figure averages 8 runs). The floor scales with $\eta$; momentum with $\eta = 0.01$ lands near SGD with $\eta = 0.1$, as the $1/(1-\beta)$ argument says; and the decayed schedule beats every constant step by orders of magnitude.

Exercises

  1. Sampling a batch of $B$ distinct indices without replacement: show the minibatch gradient is still unbiased.
  2. Using the noise-floor formula, by what factor does the floor change if you multiply the batch size by 4 and keep $\eta$ fixed? If you multiply both $B$ and $\eta$ by 4?
  3. Check that $\eta_t = c/t$ satisfies the Robbins–Monro conditions but $\eta_t = c/t^2$ does not. What goes wrong with $c/t^2$?
  4. For heavy-ball momentum with a constant gradient $\mathbf{g}$, show $\mathbf{v}_t \to \mathbf{g}/(1-\beta)$. With $\beta = 0.99$, how much longer is the steady-state step than plain SGD's?
  5. Suppose every gradient coordinate is a constant, $g_j = c_j \ne 0$. What is Adam's step in coordinate $j$ (after bias correction, ignoring $\epsilon$)? What does that say about Adam on a function like $f(w) = |w|$ near its minimum?
  6. With plain Adam and an L2 penalty in the loss, the decay term in coordinate $j$ is effectively $\eta\lambda w_j/(\sqrt{\hat v_j} + \epsilon)$. Explain which weights end up decayed least, and why AdamW's version is closer to a Gaussian prior.
Answers

1. By symmetry each index $i$ appears in the batch with probability $B/n$. So $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}}] = \frac1B\sum_i\frac{B}{n}\mathbf{g}_i = \bar{\mathbf{g}}$.

2. The floor depends on $\eta/B$ (apart from the small $\eta h$ correction). $B\times4$ alone: floor $\div4$. Both $\times4$: floor unchanged, but each epoch has a quarter as many steps, which is the linear scaling rule.

3. $\sum 1/t$ diverges and $\sum 1/t^2 = \pi^2/6$ converges, so $c/t$ satisfies both. With $c/t^2$, $\sum\eta_t$ is finite: the total distance the iterate can travel is bounded, so if it starts far from the minimum it can stall before it gets there.

4. $\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{g}(1 + \beta + \dots + \beta^{t-1}) = \mathbf{g}(1-\beta^t)/(1-\beta) \to \mathbf{g}/(1-\beta)$. With $\beta = 0.99$, 100 times longer.

5. $\hat m_j = c_j$ and $\hat v_j = c_j^2$, so the step is $-\eta\operatorname{sign}(c_j)$: a fixed step of size $\eta$ whatever the gradient's magnitude. On $|w|$ the gradient is $\pm1$ everywhere, so Adam (like sign descent) bounces around the minimum at distance about $\eta$ until $\eta$ is decayed.

6. Weights with large or noisy gradients have large $\hat v_j$, so their decay is divided by a large number and they are barely regularised; rarely-updated weights with tiny gradients get decayed a lot. That ties the strength of the "prior" to the gradient history. AdamW shrinks every weight by the same factor $1 - \eta\lambda$ per step, which is the gradient of a fixed isotropic Gaussian prior, independent of the optimiser's preconditioning.
