Lesson 22 · Stage 6: generative models

Autoencoders and variational autoencoders

A generative model learns a probability distribution from samples, so that it can produce new samples and say how likely a given one is. The variational autoencoder (VAE) does this by imagining that each data point was made from a few hidden numbers, a latent code, passed through a neural network. The likelihood of the data then involves an integral over all codes that can't be computed, and the VAE maximises a lower bound on it instead, the evidence lower bound (ELBO). This lesson starts from plain autoencoders, derives the ELBO in two ways, the Gaussian KL divergence in closed form, and the reparameterisation trick, compares its gradient variance with the alternative, and trains a VAE whose two latent dimensions discover the angle and length of a bar.

Prerequisites: M3, M4, lesson 13Math pulled in: latent-variable models, Jensen's inequality, KL divergence between Gaussians, Monte Carlo gradient estimators, importance sampling

Autoencoders

An autoencoder is a network trained to output its own input through a narrow middle layer. An encoder $f$ maps $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^D$ to a code $\mathbf{z} = f(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^k$ with $k\ll D$, a decoder $g$ maps it back, and both are trained to minimise the reconstruction error $\lVert\mathbf{x} - g(f(\mathbf{x}))\rVert^2$. Since the code is small, the network has to find the few numbers that describe the data best.

With linear maps $f(\mathbf{x}) = E\mathbf{x}$ and $g(\mathbf{z}) = D\mathbf{z}$ on centred data, the best reconstruction $DE\mathbf{x}$ is a projection onto a $k$-dimensional subspace, and the subspace that minimises the squared error is the span of the top $k$ principal components (M1, the Eckart–Young theorem). A linear autoencoder rediscovers PCA, though its $E$ and $D$ needn't be orthogonal: any invertible $A$ gives the same reconstruction with $E' = AE$ and $D' = DA^{-1}$. Nonlinear encoders and decoders find curved low-dimensional surfaces instead of flat ones.

An autoencoder is not a generative model, though. To make a new image you would pick a code $\mathbf{z}$ and decode it, but nothing says which codes are valid. The codes of training points can be scattered over an irregular region with gaps, and the decoder has never been trained on the gaps, so a random code usually decodes to garbage. The VAE fixes this by making the code distribution part of the model.

Latent-variable models

Suppose each data point $\mathbf{x}$ is generated by first drawing a latent code from a simple prior, $\mathbf{z}\sim p(\mathbf{z}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$, and then drawing $\mathbf{x}\sim p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})$, a distribution whose parameters are computed from $\mathbf{z}$ by a neural network, the decoder. For binary images, $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) = \prod_j\pi_j^{x_j}(1 - \pi_j)^{1 - x_j}$ with pixel probabilities $\boldsymbol{\pi} = \sigma(\text{decoder}_\theta(\mathbf{z}))$. Sampling is easy: draw $\mathbf{z}$, decode, draw pixels. The model's probability of a data point is

$$p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \int p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\,p(\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z},$$

and maximum likelihood (M3) would maximise $\sum_i\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i)$. The trouble is the integral. With a neural-network decoder it has no closed form, and estimating it by sampling $\mathbf{z}$ from the prior is hopeless in more than a few dimensions: for a given image, almost every random $\mathbf{z}$ decodes to something else, so $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})$ is astronomically small for all but a tiny region of codes, which random draws almost never hit. What we need is the posterior $p_\theta(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})$, the codes that could have produced $\mathbf{x}$; but by Bayes' rule it is $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{z})/p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$, which contains the same integral.

A Gaussian mixture (M3) is the special case with a discrete latent: $z\in\{1, \dots, K\}$ picks a component. There the posterior is a finite sum and the EM algorithm computes it exactly. The VAE handles continuous latents and nonlinear decoders by approximating the posterior with another network.

The evidence lower bound

Introduce an encoder $q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})$, a distribution over codes computed from $\mathbf{x}$; in a VAE it is a Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_\phi(\mathbf{x}), \operatorname{diag}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2_\phi(\mathbf{x}))$ whose mean and log-variance are outputs of a network. For any such $q$,

$$\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})}{q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}\right] + \operatorname{KL}\big(q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\,\big\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\big).$$

Proof. $\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ doesn't depend on $\mathbf{z}$, so it equals its own expectation under $q$. Write $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = p_\theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})/p_\theta(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})$, and multiply and divide by $q$ inside the logarithm:

$$\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}_q\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})}{p_\theta(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}\right] = \mathbb{E}_q\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})}{q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}\right] + \mathbb{E}_q\left[\log\frac{q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}{p_\theta(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}\right],$$

and the second term is the KL divergence by definition. Since KL divergences are non-negative (M4), the first term is a lower bound on the log-likelihood, the evidence lower bound:

The ELBO

$$\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})\ \ge\ \mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}\big[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\big] - \operatorname{KL}\big(q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\,\big\|\,p(\mathbf{z})\big),$$

with equality exactly when $q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) = p_\theta(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})$.

The second form follows from $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{z})$. (A shorter route to the inequality alone is Jensen's: $\log\mathbb{E}_q[p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})/q] \ge \mathbb{E}_q\log[p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})/q]$, since log is concave; the decomposition above also says how big the gap is.) The two terms explain the name autoencoder. The first is a reconstruction term: encode $\mathbf{x}$ to a distribution over codes, sample one, decode, and score how well the result explains $\mathbf{x}$. The second is a regulariser that pulls each encoding distribution towards the prior, which fills in the gaps that made the plain autoencoder useless for sampling: every region of the prior is near some data point's encoding.

Maximising $\mathcal{L}$ over both $\theta$ and $\phi$ does two things at once. Over $\phi$, since $\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ doesn't depend on $\phi$, it minimises the KL divergence from the true posterior: it is variational inference, fitting an approximate posterior. Over $\theta$, it pushes up a lower bound on the likelihood. Using one network to output the posterior approximation for every $\mathbf{x}$, instead of fitting separate parameters per data point, is called amortised inference.

The KL term in closed form

For one dimension, $q = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $p = \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$: $\log q(z) = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - (z - \mu)^2/2\sigma^2$ and $\log p(z) = -\tfrac12\log2\pi - z^2/2$. Take the expectation of the difference under $q$, using $\mathbb{E}_q[(z - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2$ and $\mathbb{E}_q[z^2] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$:

$$\operatorname{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q - \log p] = -\tfrac12\log\sigma^2 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2) = \tfrac12\left(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\right).$$

With a diagonal covariance the dimensions are independent and the KL is the sum over dimensions. It is zero only when $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$; the $\mu^2$ term pulls the codes towards the origin, and $\sigma^2 - \log\sigma^2$, minimised at $\sigma = 1$, stops the encoder from collapsing each code to a point. Part 1 of the code checks the formula against a Monte Carlo average.

Gradients through sampling: the reparameterisation trick

The reconstruction term is an expectation over $\mathbf{z}\sim q_\phi$, and we need its gradient with respect to $\phi$, the parameters of the distribution we are sampling from. We can't differentiate through "draw a random number". There are two ways round this.

The score-function estimator (also called REINFORCE, and it will return in reinforcement learning as the policy gradient). Differentiate under the integral and use $\nabla_\phi q_\phi = q_\phi\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi$:

$$\nabla_\phi\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f(\mathbf{z})] = \int f(\mathbf{z})\nabla_\phi q_\phi(\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z} = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\big[f(\mathbf{z})\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi(\mathbf{z})\big].$$

It works for any $f$, even a discontinuous one, but it only uses the values of $f$, not its slope, and its variance is usually large.

The reparameterisation trick (Kingma & Welling, 2014; Rezende et al., 2014). Write the sample as a deterministic function of the parameters and of noise that doesn't depend on them: $\mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_\phi + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_\phi\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, with $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$. Then the expectation is over $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, which doesn't involve $\phi$, and the gradient moves inside:

$$\nabla_\phi\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f(\mathbf{z})] = \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\big[\nabla_\phi f(\boldsymbol{\mu}_\phi + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_\phi\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\big].$$

Now ordinary backpropagation goes through the sample: $\partial\mathbf{z}/\partial\boldsymbol{\mu} = I$ and $\partial z_j/\partial\log\sigma_j^2 = \tfrac12\sigma_j\epsilon_j$. This estimator uses the derivative of $f$ and typically has far lower variance. For $f(z) = z^2$ with $z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu, 1)$ and $\mu = 1.5$, the code finds a standard deviation of 7.25 for the score-function estimate of the gradient and 2.00 for the reparameterised one, so about 13 times fewer samples give the same accuracy. Exercise 4 derives these numbers.

The training loop for one batch is then: encode, sample $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, form $\mathbf{z}$, decode, compute $\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) - \operatorname{KL}$, and backpropagate. One sample of $\mathbf{z}$ per data point is enough; the noise averages out over the batch and over steps.

Decoders, blur and the balance between the terms

For real-valued data a Gaussian decoder $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{g}_\theta(\mathbf{z}), s^2I)$ gives $\log p = -\lVert\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{g}_\theta(\mathbf{z})\rVert^2/2s^2 + \text{const}$: the reconstruction term is a squared error weighted by $1/2s^2$. The noise level $s$ therefore sets the exchange rate between reconstruction and KL. Many implementations write the loss as $\text{MSE} + \beta\operatorname{KL}$, which corresponds to some $s$; the $\beta$-VAE (Higgins et al., 2017) raises $\beta$ above 1 to push the codes harder towards the factorised prior, which tends to give more interpretable ("disentangled") dimensions at the cost of worse reconstructions.

Two weaknesses are well known. VAE samples of natural images are blurry: the decoder outputs the mean of $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})$, and when one code is compatible with several sharp images (an edge slightly left or slightly right), the mean is their average. And with a very powerful decoder, such as an autoregressive one, the model can ignore the latent altogether: setting $q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) = p(\mathbf{z})$ makes the KL term zero, and if the decoder can model the data alone, nothing is lost. This is posterior collapse. Still, the VAE is everywhere in modern generative modelling in a different role: the "latent" in latent diffusion (lesson 25) is the code of an autoencoder of this kind, trained so that diffusion can work on a small array instead of on pixels.

How tight is the bound?

The ELBO is a bound, so how far below $\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ is it? Importance sampling (M3) gives an answer. Since $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}_q[p_\theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})/q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})]$, the average of $K$ weights $w_k = p_\theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}_k)/q(\mathbf{z}_k\mid\mathbf{x})$ with $\mathbf{z}_k\sim q$ is an unbiased estimate of $p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$. Its logarithm is not unbiased, but by Jensen's inequality

$$\mathcal{L}_K = \mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^Kw_k\right]\ \le\ \log\mathbb{E}\left[\frac1K\sum_kw_k\right] = \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}).$$

$\mathcal{L}_1$ is the ELBO, $\mathcal{L}_K$ increases with $K$ (Burda et al., 2016), and $\mathcal{L}_K\to\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ as $K\to\infty$. Training on $\mathcal{L}_K$ instead of $\mathcal{L}_1$ is the importance-weighted autoencoder (IWAE). Here we use it only to measure the gap, after training.

Code from scratch

Part 1 checks the KL formula and compares the two gradient estimators. Part 2 makes the data: $16\times16$ black-and-white images of a bar through the centre, with a random angle between 0 and 180 degrees and a random half-length between 3 and 7 pixels; each pixel is on with a probability given by the bar's soft outline, plus a little background noise. Two numbers determine each image's distribution, so we give the VAE a 2-dimensional latent space. Part 3 is the VAE: a 128-unit encoder and decoder, a Bernoulli decoder, the ELBO with one reparameterised sample, and its gradients written out by hand and checked against finite differences (with the noise held fixed). Part 4 measures the gap between the ELBO and $\log p(\mathbf{x})$, and part 5 asks what the latent dimensions mean.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. Two facts the lesson derives, checked numerically.
# (a) KL(N(mu, s^2) || N(0, 1)) = (mu^2 + s^2 - 1 - log s^2) / 2, against a Monte Carlo average of log q - log p
mu, s = 1.5, 0.5
z = mu + s * rng.standard_normal(1_000_000)
mc = np.mean(-0.5 * ((z - mu) / s) ** 2 - np.log(s) + 0.5 * z ** 2)
print(f"KL closed form {(mu**2 + s**2 - 1 - np.log(s**2)) / 2:.4f}   Monte Carlo {mc:.4f}")

# (b) Two unbiased estimators of d/dmu E_{z ~ N(mu, 1)}[z^2] (true value 2 mu): the score-function
# estimator f(z) (z - mu), and the reparameterised one f'(mu + eps) = 2 (mu + eps)
eps = rng.standard_normal(100_000); z = mu + eps
score, reparam = z ** 2 * (z - mu), 2 * z
print(f"gradient estimates (true {2 * mu}): score function {score.mean():.3f} (std {score.std():.2f}),"
      f" reparameterised {reparam.mean():.3f} (std {reparam.std():.2f})")

# 2. Data: 16x16 images of a soft bar with a random angle (0 to 180 degrees) and length (3 to 7 pixels).
# Each pixel is black or white, on with probability given by the bar's ink. Two numbers determine
# the bar, so a 2-dimensional latent space is enough in principle.
yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:16, 0:16] - 7.5
def bars(n):
    th, ln = rng.uniform(0, np.pi, n), rng.uniform(3, 7, n)
    u = np.cos(th)[:, None, None] * xx + np.sin(th)[:, None, None] * yy        # along the bar
    v = -np.sin(th)[:, None, None] * xx + np.cos(th)[:, None, None] * yy       # across it
    ink = 0.02 + 0.96 * np.exp(-v ** 2 / 0.8) / (1 + np.exp(6 * (np.abs(u) - ln[:, None, None])))
    return (rng.random((n, 256)) < ink.reshape(n, 256)).astype(float), th, ln   # binary pixels

# 3. The VAE: encoder 256 -> 128 -> (mu, log var) in 2-D; decoder 2 -> 128 -> 256 Bernoulli means.
def init(a, b): return [rng.standard_normal((a, b)) * np.sqrt(2 / a), np.zeros(b)]
P = {}
P["W1"], P["b1"] = init(256, 128); P["Wm"], P["bm"] = init(128, 2); P["Wv"], P["bv"] = init(128, 2)
P["W2"], P["b2"] = init(2, 128); P["W3"], P["b3"] = init(128, 256)

def encode(X):
    H = np.maximum(X @ P["W1"] + P["b1"], 0)
    return H, H @ P["Wm"] + P["bm"], H @ P["Wv"] + P["bv"]

def decode(Z):
    G = np.maximum(Z @ P["W2"] + P["b2"], 0)
    return G, G @ P["W3"] + P["b3"]                                  # logits of the pixel means

def log_bernoulli(X, L):
    """log p(x | z) summed over pixels, for Bernoulli means sigmoid(L), written stably."""
    return np.sum(X * L - np.logaddexp(0, L), 1)

def elbo_and_grads(X):
    """Average ELBO over the batch, with one sample of z per example, and its gradients."""
    N = len(X)
    H, m, lv = encode(X)
    e = rng.standard_normal(m.shape); sd = np.exp(lv / 2); Z = m + sd * e     # reparameterisation
    G, L = decode(Z)
    rec = log_bernoulli(X, L); kl = 0.5 * np.sum(m ** 2 + sd ** 2 - 1 - lv, 1)
    # backward pass of -ELBO / N
    dL = (1 / (1 + np.exp(-L)) - X) / N
    g = {"W3": G.T @ dL, "b3": dL.sum(0)}
    dG = (dL @ P["W3"].T) * (G > 0)
    g["W2"], g["b2"] = Z.T @ dG, dG.sum(0)
    dZ = dG @ P["W2"].T
    dm = dZ + m / N                                                    # through z = m + sd e, and the KL
    dlv = dZ * e * sd / 2 + 0.5 * (sd ** 2 - 1) / N
    g["Wm"], g["bm"], g["Wv"], g["bv"] = H.T @ dm, dm.sum(0), H.T @ dlv, dlv.sum(0)
    dH = (dm @ P["Wm"].T + dlv @ P["Wv"].T) * (H > 0)
    g["W1"], g["b1"] = X.T @ dH, dH.sum(0)
    return np.mean(rec - kl), np.mean(rec), np.mean(kl), g

# gradient check: fix the noise by reseeding before each evaluation
X, _, _ = bars(8); worst = 0
state = rng.bit_generator.state
_, _, _, g = elbo_and_grads(X)
for k in P:
    for i in [tuple(rng.integers(0, s) for s in P[k].shape) for _ in range(3)]:
        old = P[k][i]
        P[k][i] = old + 1e-6; rng.bit_generator.state = state; up = elbo_and_grads(X)[0]
        P[k][i] = old - 1e-6; rng.bit_generator.state = state; dn = elbo_and_grads(X)[0]
        P[k][i] = old
        worst = max(worst, abs(-(up - dn) / 2e-6 - g[k][i]))
print(f"gradient check: max |backprop - finite difference| {worst:.1e}")

# train with Adam on fresh data each step
M = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}; V = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}
for t in range(1, 8001):
    X, _, _ = bars(128)
    elbo, rec, kl, g = elbo_and_grads(X)
    for k in P:
        M[k] = 0.9 * M[k] + 0.1 * g[k]; V[k] = 0.999 * V[k] + 0.001 * g[k] ** 2
        P[k] -= 1e-3 * (M[k] / (1 - 0.9 ** t)) / (np.sqrt(V[k] / (1 - 0.999 ** t)) + 1e-8)
    if t in (1, 500, 2000, 8000):
        print(f"step {t:4d}: ELBO {elbo:8.2f}  = reconstruction {rec:8.2f} - KL {kl:5.2f}")

# 4. How tight is the bound? Importance-weighted estimates of log p(x) with K samples from q(z|x):
# log (1/K) sum_k p(x, z_k) / q(z_k | x) is a lower bound for every K and tends to log p(x) as K grows.
Xte, th_te, ln_te = bars(500)
_, m, lv = encode(Xte); sd = np.exp(lv / 2)
for K in [1, 10, 100, 1000]:
    lw = []
    for _ in range(K):
        e = rng.standard_normal(m.shape); Z = m + sd * e
        log_q = np.sum(-0.5 * e ** 2 - np.log(sd) - 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi), 1)
        log_prior = np.sum(-0.5 * Z ** 2 - 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi), 1)
        lw.append(log_bernoulli(Xte, decode(Z)[1]) + log_prior - log_q)
    lw = np.array(lw)
    est = lw.max(0) + np.log(np.mean(np.exp(lw - lw.max(0)), 0))
    print(f"K = {K:4d}: estimate of log p(x) averaged over the test set {est.mean():8.2f}")
# 5. What did the two latent dimensions learn?
r, phi = np.linalg.norm(m, axis=1), np.arctan2(m[:, 1], m[:, 0])
print(f"distance of mu from 0 against bar length: correlation {np.corrcoef(r, ln_te)[0, 1]:.3f}")
c = np.abs(np.mean(np.exp(1j * (phi - 2 * th_te))))                  # 1 if phi = 2 theta + constant
c2 = np.abs(np.mean(np.exp(1j * (phi + 2 * th_te))))
print(f"direction of mu against bar angle: |mean exp(i(phi -+ 2 theta))| = {max(c, c2):.3f}  (1 = phi is 2 theta, up to rotation and reflection)")

# KL closed form 1.4431   Monte Carlo 1.4434
# gradient estimates (true 3.0): score function 3.004 (std 7.25), reparameterised 3.000 (std 2.00)
# gradient check: max |backprop - finite difference| 2.3e-08
# step    1: ELBO  -222.80  = reconstruction  -222.61 - KL  0.19
# step  500: ELBO   -50.16  = reconstruction   -46.34 - KL  3.82
# step 2000: ELBO   -45.17  = reconstruction   -41.87 - KL  3.31
# step 8000: ELBO   -43.62  = reconstruction   -39.99 - KL  3.63
# K =    1: estimate of log p(x) averaged over the test set   -43.78
# K =   10: estimate of log p(x) averaged over the test set   -43.60
# K =  100: estimate of log p(x) averaged over the test set   -43.57
# K = 1000: estimate of log p(x) averaged over the test set   -43.57
# distance of mu from 0 against bar length: correlation 0.903
# direction of mu against bar angle: |mean exp(i(phi -+ 2 theta))| = 0.987  (1 = phi is 2 theta, up to rotation and reflection)

The ELBO rises from $-224$ to $-43.6$ nats per image. The KL term settles at 3.6 nats: each image's code is pinned down to a small region of the 2-D prior, as the posterior standard deviations of about 0.1 to 0.2 show. The importance-weighted estimates rise with $K$ and level off at $-43.57$, only 0.2 nats above the ELBO: the Gaussian encoder approximates this model's posterior well.

Left: the encoder's mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathbf{x})$ for 500 test images, coloured by the bar's angle. Right: images decoded from a grid of codes (the pixel probabilities, darker is more likely on).

The latent space the VAE found has a clear geometry. The direction of the code encodes the angle: the angle of $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ equals twice the bar's angle, up to a rotation or reflection, with a circular correlation of 0.987. Twice, because a bar at 180 degrees is the same bar as at 0, so the angle is a circle, and the encoder maps it once around the origin. The distance from the origin encodes the length (correlation 0.90). Nobody told the model either fact. The grid of decoded images shows it from the other side: walking around the origin rotates the bar, and walking outward lengthens it. Near the centre, codes that the prior makes likely but no data maps to decode to blurred averages of all angles, the VAE's blur in its plainest form.

Then show, for any $q$, that $\mathcal{L} = \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) - \operatorname{KL}(q\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}))$, and conclude that maximising the ELBO over $q$ is the same as minimising this KL.
  2. Derive the KL divergence between two $d$-dimensional Gaussians $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \Sigma_1)$ and $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_2, \Sigma_2)$, and check that it reduces to the lesson's formula.
  3. In a VAE with a linear Gaussian decoder $p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) = \mathcal{N}(W\mathbf{z} + \mathbf{b}, s^2I)$ and the prior $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$, compute $p(\mathbf{x})$ in closed form. (This is probabilistic PCA; its maximum-likelihood $W$ spans the principal subspace.)
  4. For $f(z) = z^2$ and $z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu, 1)$, compute the variance of the score-function estimator $f(z)(z - \mu)$ and of the reparameterised estimator $2(\mu + \epsilon)$. Evaluate both at $\mu = 1.5$ and compare with the code.
  5. Explain why the score-function estimator's variance can be reduced by subtracting a constant $b$ from $f$: show that $\mathbb{E}_q[b\nabla\log q] = 0$. What value of $b$ would you try first?
  6. Show that $\mathcal{L}_K\le\mathcal{L}_{2K}$. (Hint: write the average of $2K$ weights as the average of two averages of $K$.)
  7. Change the code's latent dimension to 1. What must the VAE give up, and what do you expect the ELBO and the latent code to look like? Try it.
Answers

1. If $q = p_\theta(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})$ the KL term in the decomposition is 0. The identity is the decomposition rearranged. $\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ doesn't depend on $q$, so increasing $\mathcal{L}$ over $q$ is exactly decreasing the KL.

2. $\operatorname{KL} = \tfrac12\left[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_2^{-1}\Sigma_1) + (\boldsymbol{\mu}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_1)^\top\Sigma_2^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_1) - d + \log\frac{\det\Sigma_2}{\det\Sigma_1}\right]$, from $\mathbb{E}_1[(\mathbf{z} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top A(\mathbf{z} - \boldsymbol{\mu})] = \operatorname{tr}(A\Sigma_1) + (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top A(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu})$. With $\boldsymbol{\mu}_2 = \mathbf{0}$, $\Sigma_2 = I$ and $\Sigma_1 = \operatorname{diag}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2$: $\tfrac12\sum_j(\sigma_j^2 + \mu_j^2 - 1 - \log\sigma_j^2)$.

3. $\mathbf{x} = W\mathbf{z} + \mathbf{b} + s\boldsymbol{\eta}$ is a linear function of Gaussians, so it is Gaussian with mean $\mathbf{b}$ and covariance $WW^\top + s^2I$. Tipping and Bishop (1999) showed that the maximum-likelihood $W$ is $U_k(\Lambda_k - s^2I)^{1/2}R$, with $U_k, \Lambda_k$ the top $k$ eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the sample covariance and $R$ any rotation: the principal subspace again, and a rotation that the likelihood can't determine.

4. Reparameterised: $\operatorname{Var}(2(\mu + \epsilon)) = 4$, standard deviation 2. Score function: with $\epsilon = z - \mu$, the estimator is $(\mu + \epsilon)^2\epsilon = \mu^2\epsilon + 2\mu\epsilon^2 + \epsilon^3$. Its mean is $2\mu$; its second moment, using $\mathbb{E}\epsilon^2 = 1$, $\mathbb{E}\epsilon^4 = 3$, $\mathbb{E}\epsilon^6 = 15$ and odd moments 0, is $\mu^4 + 4\mu^2\cdot3 + 15 + 2\mu^2\cdot3 = \mu^4 + 18\mu^2 + 15$. So the variance is $\mu^4 + 14\mu^2 + 15$; at $\mu = 1.5$, $5.06 + 31.5 + 15 = 51.6$, standard deviation 7.18. The code's 7.25 is this, up to sampling error.

5. $\mathbb{E}_q[\nabla\log q] = \int q\,\nabla q/q = \nabla\int q = \nabla1 = 0$, so subtracting $b$ leaves the mean unchanged and can reduce the variance. A natural first choice is $b\approx\mathbb{E}_q[f]$, a running average of $f$: it is the "baseline" of the REINFORCE algorithm, and its relative, the value function, is the critic in actor–critic methods.

6. Let $A$ and $B$ be the averages of the first and last $K$ of $2K$ weights. Then $\log\frac{A + B}{2}\ge\frac12(\log A + \log B)$ by concavity, and taking expectations gives $\mathcal{L}_{2K}\ge\frac12(\mathcal{L}_K + \mathcal{L}_K) = \mathcal{L}_K$. (Burda et al. prove the stronger $\mathcal{L}_K\le\mathcal{L}_{K+1}$.)

7. One number can't encode a circle and a length continuously. Expect the code to follow the angle (the factor that changes most pixels), with a discontinuity somewhere around the circle where the decoder must jump from 180 degrees back to 0, and length to be largely ignored, so the reconstruction term and the ELBO get worse by several nats.
