Lesson 30 · Stage 7: large language models

In-context learning and reasoning

A trained language model is a fixed function, yet it can learn a new task from a few examples in its prompt, and it can solve harder problems if it writes out intermediate steps first. This lesson explains both, as far as they are understood. It covers how the next-token distribution is turned into text (temperature, top-k, top-p), and what in-context learning is: Bayesian inference over tasks, and, in a clean case, a transformer layer running gradient descent. It shows why a chain of thought helps, by turning one hard function into a sequence of easy ones. Then it turns to spending more compute at test time: majority voting, verifiers, pass@k, and the reasoning models trained with reinforcement learning on checkable answers. Each mechanism is demonstrated in code.

Prerequisites: lesson 26, lesson 28, lesson 19Math pulled in: truncated and tempered distributions, Bayesian posterior predictive, linear attention as a kernel sum, the multinomial distribution and majority vote, unbiased estimation of pass@k

The idea

GPT-3's paper was titled "Language models are few-shot learners" (Brown et al., 2020). Show the model three English–French word pairs and an English word, and it continues with the French. No weights change: the "learning" happens in the forward pass, conditioned on the prompt. A year and a half later, Wei et al. (2022) found that showing worked solutions, rather than bare answers, made large models far better at arithmetic word problems: chain-of-thought prompting. And since 2024 the strongest models are trained specifically to produce long chains of reasoning before answering, so that the amount of computation spent on a problem can grow with its difficulty. This lesson takes these in turn, starting with how text is produced from the model at all.

Decoding

Lesson 26 sampled each token from $q(\cdot\mid x_{<i})$, possibly reshaped by a temperature $\tau$. Other common choices:

  • Greedy decoding takes the most likely token each time ($\tau\to0$). It is deterministic and good for short factual answers, but tends to fall into repetitive loops in long text. Beam search keeps the $b$ most probable partial sequences; it is good for translation, where there is one right answer, and bad for open-ended text, which it makes bland.
  • Top-$k$ sampling (Fan et al., 2018) keeps the $k$ most likely tokens, renormalises, and samples.
  • Top-$p$ or nucleus sampling (Holtzman et al., 2020) keeps the smallest set of most likely tokens whose total probability is at least $p$, renormalises, and samples. Unlike top-$k$, the size of the set adapts: after "The capital of France is", the nucleus is one or two tokens; at the start of a story it is hundreds.

The reason for truncating at all is the tail. A model's distribution over 100,000 tokens puts a small probability on each of tens of thousands of bad continuations, and together they add up: sample a few hundred tokens and you are likely to hit one, after which the text goes off the rails, because the model conditions on its own mistake. Truncation removes the tail at the cost of some diversity. None of these rules changes the model, and each is a different trade between quality and variety.

In-context learning

A Bayesian view. Pretraining text is a mixture of documents, each generated under some latent "concept": a topic, a format, a task. Predicting the next token well requires inferring the concept from what came before. Xie et al. (2022) made this precise: if documents are generated by a mixture of tasks $\theta$, the ideal next-token predictor is the posterior predictive

$$p(y\mid x, \text{prompt}) = \int p(y\mid x, \theta)\,p(\theta\mid\text{prompt})\,d\theta ,$$

and each example in the prompt sharpens $p(\theta\mid\text{prompt})$ towards the task it demonstrates. On this view, in-context learning is not a separate skill but a consequence of good next-token prediction. It also explains a known observation: the format and the distribution of the examples matter, while whether their labels are correct matters surprisingly little (Min et al., 2022). The examples mainly identify the task, and the model brings the knowledge.

An algorithmic view. Garg et al. (2022) trained transformers on prompts made of $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$ pairs from random linear functions, and found that they learn to predict $y$ for a new $\mathbf{x}$ almost as well as least squares on the in-context examples. How can a forward pass do regression? Von Oswald et al. (2023) gave an exact construction. Drop the softmax (linear self-attention) and let each token be $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$, with the query token $(\mathbf{x}_q, 0)$. Choose the query and key matrices to read off $\mathbf{x}$, and the value matrix to read off $y$ scaled by $\eta/n$. The attention output at the query is then

$$\sum_{i=1}^n(\mathbf{x}_q^\top\mathbf{x}_i)\frac{\eta}{n}y_i = \mathbf{x}_q^\top\Big(\frac{\eta}{n}\sum_i y_i\mathbf{x}_i\Big) = \mathbf{x}_q^\top\mathbf{w}_1,$$

where $\mathbf{w}_1 = \frac{\eta}{n}\sum_i y_i\mathbf{x}_i$ is exactly one gradient-descent step on the squared error $\frac{1}{2n}\sum_i(y_i - \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i)^2$ from $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$. Stacking layers gives more steps. Trained transformers were found to implement something close to this. Not every in-context learning is gradient descent, but it shows that an architecture with fixed weights can run a learning algorithm on its input.

A mechanistic view. Olsson et al. (2022) found induction heads in trained transformers: a pair of attention heads that implements "find the previous occurrence of the current token and copy what followed it". In text "…Harry Potter… Harry", they predict "Potter". Induction heads appear abruptly during training, at the same point as a sudden improvement in in-context learning, and are a simple, concrete mechanism for using the context.

Chain of thought

A transformer does a fixed amount of computation per token: a fixed number of layers, each a fixed number of operations. A problem that needs more sequential steps than the model has layers cannot be solved in a single forward pass, however the weights are set. Writing out intermediate results lets the model use the tokens it has generated as a scratchpad. Each new token's computation can build on all the previous ones, so the total computation grows with the length of the chain. Theory supports this: with a chain of thought of polynomial length, transformers can solve problems that are believed impossible for them without one (Feng et al., 2023; Merrill and Sabharwal, 2024).

There is also a learning argument, which the code demonstrates. The parity of $n$ bits is a famously hard function for gradient descent to learn from examples: each subset of bits is uncorrelated with the answer unless it includes all $n$ bits, so the gradient carries almost no signal. But parity decomposes into $n$ trivial steps, each the exclusive-or of the running parity with the next bit. A model trained to produce the chain, one running parity at a time, only has to learn the easy step, and then the chain computes the hard function. Breaking a task into sub-tasks the model can learn is what intermediate steps buy (Wies et al., 2023).

Chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) showed the model worked examples; Kojima et al. (2022) found that just adding "Let's think step by step" to a question had a similar effect on large models. Both gains appeared only in models above roughly 100 billion parameters at the time, presumably because smaller models could not yet produce correct steps reliably.

Spending more compute at test time

If a model can produce several different chains of reasoning for the same problem, sampling more of them should help. Three ways to use them:

Majority vote (self-consistency). Sample $N$ chains, extract their final answers, and return the most common answer (Wang et al., 2023). Suppose each chain's answer is correct with probability $p_c$ and otherwise is spread over wrong answers, the most likely of which has probability $p_w$. By the law of large numbers, the answer counts divided by $N$ converge to these probabilities, so the majority answer converges to the correct one if and only if $p_c > p_w$. Note that the condition is not $p_c > 1/2$: a model right only 40% of the time still converges to the right answer if no single wrong answer is as likely. But a systematic error, a wrong answer the model prefers, is amplified: if one wrong answer has probability 0.6, voting converges to it. This is Condorcet's jury theorem for many candidates.

Accuracy of the majority answer from the code's simulation (4,000 trials per point, ties broken at random). The dashed line is the single-sample accuracy of the first two models.

Best-of-$N$ with a verifier. Instead of voting, score each chain with a learned verifier or reward model and return the best. This also works for problems whose answers are not short strings. Its limit is the verifier: with enough samples, best-of-$N$ finds chains that fool it, the over-optimisation of lesson 28 again. Lightman et al. (2023) found that verifiers trained to judge each step of a solution ("process supervision") were much more reliable than ones that only judge the final answer.

pass@k. For code, tests can check a solution exactly, so a problem counts as solved if any of $k$ samples passes. To estimate pass@$k$ without bias, draw $n\ge k$ samples, count the $c$ that pass, and compute the probability that a random subset of $k$ of them contains at least one passing sample (Chen et al., 2021):

$$\widehat{\text{pass@}k} = 1 - \binom{n - c}{k}\Big/\binom{n}{k}.$$

Taking the fraction of $n$ samples that pass, and computing $1 - (1 - c/n)^k$, is biased: it is a concave function of an unbiased estimate (exercise 5).

Reasoning models

Prompting elicits chains of thought; training can improve them. The reasoning models released from late 2024, OpenAI's o1 and DeepSeek-R1 among them, are trained with reinforcement learning on problems whose answers can be checked: maths with known answers, code with tests. The reward is simply whether the final answer is right (lesson 28's "verifiable rewards"), and the policy-gradient update raises the probability of every token in chains that ended correctly. DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) used GRPO: sample a group of chains for each problem, and use each chain's reward minus the group's mean, divided by the group's standard deviation, as its advantage. They reported that, with this training alone, the chains grew longer over training and began to show behaviours nobody had programmed: checking intermediate results, and abandoning an approach to try another.

The result is a new axis of scaling. Accuracy rises smoothly with the length of the chain the model is allowed to think for, much as pretraining loss falls with compute. Thinking longer at test time can substitute for a much larger model. The open questions are about faithfulness, whether the written chain is the reasoning the model actually used, and about how far RL on checkable problems carries to problems that cannot be checked.

Code from scratch

Part 1 applies temperature, top-$k$ and top-$p$ to a Zipf-like distribution over 1,000 tokens and measures what each leaves. Part 2 builds the linear self-attention layer above and compares its output with one step of gradient descent on 20 in-context examples. Part 3 trains the same small MLP (128 ReLU units, Adam, 4,000 steps of batch 128) on 5,000 random 24-bit strings in two ways. The direct model maps the bits to their parity. The chain model maps the bits, a one-hot code of the step number and its own previous output to the next running parity; at test time it is run for 24 steps, feeding back its own outputs. Part 4 simulates majority voting for a model that is right 40% of the time. Part 5 evaluates the pass@$k$ estimator.

import numpy as np
from math import comb

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. Decoding rules applied to one next-token distribution (Zipf-like over 1,000 tokens).
p = 1 / np.arange(1, 1001) ** 1.1; p /= p.sum()
H = lambda q: -np.sum(q[q > 0] * np.log2(q[q > 0]))
def top_k(p, k):
    q = np.where(np.arange(len(p)) < k, p, 0); return q / q.sum()          # p is sorted, largest first
def top_p(p, thr):
    keep = np.cumsum(p) - p < thr                                        # smallest prefix with mass >= thr
    q = np.where(keep, p, 0); return q / q.sum(), keep.sum()
def temperature(p, tau):
    q = p ** (1 / tau); return q / q.sum()
print(f"model's distribution: entropy {H(p):.2f} bits, top token {p[0]:.3f}, top 10 hold {p[:10].sum():.3f}")
print(f"temperature 0.7: entropy {H(temperature(p, 0.7)):.2f} bits;  top-k 50: {H(top_k(p, 50)):.2f} bits")
for thr in [0.5, 0.9, 0.95]:
    q, n = top_p(p, thr)
    print(f"top-p {thr}: keeps {n} tokens, entropy {H(q):.2f} bits")

# 2. In-context learning as an algorithm: one layer of linear self-attention, with suitable
# weights, makes exactly the prediction of one gradient-descent step on the in-context examples
# (von Oswald et al., 2023). Tokens are (x_i, y_i); the query token is (x_q, 0).
d, n_ctx, lr = 5, 20, 0.1
w_true = rng.standard_normal(d)
Xc = rng.standard_normal((n_ctx, d)); yc = Xc @ w_true
xq = rng.standard_normal(d)
Z = np.vstack([np.column_stack([Xc, yc]), np.append(xq, 0)])             # (n_ctx + 1) tokens of size d + 1
WK = np.zeros((d + 1, d + 1)); WK[:d, :d] = np.eye(d)                      # keys and queries read x
WQ = WK.copy()
WV = np.zeros((d + 1, d + 1)); WV[d, d] = lr / n_ctx                       # values read y, scaled
attn = (Z @ WQ) @ (Z[:-1] @ WK).T                                          # linear attention, no softmax,
lsa_pred = (attn @ (Z[:-1] @ WV))[-1, d]                                   # over the context tokens only
w_gd = lr * Xc.T @ yc / n_ctx                                              # one GD step on 1/2 mean sq. error from w = 0
print(f"linear self-attention prediction {lsa_pred:.6f}; one step of gradient descent {w_gd @ xq:.6f}")

# 3. Chain of thought as decomposition: the parity of 24 random bits. The same small MLP is
# trained either to output the parity directly, or to produce the running parity one step at
# a time, where each step sees the bits, which step it is on, and its own previous output.
n_bits, n_train = 24, 5000
def mlp_train(X, y, steps=4000, hidden=128, lr=0.01, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    W1 = r.standard_normal((X.shape[1], hidden)) * np.sqrt(2 / X.shape[1]); b1 = np.zeros(hidden)
    w2 = r.standard_normal(hidden) * np.sqrt(1 / hidden); b2 = 0.0
    params = [W1, b1, w2]; ms = [np.zeros_like(q) for q in params]; vs = [np.zeros_like(q) for q in params]
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        idx = r.integers(0, len(X), 128)
        h = np.maximum(X[idx] @ W1 + b1, 0); z = h @ w2 + b2
        g = (1 / (1 + np.exp(-z)) - y[idx]) / len(idx)                     # logistic loss gradient
        gh = np.outer(g, w2) * (h > 0)
        grads = [X[idx].T @ gh, gh.sum(0), h.T @ g]; b2 -= lr * g.sum()
        for q, gq, m, v in zip(params, grads, ms, vs):
            m *= 0.9; m += 0.1 * gq; v *= 0.999; v += 0.001 * gq * gq
            q -= lr * (m / (1 - 0.9 ** t)) / (np.sqrt(v / (1 - 0.999 ** t)) + 1e-8)
    return lambda X: (np.maximum(X @ W1 + b1, 0) @ w2 + b2) > 0
bits = rng.integers(0, 2, (n_train, n_bits)); test = rng.integers(0, 2, (2000, n_bits))
direct = mlp_train(2.0 * bits - 1, bits.sum(1) % 2)
print(f"direct: train accuracy {np.mean(direct(2.0 * bits - 1) == bits.sum(1) % 2):.3f},"
      f" test accuracy {np.mean(direct(2.0 * test - 1) == test.sum(1) % 2):.3f}")
def step_inputs(B, i, prev):                       # the bits, a one-hot of the step, the previous output
    return np.column_stack([2.0 * B - 1, np.eye(n_bits)[np.full(len(B), i)], 2.0 * prev - 1])
running = np.cumsum(bits, 1) % 2
Xs = np.vstack([step_inputs(bits, i, running[:, i - 1] if i else np.zeros(n_train)) for i in range(n_bits)])
ys = np.concatenate([running[:, i] for i in range(n_bits)])
step = mlp_train(Xs, ys)
prev = np.zeros(len(test))
for i in range(n_bits):                            # generate the chain, feeding back its own outputs
    prev = step(step_inputs(test, i, prev)).astype(float)
run_t = np.cumsum(test, 1) % 2
per_step = np.mean([step(step_inputs(test, i, run_t[:, i - 1] if i else np.zeros(len(test)))) == run_t[:, i]
                    for i in range(n_bits)])
print(f"chain of thought: each step right {per_step:.4f} of the time on test strings;"
      f" final answer right {np.mean(prev == test.sum(1) % 2):.3f} after {n_bits} steps")

# 4. Self-consistency: sample several reasoning chains and take the majority answer. A model
# that is right 40% of the time and spreads its wrong answers over many values.
def majority_accuracy(p_right, n_wrong, N, trials=20000, seed=1):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    probs = np.append(p_right, np.full(n_wrong, (1 - p_right) / n_wrong))
    wins = 0.0
    for counts in r.multinomial(N, probs, size=trials):
        top = np.flatnonzero(counts == counts.max())
        wins += (0 in top) / len(top)                                      # ties broken at random
    return wins / trials
for N in [1, 5, 15, 41]:
    print(f"majority of {N:2d} samples: accuracy {majority_accuracy(0.4, 10, N):.3f}"
          f" (wrong answers spread over 10 values) vs {majority_accuracy(0.4, 1, N):.3f} (one wrong answer)")

# 5. pass@k, estimated without bias from n samples of which c are correct (Chen et al., 2021).
n, c = 20, 3
est = lambda k: 1 - comb(n - c, k) / comb(n, k)
print(f"pass@k from {c} correct of {n} samples: " + ", ".join(f"k={k}: {est(k):.3f}" for k in [1, 5, 10]))

# model's distribution: entropy 6.80 bits, top token 0.179, top 10 hold 0.481
# temperature 0.7: entropy 3.85 bits;  top-k 50: 4.37 bits
# top-p 0.5: keeps 12 tokens, entropy 2.98 bits
# top-p 0.9: keeps 349 tokens, entropy 6.01 bits
# top-p 0.95: keeps 582 tokens, entropy 6.40 bits
# linear self-attention prediction -0.017513; one step of gradient descent -0.017513
# direct: train accuracy 0.935, test accuracy 0.493
# chain of thought: each step right 0.9998 of the time on test strings; final answer right 0.995 after 24 steps
# majority of  1 samples: accuracy 0.405 (wrong answers spread over 10 values) vs 0.398 (one wrong answer)
# majority of  5 samples: accuracy 0.637 (wrong answers spread over 10 values) vs 0.318 (one wrong answer)
# majority of 15 samples: accuracy 0.923 (wrong answers spread over 10 values) vs 0.213 (one wrong answer)
# majority of 41 samples: accuracy 0.999 (wrong answers spread over 10 values) vs 0.099 (one wrong answer)
# pass@k from 3 correct of 20 samples: k=1: 0.150, k=5: 0.601, k=10: 0.895

Reading the output. Top-$p$ adapts where top-$k$ cannot: on this long-tailed distribution, a nucleus of 0.9 keeps 349 tokens and 6.0 bits of entropy, while 0.5 keeps 12 tokens. The linear attention layer and the gradient step agree to all six digits printed, as the algebra says. The parity experiment is the clearest result. Trained directly, the network fits 93.5% of its 5,000 training strings and gets 49.3% of new strings right, which is chance: it memorised, because nothing short of all 24 bits predicts the answer. (Direct learning is not impossible, only expensive: with ten times as many strings and five times as many steps, the same network reaches 82%; problem set P7 explores this.) Trained to produce the chain, the same network learns a step that is right 99.98% of the time, and run for 24 steps it gets 99.5% of new strings right. The 0.5% that fail are almost exactly what 24 steps at 99.98% each would predict ($1 - 0.9998^{24}\approx0.5\%$). Errors compound along a chain, which is why long chains need reliable steps, or a way to check them. Majority voting turns 40% accuracy into 92.3% with 15 samples and 99.9% with 41 when wrong answers are scattered, but drives it down to 9.9% with 41 samples when one wrong answer has probability 0.6.

Exercises

  1. Show that top-$p$ sampling with threshold $p$ never removes a token whose probability is larger than that of a token it keeps, and that the kept set has total probability at least $p$. When are top-$k$ and top-$p$ the same rule?
  2. In the linear self-attention construction, what would a second layer have to compute to perform a second gradient step? (Hint: the residual stream can carry the current prediction errors $y_i - \mathbf{w}_1^\top\mathbf{x}_i$.)
  3. For the direct parity model: show that for any proper subset $S$ of the bits, the parity of the bits in $S$ is independent of the parity of all $n$ bits, when the bits are independent and uniform. Why does this make the gradient uninformative?
  4. A model answers correctly with probability 0.4 and otherwise gives one of 10 wrong answers, each with probability 0.06. Using the normal approximation to the counts, roughly how many samples does majority voting need to be right 90% of the time? Compare with the code's numbers.
  5. Show that $\binom{n - c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}$ is the probability that $k$ samples drawn without replacement from the $n$ include none of the $c$ correct ones, and that the estimator is unbiased for $1 - (1 - p)^k$ when each sample is correct with probability $p$. Show that $1 - (1 - c/n)^k$ is biased downwards.
  6. A chain of $m$ steps succeeds if every step does, each independently with probability $s$. How large must $s$ be for a 1,000-step chain to succeed 90% of the time? What does this suggest about long reasoning without checking?
Answers

1. Top-$p$ keeps a prefix of the tokens sorted by probability: it adds tokens in decreasing order until the total reaches $p$, so any kept token is at least as likely as any removed one, and the kept total is at least $p$ by construction. The rules coincide on a given distribution when the nucleus happens to have exactly $k$ tokens; they differ in how the set size varies between distributions.

2. It must update $\mathbf{w}_2 = \mathbf{w}_1 + \frac{\eta}{n}\sum_i(y_i - \mathbf{w}_1^\top\mathbf{x}_i)\mathbf{x}_i$, so the values in the second layer must be the residuals $y_i - \mathbf{w}_1^\top\mathbf{x}_i$. The first layer, applied at every token (not only the query), can write $-\mathbf{w}_1^\top\mathbf{x}_i$ into each token's $y$ slot through the residual connection, after which the second layer repeats the first one's construction.

3. Let $j\notin S$. Conditional on all bits except $b_j$, the full parity is $b_j$ XOR (something fixed), uniform whatever the parity of $S$ is. So the full parity is uniform given the parity of $S$, that is, independent of it. A network's gradient at initialisation is a sum of correlations between the label and functions of the inputs; low-order features have zero correlation with the label, so the gradient carries no signal until the network happens to represent the full 24-way interaction.

4. The majority is decided between the correct answer (mean $0.4N$, variance $0.24N$) and the leading wrong answer (each has mean $0.06N$). The difference with any single wrong answer has mean $0.34N$ and variance about $N(0.4 + 0.06) - N(0.34)^2 = 0.344N$, so a deficit has probability about $\Phi(-0.34\sqrt N/0.587)$. Requiring the right answer to beat all 10 wrong ones with total failure probability around 10% gives roughly $\Phi(-0.58\sqrt N)\approx0.01$, $\sqrt N\approx4$, $N\approx16$; the code finds 92.3% at $N = 15$.

5. Choosing $k$ of the $n$ samples uniformly, there are $\binom{n}{k}$ subsets, of which $\binom{n - c}{k}$ avoid the correct ones. For unbiasedness: a uniformly random $k$-subset of i.i.d. samples is itself $k$ i.i.d. samples, so the probability that it contains no correct one, averaged over the $n$ samples, is $(1 - p)^k$. And $(1 - \hat p)^k$ with $\hat p = c/n$ is convex in $\hat p$, so by Jensen's inequality $\mathbb{E}(1 - \hat p)^k\ge(1 - p)^k$, which makes $1 - (1 - \hat p)^k$ biased downwards.

6. $s^{1000}\ge0.9$ needs $s\ge0.9^{1/1000} = 0.999895$: an error rate of about 1 in 10,000 per step. Long chains are only possible if errors can be noticed and fixed along the way, which is why checking and backtracking matter so much in reasoning models.
