Lesson 42 · Stage 9: reading the papers

A guided tour of landmark papers

Every lesson in this course rests on a handful of papers. This last lesson is about reading them: how to get through a research paper efficiently, what to check before believing its claims, and how to turn a paper into code. It then maps about seventy landmark papers onto the stages of the course, each with what it contributed and the lesson that explains it, so that you can go to the source for any idea you have learned here. A short list of papers to read first, and exercises that practise reading critically, close the course.

Prerequisites: whichever lessons the papers you read belong toMath pulled in: none new; the point is to use what you have

How to read a paper

A paper is an argument that some idea works, written by people who want you to believe it. Reading it well means extracting the idea quickly and then checking the argument. Keshav's three-pass method is a good default.

  1. First pass, ten minutes. Read the title, abstract and introduction, the section headings, the conclusion, and glance at the figures and tables. Answer: what problem is this, what is the claimed contribution, what kind of evidence (a theorem, an experiment, both), and does it matter to you? Most papers stop here.
  2. Second pass, an hour. Read the whole paper but skip proofs and implementation details. Look hard at the main figure and the main results table. Write down the method in your own notation: the model, the objective, the algorithm. Mark the terms you do not understand and the references you would need.
  3. Third pass, as long as it takes. Re-derive the main equations and, if it matters, re-implement the method. Assume nothing: every step that the paper calls "straightforward" is a step you should be able to do. This is where you find the hidden assumptions, and it is how every lesson in this course was built.

Machine learning papers have a typical skeleton: a problem, a method section with one central equation (the objective or the update rule), experiments on standard benchmarks with a results table against baselines, ablations that remove one ingredient at a time, and a related-work section. The central equation and the ablation table are usually the most informative parts. If you can write the central equation and say which ablation hurts most, you understand the paper.

Reading critically

Before believing an empirical claim, check:

  • Baselines. Are the baselines tuned as carefully as the proposed method? A new optimiser compared with an untuned SGD proves little. Henderson et al. (2018) showed that in deep RL, results changed sign with random seeds and implementation details; Engstrom et al. (2020) found that much of PPO's advantage over TRPO came from code-level choices rather than the clipped objective (lesson 39).
  • Variance. How many seeds, and are there error bars? A difference smaller than the spread across seeds is noise. Lessons 37 and 39 show how much a deep RL result moves between three seeds.
  • The evaluation. Is the test set really held out? Were hyperparameters tuned on it (lesson 04)? Is the metric the one that matters, and is the comparison at equal compute, equal data and equal parameter count?
  • Ablations. Does each claimed ingredient have an experiment that removes it? An ingredient without an ablation may not matter.
  • Scope. Results on one dataset or one model size do not always transfer. Scaling papers (lesson 27) exist because many conclusions change with scale.
  • Theory. For a theorem, read the assumptions first. Convergence guarantees often assume convexity, bounded gradients, tabular states or infinite data; lesson 37's deadly triad is what happens when the assumptions of tabular convergence proofs are dropped.

From paper to code

Implementing a paper is the only way to be sure you understand it. Start with the smallest version that can be checked: a toy problem where the answer is known, as every assignment in this course does. Check every gradient against finite differences. Reproduce one number from the paper before changing anything. When the result does not match, the cause is usually one of: a detail in the appendix (initialisation, a normalisation, a learning-rate schedule), a difference in data preprocessing, or a bug that a small test would catch. Code released with the paper is the final authority on details the text leaves out, and it often contains details the text never mentions.

Ten to read first

If you read only ten papers in full, read these: each is short enough, clear enough and central enough to repay a third pass.

  1. Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986): four pages that made backpropagation standard.
  2. He et al. (2016), ResNet: one idea, a clean ablation, and the reason networks got deep.
  3. Kingma & Welling (2014), VAE: a derivation you can follow line by line with lesson 22.
  4. Vaswani et al. (2017): read it with lesson 20 and A6 open, and check every dimension.
  5. Ho, Jain & Abbeel (2020), DDPM: the loss in its section 3.4 is the result of pages of algebra in lesson 24.
  6. Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022) together: how an empirical law is measured, and how a second paper revised the first.
  7. Rafailov et al. (2023), DPO: a short derivation that removed a whole pipeline.
  8. Mnih et al. (2015), DQN: its methods section is a complete recipe; compare it with lesson 37's code.
  9. Schulman et al. (2017), PPO: eight pages; then read Engstrom et al. (2020) to see what the paper leaves out.
  10. Silver et al. (2017), AlphaGo Zero: the cleanest statement of search as policy improvement.

Exercises

  1. Do a first pass of the PPO paper in ten minutes, and write three sentences: the problem, the contribution, the evidence. Then compare them with lesson 39.
  2. In the ResNet paper, find the experiment that shows residual connections help optimisation rather than generalisation. What would you expect to see in training error, and what do they report?
  3. The Chinchilla paper revised Kaplan et al.'s compute-optimal allocation. Find the main methodological difference that the Chinchilla authors identify, and explain it with lesson 27's notation.
  4. Pick one table of results from any paper in the map. For each row, write what would have to be true for the comparison to be fair (equal compute, equal tuning, same data). Which conditions can you verify from the paper?
  5. Choose a paper from the map whose lesson does not reproduce its main result, and write the smallest experiment that would check its central claim, in the style of this course's code sections: a toy problem with a known answer and a number to compare.
Answers

1. Problem: policy-gradient methods are sample-inefficient because each batch supports only one gradient step, and TRPO, which allows larger steps, is complicated (second-order, a constrained problem per update) and hard to combine with shared policy–value networks. Contribution: a clipped surrogate objective that removes the incentive to move the probability ratio outside $[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$, allowing several epochs of first-order minibatch updates per batch. Evidence: continuous-control benchmarks and Atari, against TRPO, A2C and others, plus a comparison of surrogate variants (no clipping, clipping, adaptive KL penalty).

2. They compare plain and residual networks of 18 and 34 layers on ImageNet. The plain 34-layer network has higher training error than the plain 18-layer one, so its problem is optimisation, not overfitting; with residual connections, the 34-layer network has lower training and validation error than the 18-layer one.

3. Kaplan et al. trained models with a fixed learning-rate schedule length regardless of the number of tokens, so models trained for fewer tokens than the schedule assumed were stopped early in an un-annealed state, which underestimates the benefit of more data. Chinchilla matched the cosine schedule to each run's length, and fitted $L(N, D)$ with three methods that agree on $N_\text{opt}\propto C^{0.5}$, $D_\text{opt}\propto C^{0.5}$, roughly 20 tokens per parameter, against Kaplan's $N_\text{opt}\propto C^{0.73}$.

4. An open-ended exercise. Typical findings: compute is often not reported for baselines; baselines are often taken from earlier papers, with different preprocessing or tuning budgets; the number of seeds is often one. Note what is verifiable from the text, the appendix and the released code.

5. An open-ended exercise. For example, for double Q-learning: the maximisation-bias MDP of lesson 36, comparing the fraction of left actions; for batch normalisation's claimed reduction of "internal covariate shift": train with and without it and measure both the shift in each layer's input distribution and the training speed, as Santurkar et al. (2018) did, finding that the speed-up remains even when the shift does not shrink.
