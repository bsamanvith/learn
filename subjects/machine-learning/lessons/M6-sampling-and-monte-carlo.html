Sampling and Monte Carlo

Much of machine learning replaces an expectation it cannot compute by an average over samples: stochastic gradients, the ELBO of a VAE, the policy gradient, the value of a state, a tree search's estimate of a move. This page explains why that works and how fast, how to draw samples from a distribution you can only write down, how to estimate rare or off-distribution quantities with importance sampling, how to reduce variance, how Markov chain Monte Carlo samples from a distribution known only up to a constant, and the two ways of differentiating an expectation.

Used by: 07, 22, 24, 31, 35, 38, 40Assumes: expectation and variance, the central limit theorem (M3)

The Monte Carlo estimator

To compute $\mu = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f(x)]$, draw $x_1, \dots, x_n$ independently from $p$ and average:

$$\hat\mu_n = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nf(x_i),\qquad\mathbb{E}[\hat\mu_n] = \mu,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_n) = \frac{\sigma_f^2}{n},\quad\sigma_f^2 = \operatorname{Var}_p(f(x)).$$

The estimator is unbiased, and its variance follows from the variances of independent terms adding. By the central limit theorem, $\hat\mu_n$ is approximately $N(\mu, \sigma_f^2/n)$ for large $n$, so $\hat\mu_n\pm1.96\,\hat\sigma_f/\sqrt n$ is an approximate 95% confidence interval, with $\hat\sigma_f$ the sample standard deviation.

The error is $\sigma_f/\sqrt n$, in any dimension

Nothing in $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_n) = \sigma_f^2/n$ depends on the dimension of $x$. A grid-based rule, by contrast, needs $m^d$ points for $m$ per axis, so with a fixed budget its resolution per axis collapses as $d$ grows. The price is the slow rate: halving the error costs four times the samples.

The code integrates $f(x) = \prod_{i=1}^d\frac\pi2\sin(\pi x_i)$ over $[0, 1]^d$, whose integral is exactly 1. The RMS error over 200 repetitions falls with slope $-0.495$ ($d = 1$) and $-0.504$ ($d = 10$) on log-log axes, and at $n = 100{,}000$ it is 0.0017 and 0.0083, matching $\sigma_f/\sqrt n$ = 0.0015 and 0.0084 (the 10-dimensional integrand has a larger $\sigma_f$, 2.67 against 0.48). In one dimension the midpoint rule with 100,000 points is far better, with error $4\times10^{-11}$. In ten dimensions, the midpoint rule with three points per axis already needs 59,049 points and is off by 0.586.

Drawing samples

Inverse transform. If $F$ is a continuous CDF and $U\sim\text{Uniform}(0, 1)$, then $X = F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$, because $P(F^{-1}(U)\le x) = P(U\le F(x)) = F(x)$. For the exponential distribution, $F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$ gives $X = -\ln(1 - U)/\lambda$. Sampling from a categorical distribution is the discrete version: find the first index whose cumulative probability exceeds $U$.

Box–Muller. The Gaussian CDF has no closed-form inverse, but in two dimensions the polar form does: with $U_1, U_2$ uniform, $R = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}$ and $\Theta = 2\pi U_2$, the pair $(R\cos\Theta, R\sin\Theta)$ is two independent standard normals. (The squared radius of a 2D standard normal is exponential with rate $\tfrac12$, and its angle is uniform.)

Rejection sampling. To sample from a density $p$ known up to a constant, $p = \tilde p/Z$, find a proposal $q$ that is easy to sample and a constant $M$ with $\tilde p(x)\le Mq(x)$ everywhere. Draw $x\sim q$ and accept it with probability $\tilde p(x)/(Mq(x))$. The accepted samples have density proportional to $q(x)\cdot\tilde p(x)/(Mq(x))\propto\tilde p(x)$, exactly $p$. The acceptance rate is $Z/M$, which in high dimension is usually exponentially small: the bound $M$ must hold in every direction at once.

Importance sampling

Often the samples come from the wrong distribution: an off-policy agent acts with $\mu$ but wants values under $\pi$ (lessons 35–37), PPO reuses a batch from the old policy (lesson 39), or $p$ puts almost no mass where $f$ matters. Multiply and divide by a proposal $q$ with $q(x) > 0$ wherever $f(x)p(x)\ne0$:

$$\mathbb{E}_p[f(x)] = \int f(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}q(x)\,dx = \mathbb{E}_q[f(x)w(x)],\qquad w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.$$

So $\hat\mu^\text{IS} = \frac1n\sum_if(x_i)w(x_i)$ with $x_i\sim q$ is unbiased. Its variance is $\frac1n\big(\mathbb{E}_q[f^2w^2] - \mu^2\big)$, which can be much smaller or much larger than plain Monte Carlo's.

The best proposal

For $f\ge0$, the proposal $q^\star(x) = f(x)p(x)/\mu$ gives $f(x)w(x) = \mu$ for every sample: zero variance. In general the variance is minimised by $q^\star\propto|f|p$.

$q^\star$ needs $\mu$, the answer, so it is never available, but it says what a good proposal looks like: it puts its mass where $|f|p$ is large. A proposal that is too narrow, or has lighter tails than $|f|p$, can make the variance infinite: rare samples with enormous weights dominate the sum. A useful diagnostic is the effective sample size of the weights $v_i = f(x_i)w(x_i)$ (or of $w_i$ alone), $\text{ESS} = (\sum_iv_i)^2/\sum_iv_i^2$, which equals $n$ when all are equal and 1 when one dominates.

The code estimates $P(Z > 4) = 3.17\times10^{-5}$ for $Z\sim N(0, 1)$ with $n = 10{,}000$. Plain Monte Carlo sees no sample beyond 4 in 71% of the 200 runs, and its relative RMS error is 1.59. Sampling from $N(s, 1)$ with weight $w(y) = e^{-sy + s^2/2}$ gives relative errors of 0.073 for $s = 2$, 0.022 for $s = 4$ and 0.061 for $s = 6$; the effective sample sizes are 181, 1,831 and 296 of 10,000. At $s = 2$ only 2% of the samples land in the event; at $s = 6$, 98% do, but the weights $e^{-6y + 18}$ vary enormously across the event, so a few samples near 4 dominate. The shift that centres the proposal on the event's boundary does best, which is roughly where $q^\star\propto\mathbf{1}[z > 4]\,p(z)$ has its mass.

Self-normalised importance sampling. If $p = \tilde p/Z$ is known only up to its constant, use $\tilde w_i = \tilde p(x_i)/q(x_i)$ and $\hat\mu = \sum_if(x_i)\tilde w_i/\sum_i\tilde w_i$. The constant cancels; the estimator is biased for finite $n$ but consistent.

Reducing variance

Control variates. If $g$ has a known mean $\mathbb{E}[g]$, then $\hat\mu_c = \frac1n\sum_i\big(f(x_i) + c(g(x_i) - \mathbb{E}[g])\big)$ is unbiased for every $c$, with variance $\frac1n\big(\sigma_f^2 + 2c\operatorname{Cov}(f, g) + c^2\sigma_g^2\big)$. Minimising over $c$:

$$c^\star = -\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(f, g)}{\operatorname{Var}(g)},\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_{c^\star}) = \frac{\sigma_f^2}{n}\big(1 - \rho_{fg}^2\big).$$

The better $g$ tracks $f$, the more variance it removes. A baseline in REINFORCE (lesson 38) is a control variate: $\nabla\log\pi\cdot b$ has mean zero, and subtracting it removes the part of the gradient noise that is explained by the baseline.

Antithetic variates. Pair each sample with a mirror image that has the same distribution, such as $U$ and $1 - U$, or $z$ and $-z$. If $f$ is monotone, the two values are negatively correlated, and their average has less variance than two independent samples.

For $\mathbb{E}[e^U] = e - 1$ with $U$ uniform and $n = 1{,}000$ evaluations, the code's plain estimate has standard deviation $1.52\times10^{-2}$; antithetic pairs reduce the variance to 3.5% of that, and the control variate $g(U) = U$, with $c = -1.688$ estimated from a pilot run (the optimum is $-1.690$), reduces it to 1.7%. Here $\rho_{fg} = 0.992$, so the formula predicts $1 - \rho^2 = 0.016$ (exercise 6), close to the measured 0.017.

Markov chain Monte Carlo

When $p = \tilde p/Z$ is known only up to its constant and there is no good proposal for rejection or importance sampling, which is the usual situation for Bayesian posteriors and energy-based models, build a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is $p$ and average along the chain. Lesson 31 shows that an ergodic chain's long-run averages converge to expectations under its stationary distribution.

Metropolis–Hastings. From $x$, propose $y\sim q(y\mid x)$ and accept with probability

$$\alpha(x, y) = \min\Big(1, \frac{\tilde p(y)q(x\mid y)}{\tilde p(x)q(y\mid x)}\Big),$$

otherwise stay at $x$. Then $\pi(x)P(x, y) = \min\big(p(x)q(y\mid x), p(y)q(x\mid y)\big)$ is symmetric in $x$ and $y$: the chain satisfies detailed balance with respect to $p$, so $p$ is stationary (lesson 31; P8, problem 3). Only the ratio $\tilde p(y)/\tilde p(x)$ appears, so $Z$ is never needed. With a symmetric random-walk proposal, $y = x + \sigma\epsilon$, the $q$ terms cancel.

Gibbs sampling updates one coordinate at a time from its conditional distribution $p(x_j\mid x_{-j})$; it is Metropolis–Hastings with a proposal that is always accepted. Langevin dynamics, $x\leftarrow x + \frac\epsilon2\nabla\log p(x) + \sqrt\epsilon\,z$, uses the gradient of $\log p$, the score, to propose moves uphill in probability; it is the sampler behind score-based diffusion models (lesson 25).

The samples of a chain are correlated, so $n$ steps are worth fewer than $n$ independent draws. With autocorrelations $\rho_k$ at lag $k$, the variance of the chain average is about $\frac{\sigma_f^2}{n}(1 + 2\sum_{k\ge1}\rho_k)$, and $n/(1 + 2\sum_k\rho_k)$ is the chain's effective sample size. The proposal width trades acceptance against step size. The code's target has two modes, at $\pm3$:

  • Width 0.1: 97% of proposals are accepted but each moves almost nothing. After 100,000 steps, 19.5% of the samples have $x > 0$, against the true 50%: the chain barely crossed between the modes, and its effective sample size is about 30. Its average of $x^2$ is 10.01, correct by luck, because both modes have the same $\mathbb{E}[x^2]$. A chain can look converged on some statistics and be wrong on others.
  • Width 2.5: 49% accepted, 51.1% of samples with $x > 0$, effective sample size 4,786.
  • Width 50: only 5% accepted, the chain sits still for long stretches, effective sample size 2,708.

Differentiating an expectation

Training often needs $\nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}[f(x)]$, where the distribution itself depends on $\theta$. There are two standard estimators.

The score function (likelihood ratio, REINFORCE): since $\nabla_\theta p_\theta = p_\theta\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta$,

$$\nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}\big[f(x)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\big].$$

It needs only samples and $\nabla\log p_\theta$, and works for discrete $x$ and for $f$ that is not differentiable, such as a reward from an environment (lesson 38). Its variance is often large, and a baseline $f - b$ reduces it.

The reparameterisation trick: if $x = g_\theta(\epsilon)$ with $\epsilon$ from a fixed distribution, such as $x = \mu + \sigma\epsilon$ with $\epsilon\sim N(0, 1)$, then

$$\nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)] = \mathbb{E}_\epsilon\big[\nabla_\theta f(g_\theta(\epsilon))\big] = \mathbb{E}_\epsilon\big[f'(g_\theta(\epsilon))\nabla_\theta g_\theta(\epsilon)\big].$$

It needs $f$ differentiable and $x$ continuous, and it uses $f$'s gradient, which carries much more information than $f$'s value: this is why VAEs train with it (lesson 22). For $\frac{d}{d\mu}\mathbb{E}[x^2]$ with $x\sim N(1, 0.5^2)$, true value 2, the code's 100,000 samples give a standard deviation per sample of 4.70 for the score function, 3.27 with the baseline $\mathbb{E}[f]$, and 1.00 for the reparameterisation estimator.

Code from scratch

Part 1 measures Monte Carlo and midpoint-rule errors in 1 and 10 dimensions. Part 2 estimates a Gaussian tail probability with and without importance sampling. Part 3 compares plain, antithetic and control-variate estimates. Part 4 runs Metropolis–Hastings with three proposal widths and estimates each chain's effective sample size, summing autocorrelations until they fall below 0.05. Part 5 compares the gradient estimators.

import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# ---- Part 1: plain Monte Carlo. The error falls as 1/sqrt(n) whatever the dimension.
# The integrand is f(x) = prod_i (pi/2) sin(pi x_i) on [0, 1]^d, whose integral is exactly 1.
def f(x):
    return np.prod(np.pi / 2 * np.sin(np.pi * x), axis=1)

ns = [100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000]
mc_err = {}
for d in (1, 10):
    mc_err[d] = []
    for n in ns:
        est = [f(rng.random((n, d))).mean() for _ in range(200)]
        mc_err[d].append(np.sqrt(np.mean((np.array(est) - 1) ** 2)))
    slope = np.polyfit(np.log10(ns), np.log10(mc_err[d]), 1)[0]
    sd = f(rng.random((1_000_000, d))).std()
    print(f"d = {d:2d}: RMS error at n = 100 ... 100,000: {', '.join(f'{e:.4f}' for e in mc_err[d])};"
          f" slope {slope:.3f}; sigma_f = {sd:.3f}, sigma_f / sqrt(100,000) = {sd / math.sqrt(1e5):.4f}")
# A grid with the same number of points: 100,000 points is 100,000 per axis in 1D, but under 4 per axis in 10D.
m = 100_000
grid1 = (np.arange(m) + 0.5) / m
print(f"midpoint rule with {m:,} points: d = 1 error {abs(f(grid1[:, None]).mean() - 1):.1e};"
      f" d = 10 allows {m ** 0.1:.2f} points per axis")
g3 = (np.arange(3) + 0.5) / 3
pts = np.array(np.meshgrid(*[g3] * 10)).reshape(10, -1).T
print(f"midpoint rule in d = 10 with 3 points per axis ({len(pts):,} points): error {abs(f(pts).mean() - 1):.3f}")

# ---- Part 2: importance sampling for a rare event, P(Z > 4) for Z ~ N(0, 1).
true_p = 0.5 * math.erfc(4 / math.sqrt(2))
n = 10_000
naive = [(rng.standard_normal(n) > 4).mean() for _ in range(200)]
def is_est(shift, n):
    y = rng.standard_normal(n) + shift                       # proposal N(shift, 1)
    w = np.exp(-shift * y + shift ** 2 / 2)                  # p(y) / q(y)
    return np.mean((y > 4) * w)
ests = {s: [is_est(s, n) for _ in range(200)] for s in (2.0, 4.0, 6.0)}
print(f"P(Z > 4) = {true_p:.4e}. With n = 10,000: naive estimates are 0 in {np.mean(np.array(naive) == 0):.0%} of 200 runs,"
      f" relative RMS error {np.sqrt(np.mean((np.array(naive) - true_p) ** 2)) / true_p:.2f}")
for s, e in ests.items():
    e = np.array(e)
    print(f"  importance sampling from N({s:.0f}, 1): relative RMS error {np.sqrt(np.mean((e - true_p) ** 2)) / true_p:.4f}")
for s in (2.0, 4.0, 6.0):                                     # effective sample size of the weights f(y) w(y)
    y = rng.standard_normal(n) + s; wy = np.exp(-s * y + s ** 2 / 2) * (y > 4)
    print(f"  N({s:.0f}, 1): fraction of samples in the event {np.mean(y > 4):.3f}, effective sample size {wy.sum() ** 2 / (wy ** 2).sum():,.0f} of {n:,}")

# ---- Part 3: variance reduction. E[e^U], U ~ Uniform(0, 1), = e - 1.
n, runs = 1_000, 2_000
U = rng.random((runs, n))
plain = np.exp(U).mean(1)
anti = (0.5 * (np.exp(U[:, : n // 2]) + np.exp(1 - U[:, : n // 2]))).mean(1)      # n/2 pairs = n evaluations
c = -np.cov(np.exp(U[0]), U[0])[0, 1] / np.var(U[0], ddof=1)                    # coefficient from a pilot run
cv = (np.exp(U) + c * (U - 0.5)).mean(1)
for name, e in [("plain", plain), ("antithetic", anti), ("control variate", cv)]:
    print(f"{name:15s}: standard deviation of the estimate {e.std():.2e} (variance ratio to plain {e.var() / plain.var():.4f})")
print(f"control variate coefficient from the pilot run {c:.4f}; the optimum -Cov(e^U, U) / Var(U) = {-12 * (1 - 0.5 * (math.e - 1)):.4f}")

# ---- Part 4: Metropolis-Hastings on a two-mode target, p(x) proportional to
# exp(-(x-3)^2/2) + exp(-(x+3)^2/2). Random-walk proposals of three widths.
def log_p(x):
    return np.logaddexp(-(x - 3) ** 2 / 2, -(x + 3) ** 2 / 2)

def mh(width, steps, x0=0.0):
    x, lp, out, acc = x0, log_p(x0), np.empty(steps), 0
    z = rng.standard_normal(steps); u = rng.random(steps)
    for t in range(steps):
        y = x + width * z[t]
        lq = log_p(y)
        if math.log(u[t]) < lq - lp:                         # symmetric proposal: accept with min(1, p(y)/p(x))
            x, lp, acc = y, lq, acc + 1
        out[t] = x
    return out, acc / steps

def ess(chain, max_lag=2000):
    c = chain - chain.mean(); var = c.var()
    rho_sum = 0.0
    for k in range(1, max_lag):
        rho = np.dot(c[:-k], c[k:]) / (len(c) * var)
        if rho < 0.05:
            break
        rho_sum += rho
    return len(chain) / (1 + 2 * rho_sum)

chains = {}
for width in (0.1, 2.5, 50.0):
    ch, acc = mh(width, 100_000)
    chains[width] = ch
    print(f"MH, proposal width {width:4}: acceptance {acc:.2f}; fraction of samples with x > 0 {np.mean(ch > 0):.3f} (true 0.5);"
          f" E[x^2] {np.mean(ch ** 2):.2f} (true 10.00); effective sample size {ess(ch):,.0f} of 100,000")

# ---- Part 5: two gradient estimators for d/dmu E[x^2], x ~ N(mu, sigma^2); the true value is 2 mu.
mu, sigma, n = 1.0, 0.5, 100_000
z = rng.standard_normal(n); x = mu + sigma * z
score = x ** 2 * (x - mu) / sigma ** 2                          # score function: f(x) d/dmu log p(x)
score_b = (x ** 2 - (mu ** 2 + sigma ** 2)) * (x - mu) / sigma ** 2   # with the baseline E[f]
reparam = 2 * x                                                # reparameterisation: d/dmu f(mu + sigma z)
for name, g in [("score function", score), ("score function with baseline", score_b), ("reparameterisation", reparam)]:
    print(f"{name:29s}: mean {g.mean():.4f} (true {2 * mu:.1f}), standard deviation of one sample {g.std():.3f}")

# d = 10: RMS error at n = 100 ... 100,000: 0.2670, 0.0830, 0.0256, 0.0083; slope -0.504; sigma_f = 2.669, sigma_f / sqrt(100,000) = 0.0084

Exercises

  1. Show that $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_n) = \sigma_f^2/n$, and use Chebyshev's inequality to show $P(|\hat\mu_n - \mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\sigma_f^2/(n\epsilon^2)$. How many samples guarantee an error below 0.01 with probability 0.95 if $\sigma_f = 1$? How many does the central limit theorem suggest?
  2. Derive the inverse-transform sampler for the Cauchy distribution, $p(x) = 1/(\pi(1 + x^2))$. Why would plain Monte Carlo estimation of its mean fail?
  3. Rejection sampling from $N(0, I_d)$ with proposal $N(0, \sigma^2I_d)$, $\sigma > 1$. Find the smallest $M$ and show that the acceptance rate is $\sigma^{-d}$. What is it for $\sigma = 1.1$ and $d = 100$?
  4. Show that the importance sampling variance is minimised by $q^\star\propto|f|p$. (Hint: by Cauchy–Schwarz, $\mathbb{E}_q[f^2w^2]\ge(\mathbb{E}_q[|f|w])^2$.)
  5. With $p = N(0, 1)$ and $q = N(0, \sigma^2)$, compute $\mathbb{E}_q[w^2]$ and show it is infinite for $\sigma^2\le\tfrac12$. What does this say about choosing a proposal narrower than the target?
  6. Derive the optimal control-variate coefficient and the variance $\frac{\sigma_f^2}n(1 - \rho^2)$. Check the code's 1.7% for $f = e^U$, $g = U$.
  7. Show that the score-function and reparameterisation estimators have the same mean for $f(x) = x^2$, $x\sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, and compute both variances exactly. Which grows as $\sigma\to0$?
Answers

1. $\operatorname{Var}(\frac1n\sum f(x_i)) = \frac1{n^2}\sum\operatorname{Var}f(x_i) = \sigma_f^2/n$ by independence. Chebyshev: $P(|\hat\mu - \mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\operatorname{Var}/\epsilon^2$. Setting $1/(n\cdot10^{-4}) = 0.05$ gives $n = 200{,}000$. The CLT gives $1.96/\sqrt n = 0.01$, $n\approx38{,}400$: Chebyshev is valid for every distribution and therefore pessimistic.

2. $F(x) = \frac12 + \frac1\pi\arctan x$, so $X = \tan(\pi(U - \tfrac12))$. The Cauchy distribution has no mean ($\int|x|p(x)dx = \infty$), and the average of $n$ Cauchy samples is again Cauchy with the same spread: it never settles down.

3. $p/q = \sigma^de^{-\|x\|^2(1 - \sigma^{-2})/2}\le\sigma^d$, with equality at $x = 0$, so $M = \sigma^d$ and the acceptance rate is $1/M = \sigma^{-d}$ (both densities are normalised). For $\sigma = 1.1$, $d = 100$: $1.1^{-100}\approx7.3\times10^{-5}$.

4. $\mathbb{E}_q[f^2w^2]\ge(\mathbb{E}_q[|f|w])^2 = (\int|f|p)^2$, which does not depend on $q$. Equality holds when $|f|w$ is constant $q$-almost everywhere, i.e. $q\propto|f|p$. When $f\ge0$ the bound is $\mu^2$ and the variance is zero.

5. $\mathbb{E}_q[w^2] = \int p^2/q = \int\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2(1 - 1/(2\sigma^2))}dx$, finite only if $1 - 1/(2\sigma^2) > 0$, i.e. $\sigma^2 > \tfrac12$; then it equals $\sigma^2/\sqrt{2\sigma^2 - 1}$. A proposal with lighter tails than the target gives weights that explode in the tails: the estimator is unbiased but its variance is infinite, and it converges erratically. Prefer proposals with heavier tails.

6. Differentiate $\sigma_f^2 + 2c\operatorname{Cov} + c^2\sigma_g^2$ in $c$: $c^\star = -\operatorname{Cov}/\sigma_g^2$, and substituting gives $\sigma_f^2 - \operatorname{Cov}^2/\sigma_g^2 = \sigma_f^2(1 - \rho^2)$. For $f = e^U$: $\operatorname{Var}(e^U) = \frac{e^2 - 1}2 - (e - 1)^2 = 0.2420$, $\operatorname{Cov}(e^U, U) = 1 - \frac{e - 1}2 = 0.1409$, $\operatorname{Var}(U) = \frac1{12}$, so $\rho^2 = 0.1409^2\cdot12/0.2420 = 0.984$ and $1 - \rho^2 = 0.016$.

7. Score: $f\cdot(x - \mu)/\sigma^2$ with $x = \mu + \sigma z$ is $(\mu + \sigma z)^2z/\sigma$, with mean $2\mu$ (the odd moments of $z$ vanish). Reparameterisation: $2x = 2\mu + 2\sigma z$, mean $2\mu$, variance $4\sigma^2$. Score variance: $\mathbb{E}[(\mu + \sigma z)^4z^2]/\sigma^2 - 4\mu^2 = (\mu^4 + 18\mu^2\sigma^2 + 15\sigma^4)/\sigma^2 - 4\mu^2 = \mu^4/\sigma^2 + 14\mu^2 + 15\sigma^2$. At $\mu = 1$, $\sigma = 0.5$: $4 + 14 + 3.75 = 21.75$, standard deviation 4.66 (the code's 4.70); reparameterisation: 1. As $\sigma\to0$ the score function's variance blows up like $\mu^4/\sigma^2$, while the reparameterised one goes to 0.
