Markov chains and bandits

  1. warm-up A two-state chain has $P = \begin{pmatrix}0.9 & 0.1\\0.5 & 0.5\end{pmatrix}$. Find its stationary distribution, the expected number of steps to return to state 2 starting from state 2, and the rate at which $\|\mu_t - \pi\|$ shrinks.

    Solution

    Balance of flow between the two states gives $\pi_1\cdot0.1 = \pi_2\cdot0.5$, so $\pi = (5/6, 1/6)$. By Kac's formula, the expected return time to a state is $1/\pi_i$, here 6 steps. The eigenvalues of $P$ are 1 and $\operatorname{tr}P - 1 = 0.4$, and $\mu_t - \pi = (\mu_0 - \pi)P^t$ lies in the eigenspace of 0.4, so the distance shrinks by a factor 0.4 per step.

  2. core A random walk on a connected undirected graph moves from a vertex to a uniformly chosen neighbour. Show that $\pi(v) = \deg(v)/2|E|$ is stationary by checking detailed balance. When is it the unique stationary distribution, and when does the walk converge to it from any start?

    Solution

    For an edge $\{u, v\}$, $\pi(u)P(u, v) = \frac{\deg u}{2|E|}\cdot\frac{1}{\deg u} = \frac1{2|E|}$, which is symmetric in $u$ and $v$; for non-adjacent pairs both sides are 0. Detailed balance implies stationarity (sum over $u$). The degrees sum to $2|E|$, so $\pi$ is normalised. A connected graph gives an irreducible chain, so $\pi$ is the unique stationary distribution. Convergence from any start also needs aperiodicity: the walk is periodic exactly when the graph is bipartite (every return takes an even number of steps). Adding a self-loop, or making the walk lazy, fixes it.

  3. hard Metropolis–Hastings proposes $y$ from $q(y\mid x)$ and accepts with probability $\alpha(x, y) = \min\big(1, \frac{\pi(y)q(x\mid y)}{\pi(x)q(y\mid x)}\big)$. Show that the resulting chain satisfies detailed balance with respect to $\pi$, and explain why only ratios of $\pi$ are needed.

    Solution

    For $y\ne x$, the transition probability is $P(x, y) = q(y\mid x)\alpha(x, y)$. Then $\pi(x)P(x, y) = \min\big(\pi(x)q(y\mid x), \pi(y)q(x\mid y)\big)$, which is symmetric in $x$ and $y$, so $\pi(x)P(x, y) = \pi(y)P(y, x)$. The rejected mass stays at $x$ and does not affect balance between distinct states. The acceptance ratio involves $\pi(y)/\pi(x)$, so a normalising constant cancels: one can sample from a posterior known only up to its evidence.

  4. warm-up Show that $\varepsilon$-greedy with a fixed $\varepsilon > 0$ has regret that grows linearly in $T$. What schedule of $\varepsilon_t$ fixes this, in principle?

    Solution

    At every step, with probability $\varepsilon$, the arm is chosen uniformly, which costs on average $\bar\Delta = \frac1K\sum_a\Delta_a > 0$. So the expected regret is at least $\varepsilon\bar\Delta T$. A decaying schedule $\varepsilon_t = \min(1, cK/(d^2t))$, with $d$ a lower bound on the smallest gap, gives logarithmic regret (Auer et al., 2002); the catch is that it needs to know $d$, which UCB does not.

  5. core Two Bernoulli arms have means 0.5 and 0.6. Compute the Lai–Robbins constant $\Delta/D_\text{KL}(0.5\,\|\,0.6)$ that multiplies $\ln T$ in the lower bound, and compare it with the constant in lesson 32's UCB1 bound, $8/\Delta$.

    Solution

    $D_\text{KL}(0.5\,\|\,0.6) = 0.5\ln\frac{0.5}{0.6} + 0.5\ln\frac{0.5}{0.4} = 0.5(-0.1823 + 0.2231) = 0.0204$ nats. The constant is $0.1/0.0204 = 4.90$. UCB1's bound has $8/0.1 = 80$, sixteen times larger. The bound's constant is loose, and UCB1's Hoeffding bonus ignores that the variance of a Bernoulli near 0.5 is $\tfrac14$; KL-UCB and Thompson sampling attain the Lai–Robbins constant.

  6. code Run UCB1 and Thompson sampling (Beta(1, 1) priors) on the two arms of the previous problem for $T = 10{,}000$ steps, 200 runs each, and compare the mean regret with $4.90\ln T$. Does Thompson sampling beat the lower bound? Explain.

    Solution

    The reference solution gets a mean regret of 87.1 (standard error 1.4) for UCB1 and 18.7 (1.1) for Thompson sampling; $4.90\ln10^4 = 45.1$. Thompson sampling is below it, which is no contradiction: Lai and Robbins bound $\liminf_{T\to\infty}\text{regret}/\ln T$, a statement about the limit. At finite $T$ lower-order terms matter, and the bound's $\ln T$ growth only dominates eventually. UCB1, with its larger constant, is above the bound at every $T$.

MDPs and dynamic programming

  1. warm-up An agent receives reward 1 at every step forever. What is its return with $\gamma = 0.9$, and with $\gamma = 0.99$? After how many steps has it collected half of the return?

    Solution

    $\sum_t\gamma^t = 1/(1 - \gamma)$: 10 and 100. The first $k$ steps collect $1 - \gamma^k$ of it, so half needs $\gamma^k = \tfrac12$, $k = \ln2/\ln(1/\gamma)$: 6.6 steps for $\gamma = 0.9$ and 69 for $0.99$. The "effective horizon" $1/(1 - \gamma)$ is the right order of magnitude.

  2. core A two-state MDP with $\gamma = 0.9$. In state A, "stay" pays 1 and stays in A; "go" pays 0 and moves to B. In state B, the only action pays 2 and stays in B. Solve the Bellman optimality equations by hand and give $Q^\star$.

    Solution

    $V^\star(B) = 2 + 0.9V^\star(B)$, so $V^\star(B) = 20$. In A, staying forever is worth $1/(1 - 0.9) = 10$, going is worth $0 + 0.9\cdot20 = 18$. Check the optimality equation: $V^\star(A) = \max(1 + 0.9V^\star(A), 18)$; with $V^\star(A) = 18$, $\max(17.2, 18) = 18$, consistent. So $Q^\star(A, \text{stay}) = 17.2$, $Q^\star(A, \text{go}) = 18$, $Q^\star(B) = 20$. The immediate reward prefers "stay", the value prefers "go": one step of sacrifice buys a better stream.

  3. core Show that $V^\pi = (I - \gamma P_\pi)^{-1}\mathbf{r}_\pi$, that the matrix $(I - \gamma P_\pi)^{-1}$ has nonnegative entries, and that each of its rows sums to $1/(1 - \gamma)$. What does row $s$ of $(1 - \gamma)(I - \gamma P_\pi)^{-1}$ represent?

    Solution

    The Bellman expectation equation is $V = \mathbf{r}_\pi + \gamma P_\pi V$, and $I - \gamma P_\pi$ is invertible because $\|\gamma P_\pi\|_\infty = \gamma < 1$. The Neumann series $(I - \gamma P_\pi)^{-1} = \sum_t\gamma^tP_\pi^t$ converges and every term is nonnegative. $P_\pi^t\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$, so the rows sum to $\sum_t\gamma^t = 1/(1 - \gamma)$. Row $s$ of $(1 - \gamma)\sum_t\gamma^tP_\pi^t$ is the normalised discounted state-visitation distribution from $s$, the $\eta$ of lessons 38 and 39.

  4. hard Potential-based reward shaping adds $F(s, s') = \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$ to every reward, for any function $\Phi$ (with $\Phi = 0$ at terminal states). Show that $Q'^\pi(s, a) = Q^\pi(s, a) - \Phi(s)$ for every policy, and conclude that shaping does not change the optimal policy.

    Solution

    The shaped return from $s_0$ is $\sum_t\gamma^t(r_t + \gamma\Phi(s_{t+1}) - \Phi(s_t)) = G - \Phi(s_0)$, because the shaping terms telescope: $\sum_t(\gamma^{t+1}\Phi(s_{t+1}) - \gamma^t\Phi(s_t)) = -\Phi(s_0) + \lim\gamma^T\Phi(s_T) = -\Phi(s_0)$, for bounded $\Phi$ or a terminal state. Taking expectations given $s_0 = s$, $a_0 = a$ gives $Q'^\pi = Q^\pi - \Phi(s)$. Subtracting a function of $s$ alone does not change $\arg\max_aQ$, so the greedy and optimal policies are the same. (Ng, Harada and Russell, 1999, also show potential-based shaping is the only kind with this guarantee.) A good $\Phi$, close to $V^\star$, makes the shaped rewards nearly zero along optimal paths and gives early, informative feedback.

  5. core Rewards lie in $[0, 1]$, $\gamma = 0.99$ and value iteration starts from $V_0 = 0$. Use the contraction property to bound the number of iterations needed for $\|V_k - V^\star\|_\infty\le0.01$.

    Solution

    $\|V_k - V^\star\|\le\gamma^k\|V_0 - V^\star\|\le\gamma^k/(1 - \gamma) = 100\gamma^k$. Requiring $100\cdot0.99^k\le0.01$ gives $k\ge\ln(10^{-4})/\ln0.99 = 916.4$, so 917 iterations suffice. The bound is worst case; episodic tasks with terminal states converge much faster (the next problem).

  6. code Run value iteration on lesson 33's 4×3 gridworld with living reward $-0.04$, for $\gamma = 0.9$ and $\gamma = 0.99$, until successive iterates differ by less than $10^{-10}$. How many sweeps does it take, what is $V^\star$ at the start, and does the optimal policy change between the two discount factors?

    Solution

    The reference solution takes 33 sweeps for $\gamma = 0.9$, with $V^\star(\text{start}) = 0.2965$, and 39 sweeps for $\gamma = 0.99$, with $0.6507$ (lesson 33's value). Far fewer than problem 11's bound, because every path ends at a terminal state within a few steps and the error stops propagating. The policies differ in one cell: in the bottom row, second from the left, the optimal action is "right" with $\gamma = 0.9$ and "left" with $\gamma = 0.99$. With heavier discounting the long safe route around the left is not worth its length, and the agent takes the shorter route past the −1 square.

  7. hard Show that if $\|V_{k+1} - V_k\|_\infty\le\epsilon$ then $\|V_{k+1} - V^\star\|_\infty\le\frac{\gamma\epsilon}{1 - \gamma}$. Why is this a useful stopping rule, while the bound of problem 11 is not?

    Solution

    Write $T$ for the Bellman optimality operator. $\|V_{k+1} - V^\star\| = \|TV_k - TV^\star\|\le\gamma\|V_k - V^\star\|\le\gamma(\|V_k - V_{k+1}\| + \|V_{k+1} - V^\star\|)$. Rearranging, $(1 - \gamma)\|V_{k+1} - V^\star\|\le\gamma\epsilon$. It uses only quantities the algorithm can compute, while problem 11's bound needs $\|V_0 - V^\star\|$, which is unknown (we used a crude upper bound). Combined with the fact that a greedy policy with respect to $V$ loses at most $\frac{2\gamma}{1 - \gamma}\|V - V^\star\|$, it gives a certified stopping rule.

  8. core Prove that policy iteration terminates after finitely many iterations in a finite MDP, and that it terminates at an optimal policy.

    Solution

    By the policy improvement theorem, each greedy step gives $V^{\pi_{k+1}}\ge V^{\pi_k}$ everywhere, with strict inequality somewhere unless $\pi_k$ is already greedy with respect to its own values. So no policy repeats, and there are only $|\mathcal{A}|^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ deterministic policies, so the sequence stops. It stops when $\pi_k$ is greedy with respect to $V^{\pi_k}$, which means $V^{\pi_k}$ satisfies the Bellman optimality equation, whose unique solution is $V^\star$ (the operator is a contraction).

Learning from experience

  1. warm-up With $V(A) = 0.5$, $V(B) = 0.2$, $\alpha = 0.1$ and $\gamma = 1$, the agent sees $A\to B$ with reward 0, then $B\to$ terminal with reward 1. Give the TD(0) updates in order, and the every-visit Monte Carlo updates.

    Solution

    TD(0): $V(A)\leftarrow0.5 + 0.1(0 + 0.2 - 0.5) = 0.47$; then $V(B)\leftarrow0.2 + 0.1(1 - 0.2) = 0.28$. Monte Carlo: both returns are 1, so $V(A)\leftarrow0.5 + 0.1(1 - 0.5) = 0.55$ and $V(B)\leftarrow0.28$. TD moved $V(A)$ down, towards the current (too low) estimate of $B$; Monte Carlo moved it up, towards the actual outcome. TD will catch up once $V(B)$ has learned.

  2. core Eight episodes: one is $A, 0, B, 0$; six are $B, 1$; one is $B, 0$ (Sutton and Barto, example 6.4). Compute the batch Monte Carlo and batch TD(0) estimates of $V(A)$ and $V(B)$, and say which minimises what.

    Solution

    $V(B) = 6/8 = 0.75$ for both. Batch MC averages the returns observed from A: one episode with return 0, so $V(A) = 0$, which minimises the squared error on the training returns. Batch TD converges to the value under the maximum-likelihood model: A always goes to B with reward 0, so $V(A) = V(B) = 0.75$. The certainty-equivalence estimate is the one that will generalise better if the process really is Markov.

  3. core Show that the weights $(1 - \lambda)\lambda^{n-1}$ of the $n$-step returns in the $\lambda$-return sum to 1 in a continuing task, and show how the weights are adjusted in an episode that terminates after $T - t$ steps. What are the $\lambda$-returns for $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda = 1$?

    Solution

    $\sum_{n\ge1}(1 - \lambda)\lambda^{n-1} = (1 - \lambda)/(1 - \lambda) = 1$. In an episode, every $n$-step return with $n\ge T - t$ equals the full return $G_t$, so their weights are collected: $G^\lambda_t = (1 - \lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{T-t-1}\lambda^{n-1}G^{(n)}_t + \lambda^{T-t-1}G_t$. For $\lambda = 0$ only $G^{(1)}_t$ remains: TD(0). For $\lambda = 1$ only $G_t$ remains: Monte Carlo.

  4. hard Prove the error-reduction property of $n$-step returns: for any $V$, $\max_s\big|\mathbb{E}_\pi[G^{(n)}_t\mid S_t = s] - v_\pi(s)\big|\le\gamma^n\max_s|V(s) - v_\pi(s)|$, where $G^{(n)}_t$ bootstraps from $V$.

    Solution

    $G^{(n)}_t = R_{t+1} + \dots + \gamma^{n-1}R_{t+n} + \gamma^nV(S_{t+n})$, and $v_\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[R_{t+1} + \dots + \gamma^{n-1}R_{t+n} + \gamma^nv_\pi(S_{t+n})\mid S_t = s]$, by unrolling the Bellman equation $n$ times. Subtracting, the rewards cancel and $\mathbb{E}[G^{(n)}_t\mid s] - v_\pi(s) = \gamma^n\mathbb{E}[V(S_{t+n}) - v_\pi(S_{t+n})\mid s]$, whose absolute value is at most $\gamma^n\max|V - v_\pi|$. (If the episode ends before $t + n$, the bootstrap term is absent and the error is even smaller.) This is the contraction that makes $n$-step TD converge, and it shows the trade-off: larger $n$ means less bias from $V$ but more variance from the rewards.

  5. warm-up With $\varepsilon = 0.1$ and four actions, compute the Expected SARSA target at a state with $Q = (1, 3, 2, 3)$ for reward $-1$ and $\gamma = 1$, breaking the greedy tie by splitting the greedy probability equally. Compare with the Q-learning target.

    Solution

    Each action gets $\varepsilon/4 = 0.025$; the two greedy actions share $0.9$, so they get $0.475$ each. The expectation is $0.025\cdot1 + 0.475\cdot3 + 0.025\cdot2 + 0.475\cdot3 = 2.925$, and the target is $1.925$. Q-learning's target is $-1 + 3 = 2$. Expected SARSA accounts for the 5% chance of exploring into worse actions.

  6. core Let $X_1, X_2$ be independent $N(0, 1)$ estimates of two actions whose true values are both 0. Show that $\mathbb{E}[\max(X_1, X_2)] = 1/\sqrt\pi$, and explain how double Q-learning removes the bias.

    Solution

    $\max(X_1, X_2) = \frac{X_1 + X_2}2 + \frac{|X_1 - X_2|}2$. The first term has mean 0. $X_1 - X_2\sim N(0, 2)$, and $\mathbb{E}|Z| = \sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}$ for $Z\sim N(0, \sigma^2)$, so $\mathbb{E}|X_1 - X_2| = \sqrt2\sqrt{2/\pi} = 2/\sqrt\pi$, and the expectation is $1/\sqrt\pi\approx0.564$, though $\max$ of the true values is 0. Double Q-learning picks the action with one estimate and evaluates it with an independent one: $\mathbb{E}[Y_{\arg\max X}] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y_a]] = 0$ when $Y$ is independent of $X$. It can underestimate when the true values differ, but not overestimate by this mechanism.

Function approximation and policy gradients

  1. core Two states have one feature each, $x(1) = 1$ and $x(2) = 2$, so $\hat v(s) = wx(s)$, and every reward is 0. Off-policy updates are made only on the transition $1\to2$. Show that semi-gradient TD multiplies $w$ by $1 + \alpha(2\gamma - 1)$ per update, and so diverges for $\gamma > \tfrac12$ unless $w = 0$. Which of the three ingredients of the deadly triad are present?

    Solution

    The update is $w\leftarrow w + \alpha(0 + \gamma\cdot2w - w)\cdot x(1) = w(1 + \alpha(2\gamma - 1))$. For $\gamma > \tfrac12$ the factor exceeds 1 and $|w|$ grows geometrically, although the true values are 0 and $w = 0$ represents them exactly. All three are present: function approximation (one weight shared by both states), bootstrapping (the target uses $\hat v(2)$), and off-policy updates (state 2 is never updated, so nothing corrects the increase in $\hat v(2)$). On-policy, the transitions out of state 2 would also be updated and pull $w$ back.

  2. core DQN uses the Huber loss $\ell(\delta) = \tfrac12\delta^2$ for $|\delta|\le1$ and $|\delta| - \tfrac12$ otherwise. Give its gradient, and explain why it is equivalent to clipping the TD error in the update, and why that helps early in training.

    Solution

    $\ell'(\delta) = \delta$ for $|\delta|\le1$ and $\operatorname{sign}(\delta)$ otherwise, which is $\operatorname{clip}(\delta, -1, 1)$. So the gradient step is the squared-error step with the TD error clipped. Early on, targets bootstrap from an untrained network and some TD errors are huge; with the squared loss these dominate the update and can blow up the weights. Clipping bounds each sample's influence, like a robust regression loss.

  3. warm-up For a softmax policy $\pi(a) = e^{\theta_a}/\sum_be^{\theta_b}$, show that $\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a) = \mathbf{e}_a - \boldsymbol\pi$ and that its expectation under $\pi$ is zero.

    Solution

    $\log\pi(a) = \theta_a - \log\sum_be^{\theta_b}$, and $\partial/\partial\theta_c$ gives $[a = c] - \pi(c)$. Its expectation is $\sum_a\pi(a)(\mathbf{e}_a - \boldsymbol\pi) = \boldsymbol\pi - \boldsymbol\pi = 0$, the special case of $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi] = \nabla\sum_a\pi(a) = 0$.

  4. core For a one-dimensional parameter, the REINFORCE estimate with a constant baseline is $g = s(R - b)$, with $s = \partial_\theta\log\pi(A)$. Show that $\mathbb{E}[g]$ does not depend on $b$, and that the variance is minimised by $b^\star = \mathbb{E}[s^2R]/\mathbb{E}[s^2]$. When is $b^\star$ simply $\mathbb{E}[R]$?

    Solution

    $\mathbb{E}[sb] = b\,\mathbb{E}[s] = 0$. $\operatorname{Var}(g) = \mathbb{E}[s^2(R - b)^2] - (\mathbb{E}[g])^2$, and the second term does not depend on $b$; the first is a quadratic in $b$ with derivative $-2\mathbb{E}[s^2(R - b)]$, zero at $b^\star$. It equals $\mathbb{E}[R]$ when $s^2$ and $R$ are uncorrelated. In practice a learned $V(s)$ is used: not the minimum-variance baseline, but close, and it adapts to the state.

  5. core A Gaussian policy $a\sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with fixed $\sigma$. Show that $\partial_\mu\log\pi(a) = (a - \mu)/\sigma^2$. For a reward $R(a) = -a^2$, compute the exact gradient $\partial_\mu\mathbb{E}[R]$ and check that REINFORCE is unbiased for it. What happens to the variance of the estimate as $\sigma\to0$?

    Solution

    $\log\pi = -(a - \mu)^2/2\sigma^2 + \text{const}$, so the derivative is $(a - \mu)/\sigma^2$. $\mathbb{E}[R] = -(\mu^2 + \sigma^2)$, so the gradient is $-2\mu$. With $a = \mu + \sigma z$: $\mathbb{E}[-(\mu + \sigma z)^2z/\sigma] = -\mathbb{E}[(\mu^2z + 2\mu\sigma z^2 + \sigma^2z^3)/\sigma] = -2\mu$. Unbiased. But the term $\mu^2z/\sigma$ has variance $\mu^4/\sigma^2$, which blows up as $\sigma\to0$: the score-function estimator needs exploration, and a baseline (here $b = -\mu^2$) removes the worst of it.

  6. hard Show that the GAE advantage $\hat A_t = \sum_{l\ge0}(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l}$, with $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$, equals $G^\lambda_t - V(s_t)$, the $\lambda$-return minus the baseline, when the $\lambda$-return bootstraps from the same $V$. Which values of $\lambda$ give the one-step TD advantage and the Monte Carlo advantage?

    Solution

    The $n$-step advantage telescopes: $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\gamma^l\delta_{t+l} = \sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\gamma^lr_{t+l} + \gamma^nV(s_{t+n}) - V(s_t) = G^{(n)}_t - V(s_t)$. The $\lambda$-return weights these by $(1 - \lambda)\lambda^{n-1}$: $G^\lambda_t - V(s_t) = (1 - \lambda)\sum_n\lambda^{n-1}\sum_{l<n}\gamma^l\delta_{t+l}$. Exchanging the sums, $\delta_{t+l}$ appears for every $n > l$, with total weight $\gamma^l(1 - \lambda)\sum_{n>l}\lambda^{n-1} = \gamma^l\lambda^l$. So the two agree. $\lambda = 0$ gives $\delta_t$, the one-step TD advantage; $\lambda = 1$ gives $G_t - V(s_t)$, the Monte Carlo advantage.

  7. core For a Bernoulli policy with probability $p$, show that $D_\text{KL}(p\,\|\,p + d) = \frac{d^2}{2p(1 - p)} + O(d^3)$. If $p = \sigma(\theta)$, what is the Fisher information in $\theta$, and what does the natural gradient do near $p = 0$ or 1?

    Solution

    Expand $D = p\ln\frac{p}{p + d} + (1 - p)\ln\frac{1 - p}{1 - p - d}$ to second order: the first-order terms are $-d + d = 0$, and the second-order terms are $\frac{d^2}{2p} + \frac{d^2}{2(1 - p)} = \frac{d^2}{2p(1 - p)}$. So the Fisher information in $p$ is $1/(p(1 - p))$. With $dp/d\theta = p(1 - p)$, the Fisher information in $\theta$ is $p(1 - p)$. The natural gradient divides the ordinary gradient by $p(1 - p)$, so near $p = 0$ or 1, where the logistic is flat and the ordinary gradient vanishes, it takes large steps in $\theta$, which correspond to reasonable steps in the policy.

  8. warm-up With $\varepsilon = 0.2$, compute PPO's per-sample objective $\min(r\hat A, \operatorname{clip}(r, 0.8, 1.2)\hat A)$ and say whether its gradient is zero, for $(r, \hat A) = (1.5, 2), (1.5, -2), (0.5, 2), (0.5, -2)$.

    Solution

    $(1.5, 2)$: $\min(3, 2.4) = 2.4$, clipped, gradient zero: the action is already more likely than the clip allows. $(1.5, -2)$: $\min(-3, -2.4) = -3$, unclipped, gradient nonzero: the min keeps the pessimistic term, so a bad action that became too likely is still pushed down. $(0.5, 2)$: $\min(1, 1.6) = 1$, unclipped, gradient nonzero: a good action that became unlikely is pushed back up. $(0.5, -2)$: $\min(-1, -1.6) = -1.6$, clipped, gradient zero.

Planning and search

  1. core A node has been visited 100 times. Child 1 has $N = 70$, mean reward $0.6$ and prior $0.3$; child 2 has $N = 30$, mean $0.5$ and prior $0.7$. Which child does UCT with $c = \sqrt2$ choose, and which does PUCT with $c_\text{puct} = 1.5$ choose?

    Solution

    UCT: $\ln100 = 4.605$. Child 1: $0.6 + \sqrt{2\cdot4.605/70} = 0.6 + 0.363 = 0.963$. Child 2: $0.5 + \sqrt{2\cdot4.605/30} = 0.5 + 0.554 = 1.054$. UCT chooses child 2. PUCT: $1.5\sqrt{100} = 15$. Child 1: $0.6 + 15\cdot0.3/71 = 0.663$. Child 2: $0.5 + 15\cdot0.7/31 = 0.839$. PUCT also chooses child 2, mainly because of its prior.

  2. hard Compute the regularised policy $\bar\pi(a) = \lambda p(a)/(\alpha - q_a)$ of lesson 41 for $\mathbf{p} = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$, $\mathbf{q} = (0.1, 0.5, 0.0)$ and $\lambda = 0.2$, by solving for $\alpha$ numerically. How does $\bar\pi$ change for $\lambda = 0.05$ and $\lambda = 10$?

    Solution

    $\alpha$ must exceed 0.5 and solve $\frac{0.1}{\alpha - 0.1} + \frac{0.06}{\alpha - 0.5} + \frac{0.04}{\alpha} = 1$; bisection gives $\alpha = 0.5828$ and $\bar\pi = (0.207, 0.724, 0.069)$. The search has moved most of the probability to the action with the highest value, against the prior's preference for the first. With $\lambda = 0.05$, $\bar\pi = (0.060, 0.921, 0.019)$, nearly greedy; with $\lambda = 10$, $(0.495, 0.309, 0.196)$, nearly the prior. Since $\lambda_N$ falls as $1/\sqrt N$, more simulations move the search policy from the prior towards the greedy one.
