### 0.1.1 🟢 Reading the odds board
Convert to probabilities (assume each bet is fair): **(a)** 9 to 1 against, **(b)** evens (1 to 1),
**(c)** 1 to 4 against (bookies call this "4 to 1 on").

### 0.1.2 🟢 Writing the odds board
Convert to odds against: **(a)** $p = 0.25$, **(b)** $p = 0.9$, **(c)** $p = 1/3$.

### 0.1.3 🟢 Which meaning?
Which interpretation (classical, frequentist, Bayesian, propensity) fits each statement most naturally?
1. "The chance of drawing an ace from a well-shuffled deck is 1/13."
2. "About 51% of babies born are boys."
3. "I'm 90% sure I left my keys in the car."
4. "This uranium-238 atom has a 50% chance of decaying in the next 4.5 billion years."
5. "The defendant is probably guilty."

### 0.1.4 🟢 Traffic light log
Over 50 mornings, a light was red 22 times, amber 3 times, and green 25 times. Find the relative
frequency of **(a)** red, **(b)** not green, **(c)** red or amber. Check that (b) and (c) agree, and say why they must.

### 0.1.5 🟡 Leibniz's mistake
Leibniz, one of the inventors of calculus, argued that with two dice a sum of 11 and a sum of 12 are equally likely,
"since each can be made in only one way". Find the correct probabilities. What did he miss?

### 0.1.6 🟡 The simulation budget
Using the rule "error ≈ $1/\sqrt N$" (95% of the time), how many simulated trials do you need to estimate a probability
to within **(a)** ±0.005, **(b)** ±0.001? **(c)** If you know the true probability is near 0.01, use the sharper error
$2\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}$ to redo (b). Why is the answer so different?

### 0.1.7 🟡 Due for a tail?
A fair coin has come up heads 14 times in the first 20 flips. Over all 100 flips (80 to go), what is the
expected number of heads? The expected *fraction*? Is the fraction "pulled back" to exactly 0.5?

### 0.1.8 🟡 Build a Dutch book
Two friends price tickets that pay \$1 if a given horse wins a three-horse race.
- Ana's prices: A = 0.50, B = 0.30, C = 0.30.
- Ben's prices: A = 0.40, B = 0.30, C = 0.20.

For each friend, find trades *at their own prices* that guarantee you a profit whoever wins. How much do you make?

### 0.1.9 🟡 Whose frequency?
In a clinic's records, 20 of 1000 adults had condition X, 10 of 200 smokers did, and 4 of 40 smokers over 50 did.
A new patient is a 55-year-old smoker. Give three defensible "probabilities" that they have X. Which one would you
use, and what is the danger in going too narrow?

### 0.1.10 🔴 Swamped, not cancelled
After $n$ fair flips you have $n/2 + E$ heads (an excess of $E$). You flip $m$ more times.
**(a)** Show the expected number of heads is $(n+m)/2 + E$, so the expected excess stays $E$.
**(b)** Show the expected fraction of heads tends to $1/2$ as $m \to \infty$.
**(c)** If $E = 10$, roughly how many total flips before the expected fraction is within 0.001 of ½?

### 0.1.11 🟣 Coherence forces the rules
Your price for a ticket paying \$1 if $A$ happens is $q(A)$. You will buy *or* sell any such ticket at your price.
Prove that unless **(a)** $0 \le q(A) \le 1$ and **(b)** $q(A) + q(A^c) = 1$, someone can make a Dutch book against you.

### 0.1.12 🟣 Frequencies obey inclusion–exclusion
Using only $f_n(E) = n_E/n$, prove that for any events $A, B$:
**(a)** $f_n(A^c) = 1 - f_n(A)$, **(b)** $f_n(A \text{ or } B) = f_n(A) + f_n(B) - f_n(A \text{ and } B)$.

### 0.1.13 💻 Check the √N rule
Write a simulation that estimates $P(\text{two dice sum to } 7)$ with $N = 1000$ trials. Repeat it 200 times and
measure the standard deviation of the 200 estimates. Compare with $\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}$ for $p = 1/6$. Then try $N = 4000$:
does the spread halve?

## Solutions

<details><summary>0.1.1</summary>

Odds $a$ to $b$ against gives $p = b/(a+b)$.
**(a)** $1/10 = 0.1$. **(b)** $1/2$. **(c)** $4/5 = 0.8$.

Sanity check for (c): with "odds on", you stake \$4 to win \$1. You'd only do that for a likely outcome.
</details>

<details><summary>0.1.2</summary>

Odds against are $(1-p) : p$, scaled to nice numbers.
**(a)** $0.75 : 0.25 = 3$ to 1 against. **(b)** $0.1 : 0.9 = 1$ to 9 against (9 to 1 on).
**(c)** $2/3 : 1/3 = 2$ to 1 against.
</details>

<details><summary>0.1.3</summary>

1. **Classical**: 52 symmetric cards, 4 of them aces.
2. **Frequentist**: a long-run frequency over millions of births.
3. **Bayesian**: a personal degree of belief about one fact that is already settled.
4. **Propensity**, or frequentist over many atoms. This is the half-life, a physical tendency of each atom.
5. **Bayesian**: a single unrepeatable case judged on evidence.

These overlap. A frequentist would say (1) is also a long-run frequency of shuffles, for example.
The point is which reading is *most natural*, not which is "right".
</details>

<details><summary>0.1.4</summary>

**(a)** $22/50 = 0.44$. **(b)** $1 - 25/50 = 0.50$. **(c)** $(22+3)/50 = 0.50$.

They agree because "not green" and "red or amber" are the **same event**: there are only three colours.
That's rule 2 (total is 1) combined with rule 3 (disjoint events add).
</details>

<details><summary>0.1.5</summary>

Label the dice so they're distinguishable (one red, one blue). There are $6 \times 6 = 36$ equally likely ordered pairs.
- Sum 12: only (6,6). $P = 1/36$.
- Sum 11: (5,6) **and** (6,5). $P = 2/36$.

**What he missed:** a 5 and a 6 can appear in two ways, red-5/blue-6 or red-6/blue-5. Unordered outcomes like
"a 5 and a 6" are **not** equally likely, while ordered ones are. Physical dice don't know they look alike.
This "label everything" trick is the main idea of Chapter 1.3.
</details>

<details><summary>0.1.6</summary>

**(a)** $1/\sqrt N = 0.005 \Rightarrow N = 40{,}000$. **(b)** $N = 1{,}000{,}000$.

**(c)** $2\sqrt{0.01 \cdot 0.99 / N} = 0.001 \Rightarrow N = 4 \cdot 0.0099/10^{-6} = 39{,}600$.

The $1/\sqrt N$ rule assumes the worst case, $p = 1/2$, where $p(1-p) = 1/4$ is largest. For rare events the
*absolute* error is smaller. But careful: ±0.001 on a probability of 0.01 is still a 10% *relative* error.
Estimating rare events precisely, relative to their size, is hard. That's a theme of Part 6.
</details>

<details><summary>0.1.7</summary>

The 80 future flips are fair, so expect 40 heads from them. Total expected heads: $14 + 40 = 54$. Expected fraction: $0.54$.

The fraction moved from $0.70$ toward $0.5$, but **not** because tails became more likely. The first 20 flips just
count for less once they're mixed with 80 fair ones. The coin doesn't owe you anything.

**Tempting wrong answer: 50.** That assumes the remaining flips will "make up" the excess heads, which is the gambler's fallacy.
</details>

<details><summary>0.1.8</summary>

**Ana** (prices sum to $1.10 > 1$): **sell** her all three tickets for \$1.10. Exactly one horse wins, so you pay
out exactly \$1. Profit: **\$0.10**, guaranteed.

**Ben** (prices sum to $0.90 < 1$): **buy** all three tickets from him for \$0.90. Exactly one pays you \$1.
Profit: **\$0.10**, guaranteed.

The general lesson: coherent prices over a set of mutually exclusive, exhaustive outcomes **must sum to 1**.
(Real bookmakers' prices sum to more than 1 on purpose. That margin, the "overround", is their profit.)
</details>

<details><summary>0.1.9</summary>

Three reference classes give $20/1000 = 0.02$, $10/200 = 0.05$, and $4/40 = 0.10$.

The narrowest class (smokers over 50) is the **most relevant** because it shares the most features with the patient.
But it rests on only 40 people, so 4 out of 40 is a **noisy** estimate: the error is about $\sqrt{0.1 \cdot 0.9/40} \approx 0.05$.
Narrower is more relevant but less reliable. Statistics (Part 9) and Bayesian "shrinkage" are ways of balancing the two.
There is no unique "true frequency" for one person. That's the reference class problem.
</details>

<details><summary>0.1.10</summary>

**(a)** Each new flip adds ½ head in expectation, so $m$ flips add $m/2$. Total: $n/2 + E + m/2 = (n+m)/2 + E$.
The excess over the expected half of all $n+m$ flips is still $E$.

**(b)** The expected fraction is $\dfrac{(n+m)/2 + E}{n+m} = \dfrac12 + \dfrac{E}{n+m} \to \dfrac12$ as $m \to \infty$.

**(c)** We need $E/(n+m) \le 0.001$, so $n + m \ge 10/0.001 = 10{,}000$ flips.

*(Strictly, (b) is a statement about expectations. The full LLN says the actual fraction converges too: Part 6.)*
</details>

<details><summary>0.1.11</summary>

A ticket on $A$ pays \$1 if $A$ happens and \$0 otherwise, and you trade at price $q(A)$.

**(a)** If $q(A) > 1$: I **sell** you the ticket for $q(A)$. The most I ever pay out is \$1, so I gain at least $q(A) - 1 > 0$.
If $q(A) < 0$: you'd "sell" me the ticket for a negative price, which means you pay me $|q(A)|$ to take it. I gain at least $|q(A)| > 0$,
since the ticket can only add to that.

**(b)** Let $s = q(A) + q(A^c)$. Exactly one of $A$, $A^c$ happens, so the pair of tickets always pays exactly \$1.
- If $s > 1$: I sell you both for $s$ and pay out \$1. I gain $s - 1 > 0$ whatever happens.
- If $s < 1$: I buy both from you for $s$ and receive \$1. I gain $1 - s > 0$.

So avoiding a sure loss forces $s = 1$. ∎

The same trick on disjoint $A$ and $B$ (tickets on $A$, on $B$, and on "$A$ or $B$") forces $q(A \text{ or } B) = q(A) + q(B)$,
which is the third rule.
</details>

<details><summary>0.1.12</summary>

**(a)** Each of the $n$ trials lands in exactly one of $A$, $A^c$, so $n_A + n_{A^c} = n$. Divide by $n$.

**(b)** Count trials. A trial in "$A$ or $B$" is counted once in $n_A + n_B$ if it's in exactly one of them,
and **twice** if it's in both. So $n_A + n_B = n_{A \text{ or } B} + n_{A \text{ and } B}$. Rearrange and divide by $n$. ∎

Since frequencies satisfy this, and probabilities are what frequencies tend to, we'd expect probabilities to satisfy it too.
Chapter 1.2 proves it from the axioms.
</details>

<details><summary>0.1.13</summary>

```python
import random, statistics

def estimate(N):
    return sum(random.randint(1, 6) + random.randint(1, 6) == 7 for _ in range(N)) / N

for N in (1000, 4000):
    ests = [estimate(N) for _ in range(200)]
    print(N, round(statistics.stdev(ests), 4), round(((1/6) * (5/6) / N) ** 0.5, 4))
```

Theory: for $N = 1000$, $\sqrt{(1/6)(5/6)/1000} \approx 0.0118$; for $N = 4000$, about $0.0059$. The measured spreads
land close to these (±10% or so, since 200 repeats is itself a sample). Four times the trials gives **half** the spread: the $1/\sqrt N$ law.
</details>
