### 0.2.1 🟢 First to two
First to 2 wins, the score is 1–0 to A, rounds are fair, and the pot is 100 coins. Find each player's fair share.

### 0.2.2 🟢 Galileo's dice, by hand
List all ordered triples $(x, y, z)$ of dice faces with sum 9 that start with $x = 1$, then those that start with $x = 2$.
Use the pattern to confirm there are 25 in total. (Or count all 25 from the table in the chapter.)

### 0.2.3 🟢 Fix d'Alembert
Two fair coins are tossed. Find $P(\text{at least one head})$ by listing equally likely outcomes. Then explain in one sentence
why d'Alembert's three "cases" give the wrong answer.

### 0.2.4 🟡 A bigger lead
First to 4, score 3–1, fair rounds, pot 100. Find A's fair share using **both** Fermat's and Pascal's methods.

### 0.2.5 🟡 Unequal players
First to 3, A trails 1–2, and A wins each round with probability $p = 0.6$. What is A's chance of winning? Is it more or less than
A's chance at 2–1 *ahead* when $p = 0.5$?

### 0.2.6 🟡 Fill Pascal's grid
For first to 3 with fair rounds, compute $v(a, b)$ for every score with $a, b \in \{0, 1, 2\}$, starting from the finish and working
backwards. Check your table against the widget. What is $v(0,0)$, and why must it be that?

### 0.2.7 🔴 Three gamblers
Three players, A, B and C, play rounds, each round won by exactly one of them with probability ⅓ each.
A needs **1** more win, B needs **2**, C needs **2**. The first to reach their target takes the pot.
**(a)** Find each player's fair share.
**(b)** A friend uses Fermat's method: "At most 3 more rounds decide it. Of the $3^3 = 27$ strings, A wins in every
string containing at least one A, which is $27 - 8 = 19$ strings." Why is $19/27$ wrong?

### 0.2.8 🔴 Huygens' dice duel
A and B take turns rolling a pair of dice, with A going first. A wins if A rolls a total of **6** before B rolls a total of **7**.
B wins if B rolls a 7 first. Find $P(\text{A wins})$. (Huygens posed this in 1657. Hint: sum a geometric series, or use
Pascal's trick of conditioning on the first round of turns.)

### 0.2.9 🟣 Why the two methods agree
Let $F(i, j)$ be Fermat's formula: the probability of at least $i$ successes in $i + j - 1$ independent rounds, each a success with probability $p$.
By conditioning on the outcome of the **first** imagined round, show that $F(i, j) = p\,F(i-1, j) + (1-p)\,F(i, j-1)$ for $i, j \ge 1$,
with $F(0, j) = 1$ and $F(i, 0) = 0$. Conclude that Fermat's and Pascal's answers are the same.

### 0.2.10 🔁 Odds on the leader *(callback to 0.1)*
In the 1654 game (first to 3, score 2–1), express A's chance of winning as odds. If a bookmaker offered "evens" on A, which side would you take?

### 0.2.11 💻 Simulate three gamblers
Simulate Exercise 0.2.7 a million times and check your answer to (a).

## Solutions

<details><summary>0.2.1</summary>

A needs 1 win and B needs 2. B wins only by taking both of the next two rounds, with probability $\frac14$.
A gets **75**, B gets **25**.
</details>

<details><summary>0.2.2</summary>

$x = 1$: then $y + z = 8$ with $y, z \in \{1..6\}$: $(2,6),(3,5),(4,4),(5,3),(6,2)$. That's **5**.
$x = 2$: $y + z = 7$: six pairs, $(1,6)$ through $(6,1)$. That's **6**.
In general, for $x$ from 1 to 6 the count of $(y,z)$ with $y + z = 9 - x$ is 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2.
Total $5+6+5+4+3+2 = 25$. ✓

(For sum 10: $y + z = 10 - x$ gives 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, which totals 27.)
</details>

<details><summary>0.2.3</summary>

Outcomes HH, HT, TH, TT are equally likely, and three contain a head: $P = 3/4$.
d'Alembert's case "heads first" lumps together HH and HT, so it is **twice as likely** as each of his other two cases.
Counting unequal cases as equal is the error.
</details>

<details><summary>0.2.4</summary>

A needs 1 and B needs 3, so the game is decided within $1 + 3 - 1 = 3$ rounds.

**Fermat:** of the $2^3 = 8$ strings, A wins unless the string is BBB. $P(A) = 7/8$.

**Pascal:** $v(3,3) = \frac12$, $v(3,2) = \frac12 + \frac12 \cdot \frac12 = \frac34$, $v(3,1) = \frac12 + \frac12 \cdot \frac34 = \frac78$. ✓

A's share: **87.5**. B's: **12.5**.
</details>

<details><summary>0.2.5</summary>

A needs 2 wins and B needs 1, so A must win **both** of the next two rounds: $0.6^2 = 0.36$.

Using the formula: $m = 2 + 1 - 1 = 2$ rounds, and we need $k \ge 2$ A-wins, giving $\binom22 0.6^2 = 0.36$. ✓

It is much **less** than A's 0.75 at 2–1 ahead with fair rounds. Being a better player doesn't make up for trailing so close to the finish.
</details>

<details><summary>0.2.6</summary>

Use $v(a,b) = \frac12 v(a+1,b) + \frac12 v(a,b+1)$, with $v(3,\cdot) = 1$ and $v(\cdot,3) = 0$.

| | a = 0 | a = 1 | a = 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **b = 2** | 1/8 | 1/4 | 1/2 |
| **b = 1** | 5/16 | 1/2 | 3/4 |
| **b = 0** | 1/2 | 11/16 | 7/8 |

$v(0,0) = \frac12$ by **symmetry**: at 0–0 with fair rounds the two players are interchangeable. The diagonal $v(a,a) = \frac12$ for the same reason.
Also notice $v(a,b) + v(b,a) = 1$ (for example $5/16 + 11/16$), which is symmetry again.
</details>

<details><summary>0.2.7</summary>

**(a)** Condition on the first round (Pascal's method).
- A wins it (prob ⅓): A wins.
- B wins it (prob ⅓): now A needs 1, B needs 1, C needs 2. Next round: A wins (⅓), or B wins (⅓), or C wins (⅓). After a C win
  everyone needs 1, so the following round decides it, ⅓ each. So A's chance from here is $\frac13 + \frac13 \cdot \frac13 = \frac49$.
- C wins it (prob ⅓): by symmetry, also $\frac49$ for A.

$P(A) = \frac13 + \frac13 \cdot \frac49 + \frac13 \cdot \frac49 = \frac{9 + 4 + 4}{27} = \mathbf{\frac{17}{27}}$, and by symmetry $P(B) = P(C) = \mathbf{\frac{5}{27}}$.

This 17 : 5 : 5 split is the answer Pascal and Fermat reached in their letters.

**(b)** With three players, "A appears somewhere in 3 rounds" isn't the same as "A gets there first". In the string **BBA**,
B reaches 2 wins after round two, before A's win in round three. The strings where someone else finishes before A's first win are
BBA, BBx, CCA, CCx and so on. Fermat's fiction still works, but you must decide each string by **who reaches their target first**.
Count that way: A wins in 17 of the 27 strings. ✓
</details>

<details><summary>0.2.8</summary>

With two dice: $P(\text{total } 6) = 5/36$ (from (1,5),(2,4),(3,3),(4,2),(5,1)) and $P(\text{total } 7) = 6/36$.

A wins on A's first roll, or after both miss once and A then hits, and so on:

$$
P(A) = \frac{5}{36} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{31}{36}\cdot\frac{30}{36}\right)^k
= \frac{5/36}{1 - \frac{930}{1296}} = \frac{5/36}{366/1296} = \frac{180}{366} = \mathbf{\frac{30}{61}} \approx 0.492.
$$

**Pascal's trick instead:** let $x = P(A)$. In the first pair of turns, A hits (5/36), or both miss ($\frac{31}{36}\cdot\frac{30}{36}$),
which puts you back at the start. So $x = \frac{5}{36} + \frac{930}{1296}\,x$, which gives $x = 30/61$.

Going first almost makes up for A's target being slightly less likely than B's.
</details>

<details><summary>0.2.9</summary>

Picture $i + j - 1$ imagined rounds and look at the first one.
- With probability $p$ it's a success. Then we need at least $i - 1$ successes in the remaining $(i-1) + j - 1$ rounds. That probability is $F(i-1, j)$.
- With probability $1-p$ it's a failure. Then we need at least $i$ successes in the remaining $i + (j-1) - 1$ rounds. That's $F(i, j-1)$.

So $F(i,j) = p\,F(i-1,j) + (1-p)\,F(i,j-1)$.
Boundaries: $F(0,j) = 1$ (needing 0 successes is automatic) and $F(i,0) = 0$ (in $i-1$ rounds you can't get $i$ successes).

Pascal's $v$ satisfies the **same** recursion with the **same** boundary values, via $v(a,b)$ with $i = N - a$ and $j = N - b$.
A recursion like this has exactly one solution: start at the boundary and fill in each value from ones already known. So $F = v$. ∎
</details>

<details><summary>0.2.10</summary>

$P(A) = 3/4$, so the odds against A are $(1/4) : (3/4) = 1$ to 3 against, or "3 to 1 on".
At evens (1 to 1), betting on A gives expected profit $\frac34(1) - \frac14(1) = +0.5$ per \$1. **Back A.**
</details>

<details><summary>0.2.11</summary>

```python
import random
N, wins = 1_000_000, {"A": 0, "B": 0, "C": 0}
for _ in range(N):
    need = {"A": 1, "B": 2, "C": 2}
    while True:
        w = random.choice("ABC")
        need[w] -= 1
        if need[w] == 0:
            wins[w] += 1
            break
print({k: v / N for k, v in wins.items()}, 17 / 27, 5 / 27)
```

You should see roughly A ≈ 0.630 and B ≈ C ≈ 0.185. Compare $17/27 \approx 0.6296$ and $5/27 \approx 0.1852$, with error about ±0.001.
</details>
