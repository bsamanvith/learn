### 0.3.1 🟢 Name the trap

### 0.3.2 🟢 Error bars

### 0.3.3 🟢 Test the extremes

## Solutions

<details><summary>0.3.1</summary>

**(a)** Expecting randomness to look even. This is the gambler's fallacy: the draw has no memory.
**(b)** Linear thinking about pairs. 30 people make 435 pairs, and P(shared birthday) ≈ 71%.
**(c)** Ignoring base rates. If very few passengers carry anything, most alarms are false.
**(d)** Treating unequal cases as equal. "One of each" covers HT and TH, so it has probability 1/2.
</details>

<details><summary>0.3.2</summary>

**(a)** ±0.05, so 0.37 to 0.47. **(b)** ±0.01, so 0.41 to 0.43. **(c)** ±0.001, so 0.419 to 0.421.
</details>

<details><summary>0.3.3</summary>

- **(a)** gives $366/365 > 1$ at $n = 366$. **Wrong.** (It's already silly at $n = 200$: 0.55, when the true value is essentially 1.)
- **(b)** gives $28 \cdot 27/730 \approx 1.04 > 1$ at $n = 28$. **Wrong.**
- **(c)** is 0 at $n = 1$, always lies between 0 and 1, and tends to 1 as $n$ grows. **Survives.**

(c) treats each of the $\binom n2$ pairs as an independent chance of a match with probability $1/365$. The pairs aren't
truly independent, but it is very close: at $n = 23$ it gives 0.500 against the exact 0.507. Chapter 1.3 develops it.
</details>

<details><summary>0.3.4</summary>

- **A:** 23 switches, longest run 2.
- **B:** 13 switches, longest run 5.

**A is the fake.** It switches far more than the expected 14.5 and has no run longer than 2, which is very unusual in 30 real flips.
People asked to "be random" alternate too often because runs *feel* non-random. B looks like real flips.
</details>

<details><summary>0.3.5</summary>

You picked door 1. The prize is behind door 1, 2 or 3 (⅓ each), and Monty opens door 2 or 3 (½ each). That's 6 equally likely cases, each with probability 1/6:

| Prize | Opened | Revealed prize? | Switching wins? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | no | no |
| 1 | 3 | no | no |
| 2 | 2 | **yes** — crossed out | |
| 2 | 3 | no | yes |
| 3 | 2 | no | yes |
| 3 | 3 | **yes** — crossed out | |

Of the 4 surviving equally likely cases, switching wins 2: **½**.
Compare the knowing host. He can't open the prize door, so the "prize 2" case always becomes "opens 3", with probability ⅓ rather than ⅙.
That shift is what produces ⅔.
</details>

<details><summary>0.3.6</summary>

- Sick: 1% of 10,000 = **100**. Positive among them: 90% = **90**.
- Healthy: **9,900**. Positive among them: 9% = **891**.
- All positives: $90 + 891 = 981$. Sick among them: $90/981 \approx$ **9.2%**.

The test is good, but healthy people outnumber sick ones 99 to 1, so their false positives swamp the true ones.
Imagining a population of people ("natural frequencies") makes Bayes' rule (Chapter 2.2) almost obvious.
</details>

<details><summary>0.3.7</summary>

**(a)** One roll of two dice is a double-six with probability $1/36$. No double-six in 24 independent rolls has probability $(35/36)^{24}$. So
$P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - (35/36)^{24} \approx 1 - 0.5086 = \mathbf{0.4914}$.

**(b)** The gap from ½ is 0.0086. For the estimate's error bar to sit clearly below ½, we need roughly $1/\sqrt N \lesssim 0.004$, so
$N \gtrsim 60{,}000$ trials. That de Méré noticed he was losing is either a sign of a *lot* of gambling, or (more likely)
that he had reasoned his way to suspecting it.
</details>

<details><summary>0.3.8</summary>

**(a)** $i = 1$: flips 1 to 6 all heads, probability $(1/2)^6 = 1/64$.
For $2 \le i \le 95$: flip $i-1$ is tails **and** flips $i$ to $i+5$ are heads, probability $(1/2)^7 = 1/128$.

**(b)** $\dfrac{1}{64} + \dfrac{94}{128} = 0.0156 + 0.7344 = 0.75$.

**(c)** Whenever there's at least one run, there's at least one run start, so $P(\text{at least one run}) = P(\#\text{starts} \ge 1)$.
For a count $X \ge 0$ that takes whole-number values, $P(X \ge 1) \le E[X]$, because $E[X] = \sum_k k\,P(X = k) \ge \sum_{k \ge 1} P(X = k)$.
So $P \le 0.75$. The true value, 0.546, is smaller because runs sometimes come several to a sequence.
Still, an expected 0.75 runs of six heads should make it clear that seeing one is not a surprise.
</details>

<details><summary>0.3.9</summary>

**Pigeonhole:** if all $n$ people had different birthdays, we'd need $n$ different days. But only $d < n$ days exist. Contradiction. ∎

**The formula:** the product $d(d-1)\cdots(d-n+1)$ has $n$ factors, counting down from $d$. If $n > d$, one factor is $d - d = 0$,
so the product is 0 and $P = 1 - 0 = 1$. The formula "knows" the pigeonhole principle: there's no way to give $n$ people
distinct days, so the count of ways is 0.
</details>

<details><summary>0.3.10</summary>

The difference is $0.125 - 0.116 \approx 0.009$. Each frequency needs an error of roughly a third of that, about 0.003, to separate them reliably.
That takes $N \approx 1/0.003^2 \approx 100{,}000$ rolls. Even a fraction of that is thousands of evenings of dice.
The story is probably more about gamblers *noticing over generations*, or reasoning it out, than about one person's tally. Either way it's a nice demonstration of how slow $1/\sqrt N$ is.
</details>

<details><summary>0.3.11</summary>

```python
import random
for n in (5, 52, 1000):
    T, total = 20_000, 0
    for _ in range(T):
        p = list(range(n)); random.shuffle(p)
        total += sum(p[i] == i for i in range(n))
    print(n, total / T)
```

All three come out ≈ 1.00. **The exact answer is 1 for every $n$.** Each card is in its original spot with probability $1/n$,
and there are $n$ cards, so $n \cdot \frac1n = 1$. This works even though the events depend on one another. That's linearity of expectation (Chapter 3.3),
probably the most useful trick in probability.
</details>
