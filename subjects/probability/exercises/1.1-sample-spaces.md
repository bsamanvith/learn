# Exercises 1.1 — Sample spaces and events

🟢 warm-up · 🟡 core · 🔴 stretch · 🟣 proof · 🔁 callback · 💻 simulate. Solutions at the bottom.

---

### 1.1.1 🟢 Write the sample space
Give a sample space for each, and say whether it's finite, countably infinite or uncountable.
**(a)** Flip a coin 3 times. **(b)** Roll a die until the first 6, and record the number of rolls. **(c)** The number of emails you receive tomorrow.
**(d)** The time until your phone battery dies. **(e)** Choose a 2-person committee from Ana, Ben, Cy and Di.

### 1.1.2 🟢 English to sets
For events $A$, $B$, $C$, write in set notation: **(a)** at least one occurs; **(b)** none occurs; **(c)** $A$ and $B$ but not $C$;
**(d)** exactly one occurs; **(e)** at most one occurs.

### 1.1.3 🟢 Count on the grid
Two dice. List the outcomes in **(a)** "sum is 5", **(b)** "maximum is 2", **(c)** both. Give each probability.

### 1.1.4 🟢 Not associative
Find a choice of $A, B, C$ (subsets of $\{1,2,3\}$ will do) with $A \cup (B \cap C) \ne (A \cup B) \cap C$.

### 1.1.5 🟡 Three coins, more events
With the 8-sequence sample space, let $H_1$ = "first flip heads", $T_3$ = "third flip tails", $R$ = "the three flips are not all the same".
List **(a)** $H_1 \cap T_3$, **(b)** $R^c$, **(c)** $H_1 \setminus R$, **(d)** $H_1 \triangle T_3$. Give the probability of each.

### 1.1.6 🟡 Indicator algebra
Show that $\mathbb{1}_{A \triangle B} = \mathbb{1}_A + \mathbb{1}_B - 2\,\mathbb{1}_A \mathbb{1}_B$ by checking all four cases.
Then find a similar formula for $\mathbb{1}_{A \cup B \cup C}$ by using $\mathbb{1}_{E^c} = 1 - \mathbb{1}_E$ and De Morgan.

### 1.1.7 🟡 Coarse and fine
A family has two children. Sample space **S1** = {0, 1, 2 girls}. Sample space **S2** = {GG, GB, BG, BB}, listed as (older, younger).
Which of these events can be written as a subset of S1? Which of S2?
**(a)** "exactly one girl" **(b)** "the older child is a girl" **(c)** "at least one boy".

### 1.1.8 🟡 Splitting along a partition
A card is drawn from a standard deck. $A$ = "the card is a face card (J, Q, K)". The suits $S_\clubsuit, S_\diamondsuit, S_\heartsuit, S_\spadesuit$ partition $\Omega$.
Write $A$ as a disjoint union using the partition, and use it to find $P(A)$.

### 1.1.9 🔴 Events about forever
Flip a coin infinitely often, and let $H_n$ = "flip $n$ is heads". Using $\cup$, $\cap$ and complements of the $H_n$, write:
**(a)** "at least one tail"; **(b)** "the first head is on flip 3"; **(c)** "only finitely many tails"; **(d)** "the sequence is eventually all heads".
Are (c) and (d) the same event?

### 1.1.10 🟣 De Morgan for many events
Prove $\left(\bigcup_{i \ge 1} A_i\right)^c = \bigcap_{i \ge 1} A_i^c$.

### 1.1.11 🟣 Distributive law
Prove $A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$ twice: once by element chasing, once with indicators.

### 1.1.12 🔁 d'Alembert's sample space, repaired *(callback to 0.2)*
d'Alembert's three cases for "toss until you get a head, at most two tosses" were $\{\text{H}, \text{TH}, \text{TT}\}$.
That's a valid sample space. Assign the correct probability to each outcome, and recompute $P(\text{at least one head})$.

### 1.1.13 💻 Brute-force the laws
In Python, build $\Omega$ for two dice as a set of tuples. Define several events as subsets and check by brute force that
De Morgan's laws and both distributive laws hold for every choice of three of them.

## Solutions

<details><summary>1.1.1</summary>

**(a)** $\{\text{HHH}, \text{HHT}, \dots, \text{TTT}\}$, 8 sequences: finite.
**(b)** $\{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$: countably infinite. (You could add "never" as an outcome. It turns out to have probability 0.)
**(c)** $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$: countably infinite. In practice there's a bound, but there's no need to pick one.
**(d)** $[0, \infty)$ in hours: uncountable.
**(e)** $\{\{A,B\},\{A,C\},\{A,D\},\{B,C\},\{B,D\},\{C,D\}\}$, 6 committees: finite. Unordered, because a committee has no first member.
</details>

<details><summary>1.1.2</summary>

**(a)** $A \cup B \cup C$. **(b)** $(A \cup B \cup C)^c = A^c \cap B^c \cap C^c$. **(c)** $A \cap B \cap C^c$.
**(d)** $(A \cap B^c \cap C^c) \cup (A^c \cap B \cap C^c) \cup (A^c \cap B^c \cap C)$.
**(e)** "None or exactly one": the union of (b) and (d). Equivalently, "no two occur": $\big((A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C) \cup (B \cap C)\big)^c$.
</details>

<details><summary>1.1.3</summary>

**(a)** $(1,4),(2,3),(3,2),(4,1)$: $4/36 = 1/9$.
**(b)** $(1,2),(2,1),(2,2)$: $3/36 = 1/12$.
**(c)** None. With a maximum of 2 the sum is at most 4, so the events are **disjoint**: probability 0.
</details>

<details><summary>1.1.4</summary>

Take $A = \{1\}$, $B = \{2\}$, $C = \{3\}$. Then $A \cup (B \cap C) = \{1\} \cup \emptyset = \{1\}$, but $(A \cup B) \cap C = \{1,2\} \cap \{3\} = \emptyset$.
Moving brackets between $\cup$ and $\cap$ changes the meaning. Always bracket mixed expressions.
</details>

<details><summary>1.1.5</summary>

**(a)** $H_1 \cap T_3 = \{\text{HHT}, \text{HTT}\}$, $P = 2/8 = 1/4$.
**(b)** $R^c$ = "all the same" $= \{\text{HHH}, \text{TTT}\}$, $P = 1/4$.
**(c)** $H_1 \setminus R = H_1 \cap R^c = \{\text{HHH}\}$, $P = 1/8$.
**(d)** In exactly one of $H_1$, $T_3$: $H_1$ without $T_3$ is $\{\text{HHH}, \text{HTH}\}$, and $T_3$ without $H_1$ is $\{\text{THT}, \text{TTT}\}$.
$P = 4/8 = 1/2$.
</details>

<details><summary>1.1.6</summary>

| In A? | In B? | $\mathbb{1}_A + \mathbb{1}_B - 2\mathbb{1}_A\mathbb{1}_B$ | in $A \triangle B$? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | $1 + 1 - 2 = 0$ | no ✓ |
| 1 | 0 | $1$ | yes ✓ |
| 0 | 1 | $1$ | yes ✓ |
| 0 | 0 | $0$ | no ✓ |

For three events: $\mathbb{1}_{A \cup B \cup C} = 1 - \mathbb{1}_{A^c \cap B^c \cap C^c} = 1 - (1 - \mathbb{1}_A)(1 - \mathbb{1}_B)(1 - \mathbb{1}_C)$.
Expanding:

$$
\mathbb{1}_{A \cup B \cup C} = \mathbb{1}_A + \mathbb{1}_B + \mathbb{1}_C - \mathbb{1}_A\mathbb{1}_B - \mathbb{1}_A\mathbb{1}_C - \mathbb{1}_B\mathbb{1}_C + \mathbb{1}_A\mathbb{1}_B\mathbb{1}_C.
$$

That's **inclusion–exclusion** for three sets, at the level of indicators. Take probabilities (Chapter 3.3) and you get Chapter 1.5's formula for free.
</details>

<details><summary>1.1.7</summary>

**S1** can express (a) as {1} and (c) as {0, 1}, since at least one boy means at most one girl. It **cannot** express (b), because the count of girls doesn't record birth order.
**S2** can express all three: (a) {GB, BG}, (b) {GG, GB}, (c) {GB, BG, BB}.
A finer sample space answers more questions. S2 also has equally likely outcomes, which S1 doesn't ($\frac14, \frac12, \frac14$).
</details>

<details><summary>1.1.8</summary>

$A = (A \cap S_\clubsuit) \cup (A \cap S_\diamondsuit) \cup (A \cap S_\heartsuit) \cup (A \cap S_\spadesuit)$. Each piece is that suit's J, Q and K, so 3 cards, and the pieces are disjoint.
$|A| = 4 \times 3 = 12$, so $P(A) = 12/52 = 3/13$.
</details>

<details><summary>1.1.9</summary>

**(a)** $\bigcup_n H_n^c$.
**(b)** $H_1^c \cap H_2^c \cap H_3$.
**(c)** "From some point on, no tails": $\bigcup_{n \ge 1} \bigcap_{m \ge n} H_m$.
**(d)** "Eventually all heads" is the same description as (c).

So **yes, (c) and (d) are the same event**: only finitely many tails means there's a last tail, and after it everything is heads.
The structure "union over $n$ of intersection over $m \ge n$" is called $\liminf H_n$, the partner of $\limsup$ from the chapter.
</details>

<details><summary>1.1.10</summary>

$\omega \in \left(\bigcup_i A_i\right)^c$
$\iff$ $\omega$ is in **no** $A_i$
$\iff$ for **every** $i$, $\omega \in A_i^c$
$\iff$ $\omega \in \bigcap_i A_i^c$. ∎

("Not (exists $i$ with $\omega \in A_i$)" is "for all $i$, $\omega \notin A_i$". De Morgan is just negating a quantifier.)
</details>

<details><summary>1.1.11</summary>

**Element chasing.** $\omega \in A \cap (B \cup C)$ $\iff$ $\omega \in A$ and ($\omega \in B$ or $\omega \in C$) $\iff$ ($\omega \in A$ and $\omega \in B$) or ($\omega \in A$ and $\omega \in C$) $\iff$ $\omega \in (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$.

**Indicators.** Left side: $\mathbb{1}_A(\mathbb{1}_B + \mathbb{1}_C - \mathbb{1}_B\mathbb{1}_C)$.
Right side: $\mathbb{1}_A\mathbb{1}_B + \mathbb{1}_A\mathbb{1}_C - \mathbb{1}_A\mathbb{1}_B\mathbb{1}_A\mathbb{1}_C$.
Since $\mathbb{1}_A^2 = \mathbb{1}_A$ (because $0^2 = 0$ and $1^2 = 1$), the last term is $\mathbb{1}_A\mathbb{1}_B\mathbb{1}_C$, and the two sides match. ∎
</details>

<details><summary>1.1.12</summary>

$P(\text{H}) = 1/2$, $P(\text{TH}) = 1/4$, $P(\text{TT}) = 1/4$. These sum to 1. ✓
$P(\text{at least one head}) = P(\{\text{H}, \text{TH}\}) = 3/4$.

The lesson: **unequal outcomes are fine**, as long as you give them the right probabilities. The error is only in pretending they're equal.
</details>

<details><summary>1.1.13</summary>

```python
from itertools import product, combinations
Omega = set(product(range(1, 7), repeat=2))
ev = {
    "sum7":  {w for w in Omega if sum(w) == 7},
    "even1": {w for w in Omega if w[0] % 2 == 0},
    "dbl":   {w for w in Omega if w[0] == w[1]},
    "ge10":  {w for w in Omega if sum(w) >= 10},
}
comp = lambda E: Omega - E
for A, B, C in combinations(ev.values(), 3):
    assert comp(A | B) == comp(A) & comp(B)
    assert comp(A & B) == comp(A) | comp(B)
    assert A & (B | C) == (A & B) | (A & C)
    assert A | (B & C) == (A | B) & (A | C)
print("all laws hold")
```

Brute force over a finite $\Omega$ is a *check*, not a proof. It only covers these events. But it catches mistakes fast, and it's how you'd test a conjecture before trying to prove it.
</details>
