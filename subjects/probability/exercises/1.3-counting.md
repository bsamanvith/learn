# Exercises 1.3 — Naive probability and counting

🟢 warm-up · 🟡 core · 🔴 stretch · 🟣 proof · 🔁 callback · 💻 simulate. Solutions at the bottom.

---

### 1.3.1 🟢 Number plates
A plate has 3 letters followed by 3 digits. How many plates are possible? How many if no letter and no digit repeats?

### 1.3.2 🟢 Warm-up counts
Compute **(a)** $\binom{10}{3}$, **(b)** the number of ordered podiums (1st, 2nd, 3rd) from 10 runners, **(c)** the number of distinct arrangements of BANANA.

### 1.3.3 🟢 A committee with a chair
Count the 3-person committees with a designated chair that can be formed from 8 people, in two different ways.

### 1.3.4 🟢 Distinct PIN
A 4-digit PIN is chosen uniformly from all 10,000. What's the probability its digits are all different? That none of them is a 0?

### 1.3.5 🟡 Poker gallery
Find the probability of **(a)** a flush (5 cards of one suit, including straight flushes), **(b)** exactly one pair (ranks like $a, a, b, c, d$ with $a, b, c, d$ distinct).

### 1.3.6 🟡 Diagnose the double count
A friend computes the number of two-pair hands as $13 \cdot \binom42 \cdot 12 \cdot \binom42 \cdot 44$. Evaluate it, compare with the correct $123{,}552$, and explain exactly which hands are counted twice.

### 1.3.7 🟡 Stars and bars
**(a)** How many solutions are there to $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 10$ in non-negative integers? **(b)** In positive integers? *(Hint for (b): give each variable 1 first.)*

### 1.3.8 🟡 The Bose–Einstein trap
Two distinguishable balls are tossed independently into two boxes, each box equally likely. Find $P(\text{one ball in each box})$.
Why is the answer not $1/3$, even though there are three "configurations"?

### 1.3.9 🟡 Urn: with and without replacement
An urn has 6 red and 4 blue balls. Draw 3. Find $P(\text{exactly 2 red})$ **(a)** without replacement, **(b)** with replacement. Which is bigger, and why does that make sense?

### 1.3.10 🟡 Sharing *your* birthday
How many other people must be in the room before it's more likely than not that someone shares **your** birthday? Why is this so different from 23?

### 1.3.11 🟡 Lotto
In 6/49 Lotto you pick 6 numbers from 1 to 49, and 6 are drawn. Find **(a)** $P(\text{jackpot})$, **(b)** $P(\text{exactly 3 of your numbers drawn})$.

### 1.3.12 🔴 Drawer of socks
A drawer holds 10 different pairs of socks (20 socks). You grab 4 at random in the dark. What's the probability you have **no** matching pair?

### 1.3.13 🔴 The lift
7 people enter a lift on the ground floor of a building with 10 upper floors. Each picks a floor uniformly and independently. Find $P(\text{all get off on different floors})$.
How does this relate to the birthday problem?

### 1.3.14 🔴 Where 23 comes from
Using $\ln(1 - x) \approx -x$, show $P(\text{no birthday match among } n) \approx e^{-n(n-1)/(2d)}$. Solve for the $n$ giving ½ when $d = 365$. Then do the same for $d = 10^6$.

### 1.3.15 🟣 Two formulas, proved
**(a)** Prove $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$ by counting ordered selections in two ways.
**(b)** Prove that the number of non-negative integer solutions of $x_1 + \cdots + x_n = k$ is $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$.

### 1.3.16 🔁 At least one ace *(callback to 1.2)*
Use the complement rule to find $P(\text{at least one ace in a 5-card hand})$.

### 1.3.17 💻 Deal a million hands
Simulate random 5-card hands and estimate $P(\text{two pair})$ and $P(\text{full house})$. How many hands do you need before the full-house estimate is good to about 10% (relative)?

<details><summary>1.3.3</summary>

**Chair first:** pick the chair (8 ways), then 2 ordinary members from the other 7: $8 \cdot \binom72 = 168$.
**Committee first:** pick the 3 members ($\binom83 = 56$), then which of them is chair (3 ways): $168$. ✓
Two correct methods agreeing is a good check. It's also the seed of the story proofs in 1.4.

<details><summary>1.3.4</summary>

All different: $\frac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7}{10^4} = 0.504$. No zero: $\left(\frac{9}{10}\right)^4 = 0.6561$.

<details><summary>1.3.5</summary>

**(a)** Choose the suit (4), then 5 of its 13 ranks: $4\binom{13}{5} = 5148$. $P = 5148/2{,}598{,}960 \approx 0.00198$.

**(b)** Choose the pair's rank (13) and its suits ($\binom42 = 6$). Choose the 3 other ranks, which are interchangeable: $\binom{12}{3} = 220$. Choose a suit for each: $4^3 = 64$.
$13 \cdot 6 \cdot 220 \cdot 64 = 1{,}098{,}240$, so $P \approx 0.4226$. Nearly half of all hands have exactly one pair.
</details>

<details><summary>1.3.6</summary>

$13 \cdot 6 \cdot 12 \cdot 6 \cdot 44 = 247{,}104 = 2 \times 123{,}552$.

The method picks a "first pair rank" and then a "second pair rank". The hand Q♠Q♥ 4♦4♣ 9♠ is produced once with queens first and once with fours first.
Every two-pair hand is produced exactly twice, so divide by 2. Equivalently, use $\binom{13}{2}$ for the pair ranks.
(The full-house count doesn't have this problem, because "rank of the triple" and "rank of the pair" are different roles.)
</details>

<details><summary>1.3.7</summary>

**(a)** $k = 10$ stars and $n - 1 = 2$ bars: $\binom{12}{2} = 66$.
**(b)** Set $y_i = x_i - 1 \ge 0$. Then $y_1 + y_2 + y_3 = 7$, giving $\binom{9}{2} = 36$.
</details>

<details><summary>1.3.8</summary>

The four equally likely placements are (A left, B left), (A left, B right), (A right, B left) and (A right, B right). Two of them have one ball in each box: $P = 1/2$.
The three configurations "2–0", "1–1" and "0–2" are a *coarse* sample space. "1–1" covers two equally likely outcomes, and each of the others covers one.
Counting configurations as equally likely is d'Alembert's error again.
</details>

<details><summary>1.3.9</summary>

**(a)** Unordered, without replacement: $\dfrac{\binom62\binom41}{\binom{10}{3}} = \dfrac{15 \cdot 4}{120} = 0.5$.
**(b)** With replacement: choose which of the 3 draws is blue (3 ways), giving $3 \cdot 0.6^2 \cdot 0.4 = 0.432$.

Without replacement the draws "self-correct": drawing a red makes the next red less likely, which pushes results toward the urn's 60/40 mix. "Exactly 2 red out of 3" is the closest you can get to 60% red, so it's more likely.
(These are the **hypergeometric** and **binomial** distributions of Chapter 3.2.)
</details>

<details><summary>1.3.10</summary>

We need $1 - (364/365)^n \ge \frac12$, so $n \ge \frac{\ln 2}{-\ln(364/365)} \approx 252.7$. That means **253** other people.

The difference from 23: here only $n$ pairs count (you with each other person). In the classic problem, **every** pair counts, which gives $\binom{n}{2}$ chances.
253 is about $\binom{23}{2}$, and that's no coincidence.
</details>

<details><summary>1.3.11</summary>

**(a)** $1/\binom{49}{6} = 1/13{,}983{,}816$.
**(b)** Choose 3 of your 6 numbers to be drawn, and 3 of the 43 others: $\dfrac{\binom63\binom{43}{3}}{\binom{49}{6}} = \dfrac{20 \cdot 12{,}341}{13{,}983{,}816} \approx 0.0177$, about 1 in 57.
</details>

<details><summary>1.3.12</summary>

$|\Omega| = \binom{20}{4} = 4845$. **No pair** means the 4 socks come from 4 different pairs. Choose those pairs ($\binom{10}{4} = 210$), then one sock from each ($2^4 = 16$).
$P = \dfrac{210 \cdot 16}{4845} = \dfrac{3360}{4845} \approx 0.693$.

A common error is to count ordered draws in the numerator ($20 \cdot 18 \cdot 16 \cdot 14$) but unordered hands in the denominator. If you count ordered draws, use $20 \cdot 19 \cdot 18 \cdot 17$ on the bottom. You get the same 0.693.
</details>

<details><summary>1.3.13</summary>

$P = \dfrac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4}{10^7} = \dfrac{604{,}800}{10^7} \approx 0.0605$.
It's exactly the birthday problem with $d = 10$ "days" (floors) and $n = 7$ "people": a shared floor is very likely (94%).
</details>

<details><summary>1.3.14</summary>

$\ln P = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \ln(1 - k/d) \approx -\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} k/d = -\frac{n(n-1)}{2d}$.

Set it equal to $\ln \frac12$: $n(n-1) = 2d\ln 2$, so $n \approx \frac12 + \sqrt{2d\ln 2 + \frac14}$.
For $d = 365$: $2 \cdot 365 \cdot 0.693 = 506$, giving $n \approx 22.99$, so **23**.
For $d = 10^6$: $n \approx \frac12 + \sqrt{1{,}386{,}294} \approx 1177.9$, so **1178**. A million possibilities, and a repeat is likely after only about 1200 tries.
</details>

<details><summary>1.3.15</summary>

**(a)** Count ordered selections of $k$ distinct objects from $n$. **Directly:** $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$.
**Via sets:** choose the set ($\binom nk$ ways), then order it ($k!$ ways). So $\binom nk k! = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$. ∎

**(b)** Stars and bars. A solution corresponds to a row of $k$ stars and $n - 1$ bars, where $x_i$ is the number of stars between bar $i-1$ and bar $i$.
The correspondence goes both ways: every arrangement gives a solution, and every solution gives exactly one arrangement.
Arrangements are determined by which $k$ of the $n + k - 1$ positions hold stars: $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$. ∎
</details>

<details><summary>1.3.16</summary>

$P(\text{no ace}) = \binom{48}{5}/\binom{52}{5} = 1{,}712{,}304/2{,}598{,}960 \approx 0.659$. So $P(\text{at least one ace}) \approx 0.341$.
</details>

<details><summary>1.3.17</summary>

```python
import random
from collections import Counter
deck = [(r, s) for r in range(13) for s in range(4)]
N, two, full = 1_000_000, 0, 0
for _ in range(N):
    counts = sorted(Counter(r for r, _ in random.sample(deck, 5)).values())
    two  += counts == [1, 2, 2]
    full += counts == [2, 3]
print(two / N, full / N)      # ≈ 0.0475, 0.00144
```

For a 10% relative error on $p \approx 0.00144$, we need $2\sqrt{p/N} \approx 0.1p$, so $N \approx 400/p \approx 280{,}000$ hands.
Rare events need many trials before their *relative* error is small (compare Exercise 0.1.6).
</details>
