# Exercises 1.5 — Inclusion–exclusion in action

🟢 warm-up · 🟡 core · 🔴 stretch · 🟣 proof · 🔁 callback · 💻 simulate. Solutions at the bottom.

---

### 1.5.1 🟢 Divisible by 3, 5 or 7
How many of the integers $1, \dots, 1000$ are divisible by at least one of 3, 5, 7?

### 1.5.2 🟢 Five derangements
Use the formula $D_n = n!\sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k/k!$ to compute $D_5$. Check that it's $5!/e$ rounded.

### 1.5.3 🟢 Exactly one letter right
4 letters go into 4 addressed envelopes at random. What's the probability that **exactly one** letter lands in its own envelope?

### 1.5.4 🟡 Small faces
Roll a fair die 4 times. What's the probability that each of the faces 1, 2 and 3 shows up at least once?

### 1.5.5 🟡 A void in bridge
A bridge hand is 13 cards from a 52-card deck. Find the probability that the hand is **void** in at least one suit (has no cards of that suit).

### 1.5.6 🟡 All three colours
A bag has equal numbers of red, green and blue balls. Draw 5 times **with replacement**. What's the probability all three colours appear?
Then count the functions from a 5-element set **onto** a 3-element set.

### 1.5.7 🟡 Totients
Compute $\varphi(60)$ and $\varphi(100)$ both by inclusion–exclusion and by the product formula.

### 1.5.8 🟡 Bonferroni at a party
For the hat check with $n = 10$ guests, use $S_1$, $S_2$ and $S_3$ to get upper and lower bounds on $P(\text{at least one match})$. How close is the true value?

### 1.5.9 🔴 The whole match distribution for $n = 4$
For 4 letters in 4 envelopes, find $P(\text{exactly } k \text{ matches})$ for $k = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$. Check they add to 1. Why is $k = 3$ impossible?

### 1.5.10 🔴 The derangement recursion
Prove $D_n = (n-1)\big(D_{n-1} + D_{n-2}\big)$ with a story. *(Hint: guest 1 takes guest $j$'s hat. Does guest $j$ take guest 1's hat?)* Use it to get $D_9$ and $D_{10}$.

### 1.5.11 🔴 Seat the couples
3 married couples sit in a row of 6 chairs at random. What's the probability that **no** couple sits side by side?
*(Hint: let $A_i$ = "couple $i$ is adjacent". Glue an adjacent couple into one block, which can face either way.)*

### 1.5.12 🟣 By induction
Prove the inclusion–exclusion formula for $n$ events by induction on $n$, using only the two-event formula $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(AB)$.

### 1.5.13 🟣 The first Bonferroni lower bound
Prove $P\big(\bigcup A_i\big) \ge S_1 - S_2$ using the counting argument from the chapter: show an outcome lying in exactly $m \ge 1$ events contributes at most 1 to $S_1 - S_2$.

### 1.5.14 🔁 At least one ace, the long way *(callback to 1.3)*
In 1.3.16 you found $P(\text{at least one ace in 5 cards}) \approx 0.341$ by the complement rule. Get it again with inclusion–exclusion over the events $A_s$ = "the ace of suit $s$ is in the hand". Which method would you rather use?

### 1.5.15 💻 Matches are Poisson
Simulate 100,000 random permutations of $n = 20$. Tabulate the number of fixed points and compare with $e^{-1}/k!$. Also compare the sample mean and variance of the count. What do you notice?

$(333 + 200 + 142) - (66 + 47 + 28) + 9 = 675 - 141 + 9 = $ **543**.

<details><summary>1.5.2</summary>

$D_5 = 120\left(1 - 1 + \frac12 - \frac16 + \frac1{24} - \frac1{120}\right) = 60 - 20 + 5 - 1 = $ **44**. And $120/e \approx 44.15$, which rounds to 44 ✓.
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.3</summary>

Choose the one matched letter (4 ways), then derange the other 3 ($D_3 = 2$ ways): $\frac{4 \cdot 2}{24} = \frac13$.
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.4</summary>

Let $M_j$ = "face $j$ never appears", for $j = 1, 2, 3$.
$P(M_1 \cup M_2 \cup M_3) = 3\left(\tfrac56\right)^4 - 3\left(\tfrac46\right)^4 + \left(\tfrac36\right)^4 \approx 1.4468 - 0.5926 + 0.0625 = 0.9167$.
So $P(\text{all three appear}) \approx$ **0.0833**, exactly $\frac{1}{12}$.

*Check by counting.* Four rolls must contain 1, 2, 3, plus one more roll. If the extra roll is 4, 5 or 6: $3 \cdot 4! = 72$ sequences. If it repeats one of 1, 2, 3: $3 \cdot \frac{4!}{2!} = 36$. Total $108/1296 = 1/12$ ✓.
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.5</summary>

Let $V_s$ = "void in suit $s$". Missing one given suit: all 13 cards come from the other 39. Missing two given suits: from 26. Missing three: from 13 (one way). Missing all four is impossible.

$$
P = \frac{4\binom{39}{13} - 6\binom{26}{13} + 4\binom{13}{13}}{\binom{52}{13}} \approx 0.0511.
$$

About 1 hand in 20 has a void.
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.6</summary>

$M_c$ = "colour $c$ never appears". $P(\text{all appear}) = 1 - 3\left(\tfrac23\right)^5 + 3\left(\tfrac13\right)^5 - 0 \approx 1 - 0.3951 + 0.0123 = $ **0.6173**.

Surjections: $3^5 - 3 \cdot 2^5 + 3 \cdot 1^5 = 243 - 96 + 3 = $ **150**, and $150/243 = 0.6173$ ✓. A draw sequence *is* a function from {draw 1, …, draw 5} to colours.
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.7</summary>

**60 = 2² · 3 · 5.** Numbers in $1..60$ divisible by 2, 3 or 5: $(30 + 20 + 12) - (10 + 6 + 4) + 2 = 44$. So $\varphi(60) = 60 - 44 = 16$. Product: $60 \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac23 \cdot \frac45 = 16$ ✓.

**100 = 2² · 5².** Divisible by 2 or 5: $50 + 20 - 10 = 60$. So $\varphi(100) = 40$. Product: $100 \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac45 = 40$ ✓.
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.8</summary>

From the chapter, $S_k = 1/k!$. So
- $S_1 = 1$ (upper bound): $P \le 1$, useless.
- $S_1 - S_2 = 0.5$ (lower bound).
- $S_1 - S_2 + S_3 \approx 0.6667$ (upper bound).

The truth is $1 - 0.36788 = 0.63212$, inside $[0.5, 0.667]$. Adding $-S_4$ gives the lower bound $0.625$, and the bracket keeps shrinking.
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.9</summary>

$P(\text{exactly } k) = \binom4k D_{4-k}/24$:

| $k$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| count | $D_4 = 9$ | $4 \cdot 2 = 8$ | $6 \cdot 1 = 6$ | $4 \cdot 0 = 0$ | $1$ |
| prob | 0.375 | 0.333 | 0.25 | 0 | 0.042 |

$9 + 8 + 6 + 0 + 1 = 24$ ✓. Exactly 3 matches is impossible: if 3 letters are right, the last letter's only remaining envelope is its own ($D_1 = 0$).
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.10</summary>

Consider a derangement of $n$ hats. Guest 1 takes someone else's hat, say guest $j$'s: $n - 1$ choices of $j$. Now two cases:
- **Guest $j$ takes guest 1's hat** (a swap). The other $n - 2$ guests must be deranged among themselves: $D_{n-2}$.
- **Guest $j$ doesn't take guest 1's hat.** Then think of hat 1 as "guest $j$'s forbidden hat". The $n - 1$ guests other than 1 must each avoid one specific hat: $D_{n-1}$.

So $D_n = (n-1)(D_{n-1} + D_{n-2})$. ∎

$D_9 = 8(14833 + 1854) = 133{,}496$. $D_{10} = 9(133{,}496 + 14{,}833) = 1{,}334{,}961$.
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.11</summary>

$|\Omega| = 6! = 720$. Let $A_i$ = "couple $i$ sits together".
- $|A_i|$: glue couple $i$ into a block (2 orientations), then arrange 5 units: $2 \cdot 5! = 240$.
- $|A_i A_j|$: two blocks, $2^2 \cdot 4! = 96$.
- $|A_1A_2A_3|$: $2^3 \cdot 3! = 48$.

$|A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3| = 3 \cdot 240 - 3 \cdot 96 + 48 = 480$. So $P(\text{no couple adjacent}) = 240/720 = \frac13$.
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.12</summary>

The case $n = 2$ is given. Suppose the formula holds for any $n - 1$ events. Write $B = A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_{n-1}$. By the two-event formula,

$$
P(B \cup A_n) = P(B) + P(A_n) - P(B \cap A_n), \qquad B \cap A_n = \bigcup_{i<n}(A_i \cap A_n).
$$

Apply the induction hypothesis to $P(B)$ (events $A_1, \dots, A_{n-1}$) and to $P(B \cap A_n)$ (events $A_1A_n, \dots, A_{n-1}A_n$).
The first gives every intersection that **doesn't** include $A_n$, with the right sign. The second, after the minus sign, gives every intersection of $k \ge 2$ events that **does** include $A_n$, with sign $-(-1)^{(k-1)+1} = (-1)^{k+1}$. Together with $P(A_n)$, that's every nonempty intersection with sign $(-1)^{k+1}$. ∎
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.13</summary>

An outcome in exactly $m$ of the events is counted $\binom m1 = m$ times in $S_1$ and $\binom m2$ times in $S_2$. Its net contribution to $S_1 - S_2$ is
$m - \frac{m(m-1)}{2} = \frac{m(3 - m)}{2}$, which is $1$ for $m = 1, 2$ and $\le 0$ for $m \ge 3$.
So every outcome contributes at most 1, and outcomes in the union contribute exactly 1 to $P(\bigcup A_i)$. Summing, $S_1 - S_2 \le P(\bigcup A_i)$. ∎

(The same argument with $\sum_{k \le r}(-1)^{k+1}\binom mk$ gives all the Bonferroni inequalities.)
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.14</summary>

$P(A_s) = 5/52$ (the ace of $s$ is one of your 5 cards). For a given set of $k$ aces all in the hand: $\frac{5 \cdot 4 \cdots (5-k+1)}{52 \cdot 51 \cdots (52-k+1)}$.

$$
P = 4 \cdot \frac{5}{52} - 6 \cdot \frac{20}{52 \cdot 51} + 4 \cdot \frac{60}{52 \cdot 51 \cdot 50} - \frac{120}{52 \cdot 51 \cdot 50 \cdot 49} \approx 0.3846 - 0.0452 + 0.0018 - 0.00002 = 0.3412. \checkmark
$$

The complement rule is one line, so prefer it. Inclusion–exclusion is the fallback when "none" is hard.
</details>

<details><summary>1.5.15</summary>

See [`sims/derangements.py`](../sims/derangements.py). The core:

```python
import numpy as np
from math import exp, factorial
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, N = 20, 100_000
fixed = np.array([(rng.permutation(n) == np.arange(n)).sum() for _ in range(N)])
for k in range(6):
    print(k, (fixed == k).mean().round(4), round(exp(-1) / factorial(k), 4))
print(fixed.mean(), fixed.var())   # both ≈ 1
```

The frequencies match Poisson(1) to within simulation noise. The mean and variance are both about 1, a Poisson fingerprint.
(The mean is exactly 1 for every $n$, by linearity: each guest matches with probability $1/n$. The variance is also exactly 1 for $n \ge 2$. Chapter 3.3 shows why.)
</details>
