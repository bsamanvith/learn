# 0.1 Three meanings of "probability"

> *"Probability is the most important concept in modern science, especially as nobody has
> the slightest notion what it means."* — attributed to Bertrand Russell

## 1. Hook: what does "70% chance of rain" mean?

The forecast says **70% chance of rain tomorrow**. Tomorrow will happen exactly once. It will
either rain or it won't. So what is the 70% *about*?

Try a few more:

- A fair coin has probability ½ of heads. Is that a fact about the **coin**?
- I flip the coin and cover it with my hand. I've seen it; you haven't. Is it still ½ — for you? For me?
- "There's a 30% chance this startup succeeds." There's only one startup. Can you repeat it?
- A die is rolled and hidden under a cup. It is *already* a 6 or not. Is P(6) still 1/6?

Every one of these makes sense in everyday talk, but they don't all mean the same thing.
There are three main answers to "what is probability?", plus one minority view, and a surprise
at the end: **for the maths, it doesn't matter which one you pick.**

## 2. Intuition: three ways of looking

### The classical view: count the equally likely cases

*Laplace, 1814.* If an experiment has $N$ equally likely outcomes and $k$ of them are
"good", then

$$
P(\text{good}) = \frac{k}{N}.
$$

A die has 6 faces, 3 are even, so $P(\text{even}) = 3/6$. This is the view behind all of
Part 1's counting.

**What's wrong with it:** "equally likely" means "equally probable", so the definition is
circular. And it can't handle a loaded die, or anything without a natural list of
symmetric outcomes ("chance of rain").

### The frequentist view: what happens in the long run

*Venn, von Mises, 19th–20th century.* Repeat the experiment many times. The probability of an
event is the fraction of times it happens **in the long run**.

> 🎛 **Play:** [coin convergence widget](../widgets/coin-convergence.html) — flip thousands of
> coins and watch the running fraction of heads settle. (Run `python3 -m http.server` from the
> repo root and open `http://localhost:8000/subjects/probability/widgets/coin-convergence.html`.)

This is the view behind every simulation in this book. When in doubt, we run the experiment
10,000 times on a computer and count.

**What's wrong with it:** some events can't be repeated, like tomorrow's weather, a particular
election, or whether a specific patient has a disease. "The long run" is infinite, and we
never get there. And the answer depends on *which* repetitions you count, which is called
the **reference class problem**. Is the patient one of "all adults", "all 40-year-old
smokers", or "all 40-year-old smokers with this gene"? Each group gives a different frequency.

### The Bayesian (subjective) view: how strongly you believe it

*Ramsey, de Finetti, 1920s–30s.* Probability measures **your degree of belief**, given what
you know. You can make it concrete with bets: your probability for $A$ is the price you
consider fair for a ticket that pays \$1 if $A$ happens.

This view handles the cup and the forecast without fuss. Before you peek, the die under the
cup is 1/6 *for you*. After you peek, it's 0 or 1. Different people with different
information can rationally hold different probabilities. Part 2's Bayes' rule is the
machinery for **updating** beliefs when evidence arrives.

**What's wrong with it:** isn't science supposed to be objective? If probability lives in
your head, what stops you believing anything you like? (The answer is coherence, below. It
stops you believing *inconsistent* things, though not *silly* ones.)

### The propensity view (bonus)

*Popper, 1950s.* Probability is a physical **tendency** of a setup to produce outcomes,
like a fragility or a mass. A radioactive atom has a propensity to decay within an hour,
even though it only decays once. Physicists often talk this way about quantum mechanics.

## 3. Definition: relative frequency

Here is the one quantity every view agrees we can measure. Repeat an experiment $n$ times
and let $n_A$ be the number of times event $A$ happens. The **relative frequency** of $A$ is

$$
f_n(A) = \frac{n_A}{n}.
$$

The frequentist *defines* probability as the long-run value of $f_n(A)$. The Bayesian
*expects* $f_n(A)$ to land near their probability. The classical theorist *predicts*
$k/N$. They all care about this number.

Now look at what $f_n$ always does, whatever the experiment:

1. $0 \le f_n(A) \le 1$, because you can't happen fewer than 0 times or more than $n$ times.
2. $f_n(\Omega) = 1$, where $\Omega$ ("omega") is the event "something happens".
3. If $A$ and $B$ can't happen together, then $f_n(A \text{ or } B) = f_n(A) + f_n(B)$.

**Keep these three in mind.** In Chapter 1.2 they become the three **axioms of
probability**. Kolmogorov's axioms are just the rules frequencies obey anyway.

## 4. Worked example: frequencies from 60 rolls

A die is rolled 60 times:

| Face | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Count | 8 | 11 | 9 | 12 | 7 | 13 |

**(a)** $f_{60}(\text{even}) = (11 + 12 + 13)/60 = 36/60 = 0.60$.

**(b)** $f_{60}(\text{at least 5}) = (7 + 13)/60 = 20/60 \approx 0.333$.

**(c)** "Even" and "exactly 1" can't both happen. Check rule 3:
$f(\text{even or 1}) = (36 + 8)/60 = 44/60$, and $f(\text{even}) + f(1) = 36/60 + 8/60$. ✓

> *Why does rule 3 hold?* Each roll lands in at most one of the two events, so no roll is
> counted twice when you add the counts.

**(d)** "Even" and "at least 5" *can* both happen (a 6 is in both). Here
$f(\text{even}) + f(\text{at least 5}) = 56/60$, but $f(\text{even or at least 5}) = (11+12+7+13)/60 = 43/60$.
The 13 sixes were counted twice: $56 - 13 = 43$. ✓ That's inclusion–exclusion, which we
meet properly in 1.2.

**(e)** Is the die fair? Classical says each face should show $60/6 = 10$ times. We got
between 7 and 13. Is that suspicious? With only 60 rolls, wiggles of about
$\sqrt{60 \cdot \tfrac16 \cdot \tfrac56} \approx 2.9$ are normal (Part 6 explains why), so no.

## 5. Faded example: turning odds into probabilities

Bookmakers quote **odds against**. "5 to 1 against" means a winning \$1 bet pays \$5 profit.
If that's a fair bet, the chance of winning is $p$, where

$$
\underbrace{p \cdot 5}_{\text{expected win}} = \underbrace{(1-p) \cdot 1}_{\text{expected loss}}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad p = \frac{1}{6}.
$$

In general, odds of $a$ to $b$ against means $p = \dfrac{b}{a+b}$.

## 6. Quick checks

1. Which view has the most trouble with "probability that it rained in Rome on 1 March 44 BC"? Which has the least?
2. You flip a coin 10 times and get 7 heads. What is $f_{10}(\text{heads})$? Does that mean $P(\text{heads}) = 0.7$?
3. In 200 trials, $f(A) = 0.3$ and $f(B) = 0.25$, and $A$, $B$ never happen together. What is $f(A \text{ or } B)$?

1. Frequentist: it isn't repeatable. Bayesian: it's a degree of belief given historical records.
2. 0.7. No: with 10 flips, wiggles of ±0.15 are routine.
3. 0.55.

## 7. Theory

### Coherence: why beliefs must follow the rules

A Bayesian can believe anything, provided the beliefs are **coherent**. The punishment for
incoherence is the **Dutch book**: a set of bets you'd accept one by one that together lose
money *whatever happens*.

**Example.** Suppose you think "rain tomorrow" is worth 0.6 and "no rain tomorrow" is also worth 0.6.
I sell you both tickets at your prices for \$1.20 in total. Exactly one of them pays \$1.
You lose \$0.20, guaranteed.

**Theorem (Ramsey–de Finetti, informally).** Your betting prices avoid every Dutch book if
and only if they satisfy the same three rules as relative frequencies. So the subjective
view arrives at the same rules by a completely different road.

We prove the "$P(A) + P(A^c) = 1$" piece in Exercise 0.1.11.

### How fast frequencies settle (preview)

The widget shows that $f_n$ never stops wiggling, but the wiggles shrink. The typical
distance between $f_n(A)$ and the true $p$ is about

$$
\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} \le \frac{1}{2\sqrt n}.
$$

**Practical rule for this whole book:** a simulation with $N$ trials estimates a probability
to within about $\pm 1/\sqrt N$, for 95% of runs. 10,000 trials gives about ±0.01, and
1,000,000 trials gives about ±0.001. To get one more decimal place, you need **100 times** more trials.

(Proved in Part 6 as the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem.)

### One set of rules, several meanings

In 1933 Kolmogorov wrote down axioms for probability (Chapter 1.2) without saying what
probability *means*. The mathematics only needs the rules. So:

- We use **counting** (classical) when outcomes are symmetric: Part 1.
- We use **frequencies** for intuition and for simulation: everywhere.
- We use **beliefs** when we update on evidence: Part 2 and Bayesian statistics.

## 8. Pitfall: the gambler's fallacy

> "Ten reds in a row on the roulette wheel. Black is *due*."

The law of large numbers says the *fraction* of reds approaches its probability. It does
**not** say the wheel compensates for the past. The wheel has no memory.

So how does the fraction settle down if the past isn't corrected? The past gets
**swamped**, not cancelled. Say that after 100 fair coin flips you have 60 heads, an excess of
10 over the expected 50. After a million more flips, the expected excess is *still 10*, because
future flips are fair and add zero excess on average. But the fraction is now about
$(500{,}060)/(1{,}000{,}100) \approx 0.50001$. The 10 hasn't gone anywhere. It's just tiny
compared with a million.
