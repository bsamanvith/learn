# 0.3 Your intuition is broken (on purpose)

Humans are superb at many things. Probability is not one of them. That's not an insult. It's
the reason this subject is fun: almost every chapter has an answer that feels wrong and
isn't. This chapter shows you a collection of them first, and then gives you the tools that
never get fooled.

## 1. Hook: guess first

Write down your gut answer to each of these **before** reading on. Don't calculate. Just guess.

1. **Birthdays.** How likely is it that two people in a room of 23 share a birthday?
2. **Monty Hall.** Three doors, one prize. You pick a door. The host, who knows where the prize is, opens another door to show no prize, and offers to let you switch. Should you?
3. **Hats.** 10 people's hats are returned at random. How likely is it that *nobody* gets their own?
4. **Streaks.** In 100 fair coin flips, how likely is a run of 6 or more heads in a row somewhere?
5. **The test.** A disease affects 1% of people. A test catches 90% of cases, but also flags 9% of healthy people. You test positive. How likely is it that you have the disease?
6. **Two children.** A family has two children, at least one of them a girl. How likely is it that both are girls?
7. **Fixed points.** Shuffle a deck of 52 cards. On average, how many cards end up in their original position?

> 🎛 **Play:** [predict, then simulate](../widgets/predict-then-simulate.html). Commit to a
> guess with a slider, then watch 10,000 simulated trials expose it.

## 2. The answers (and the chapter that explains each)

| # | Most people guess | Answer | Why the gut fails | Explained in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ~5% | **50.7%** | We think about people (23) instead of **pairs** (253) | 1.3 |
| 2 | "doesn't matter, 50–50" | **switch: 2/3** | The host's choice is *constrained*, so it carries information | 2.5 |
| 3 | "gets small as n grows" | **36.8% ≈ 1/e**, for *any* large n | Two effects cancel almost exactly | 1.5 |
| 4 | ~5% | **54.6%** | True randomness is streakier than we imagine | Exercise 0.3.8 |
| 5 | 90% | **≈ 9.2%** | We ignore the **base rate**: healthy people vastly outnumber sick ones | 2.2 |
| 6 | 1/2 | **1/3** | "At least one is a girl" is less informative than it sounds | 2.1 |
| 7 | "about 0" | **exactly 1** (for any deck size) | Linearity of expectation doesn't care about dependence | 3.5 |

Look at the "why" column. The failures fall into a few families:

- **Linear thinking about combinations.** The number of pairs grows like $n^2/2$, not $n$. (Birthday)
- **Ignoring how information was produced.** A host who must avoid the prize tells you more than one who opens a door at random. (Monty Hall, two children)
- **Ignoring base rates.** A small error rate applied to a huge group beats a high hit rate applied to a tiny group. (Medical test)
- **Expecting randomness to look "even".** Real random sequences clump. Human-made fake ones alternate too much. (Streaks)
- **Treating unequal cases as equal.** d'Alembert's and Leibniz's mistake (0.2). Not in this list, but behind many of them.

Psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman catalogued these patterns in the 1970s. One of
them they called the **"law of small numbers"**: the belief that small samples should look
like the population they came from.

## 3. Definition: a simulation

Since intuition fails, we need a referee. Ours is the **Monte Carlo simulation**:

> A **simulation** estimates $P(A)$ by (1) writing a program that performs one random trial of
> the experiment, (2) repeating it $N$ times, and (3) reporting the fraction of trials in which
> $A$ happened. By 0.1, the answer is within about $\pm 1/\sqrt N$ of the truth.

A simulation **doesn't need you to be clever.** It needs you to be *precise* about the
experiment. That turns out to be the hard part. See the pitfall in §8.

## 4. Worked example: simulating the birthday problem

**Step 1: model one trial.** "A room of 23 people with random birthdays. Is there a match?"

```python
import random

def one_room(n=23, days=365):
    birthdays = [random.randrange(days) for _ in range(n)]   # each person: a day 0..364
    return len(set(birthdays)) < n                           # a set drops duplicates
```

> *Why does `len(set(...)) < n` detect a match?* A set keeps one copy of each value. If two
> people share a day, the set is smaller than the list.

**Step 2: repeat.** **Step 3: count.**

```python
N = 100_000
hits = sum(one_room() for _ in range(N))
print(hits / N)          # ≈ 0.507
```

**Step 4: report the error.** With $N = 100{,}000$ the estimate is good to about
$\pm 1/\sqrt{100{,}000} \approx \pm 0.003$. So the answer is $0.507 \pm 0.003$: more likely
than not, and nowhere near 5%.

**Step 5: get suspicious.** Check an extreme case you know. With `n=1` the answer must be 0.
With `n=366` it must be 1. If those fail, the code is wrong.

## 5. Faded example: de Méré's first bet

The Chevalier de Méré bet even money that he'd get **at least one six in four rolls** of a die.
Fill in the simulation:

```python
def one_trial():
    rolls = [random.randint(1, ___) for _ in range(___)]
    return ___ in rolls

N = 100_000
print(sum(one_trial() for _ in range(N)) / ___)
```

<details><summary>Check</summary>

`randint(1, 6)`, `range(4)`, `6 in rolls`, and divide by `N`. You'll get ≈ 0.518. The exact value is
$1 - (5/6)^4 \approx 0.5177$ (Chapter 1.3). That's a small edge, enough to make de Méré money over many evenings.
</details>

## 6. Quick checks

1. A simulation with 2,500 trials gives 0.31. Roughly what range is the true probability in?
2. Which of the seven puzzles have answers that *don't depend on n* once n is large?
3. You write a birthday simulation and get 0.507 for `n=23` but 0.12 for `n=366`. What do you conclude?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. $1/\sqrt{2500} = 0.02$, so about 0.29 to 0.33.
2. Hats (→ 1/e) and fixed points (exactly 1).
3. There's a bug. With 366 people a match is certain (the pigeonhole principle), so the answer must be 1.
</details>

## 7. Theory: four habits that beat intuition

These habits run through the whole book.

1. **Write down the sample space.** List the outcomes, or describe them precisely. Most paradoxes vanish here. (Chapter 1.1)
2. **Check the outcomes are equally likely before counting.** Label everything that could be told apart: first and second child, red and blue die. (Chapters 0.2 and 1.3)
3. **Ask how the information was produced.** "At least one is a girl" depends on *how* you learned it. A host who knows where the prize is behaves differently from one who doesn't. (Part 2)
4. **Simulate, then prove.** The simulation tells you *what* is true. The proof tells you *why*. Neither is finished without the other.

And a fifth, for checking any answer:

5. **Test the extremes.** What does your formula say when $n = 1$? When $p = 0$ or $1$? When everything is symmetric? A formula that gives 1.3 or −0.2 anywhere is wrong.

## 8. Pitfall: the simulation simulates *your model*, not reality

Here's a Monty Hall simulation with a subtle bug:

```python
def one_game():
    prize, pick = random.randrange(3), random.randrange(3)
    opened = random.choice([d for d in range(3) if d != pick])   # host opens a random other door
    if opened == prize:
        return None                                               # host revealed the prize: discard
    switch_to = 3 - pick - opened
    return switch_to == prize
```

Throw away the `None` games and this gives **½** for switching, not ⅔. The code is correct, but
it simulates a *different game*, one in which the host doesn't know where the prize is and
happened by luck not to reveal it. In that game, switching really does win only half the time.

The real host **never** opens the prize door: `opened = choice of doors that are neither pick nor prize`.
That change in the model is exactly the information that makes switching win ⅔.

> **Lesson:** a simulation is only as good as its description of the experiment. If two
> reasonable simulations disagree, the *question* was ambiguous. Find out which one was meant.
