# 1.1 Sample spaces and events

Every probability question hides a set of possible outcomes. Most mistakes come from never
writing it down. This chapter gives the language: **sample spaces**, **events**, and the set
operations that turn English ("or", "and", "not", "at least one") into something precise.

## 1. Hook: three coins, two answers

Flip three fair coins. What's the probability of exactly two heads?

**Student A:** "The number of heads is 0, 1, 2 or 3. That's four possibilities, so $1/4$."

**Student B:** "There are 8 sequences: HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT. Exactly two heads
happens in 3 of them, so $3/8$."

Both listed **every possible outcome**. Both used "favourable over total". They can't both be right.
Student B is right, and the reason is the subject of this chapter: A's list of outcomes is a
fine **sample space**, but its outcomes aren't equally likely.

## 2. Intuition: outcomes are points, events are regions

Think of the **sample space** as a map of everything that could happen, one point per possible
outcome. An **event** is a region on that map. When the experiment runs, nature picks one point.
The event "happens" if the point lands inside the region.

> 🎛 **Play:** [event explorer](../widgets/event-explorer.html). Two dice, 36 squares. Pick
> events A and B, then light up "A or B", "A and B", "not A" and so on. Compare two expressions
> side by side to discover De Morgan's laws for yourself.

For two dice, the map is a 6×6 grid. "Sum is 7" is a diagonal line of six squares. "Doubles" is
the other diagonal. "First die is even" is three columns. Combining events is just combining regions.

## 3. Definitions

**Experiment:** any process with an uncertain result.
**Outcome:** one complete result of the experiment, written $\omega$ (little omega).
**Sample space** $\Omega$ (big omega): the set of all possible outcomes.
**Event:** a subset $A \subseteq \Omega$. We say **$A$ occurs** if the actual outcome $\omega$ is in $A$.

Sample spaces come in three sizes:

| Experiment | $\Omega$ | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Roll a die | $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ | finite |
| Flip until the first head; record the number of flips | $\{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ | countably infinite |
| Lifetime of a light bulb in hours | $[0, \infty)$ | uncountable |
| Throw a dart at the unit square | $[0,1] \times [0,1]$ | uncountable |

### The translation table

| English | Set notation | Region |
|---|---|---|
| $A$ or $B$ (or both) | $A \cup B$ (union) | everything in either |
| $A$ and $B$ | $A \cap B$ (intersection) | the overlap |
| not $A$ | $A^c$ (complement) | everything outside $A$ |
| $A$ but not $B$ | $A \setminus B = A \cap B^c$ | $A$ with the overlap removed |
| exactly one of $A$, $B$ | $A \triangle B = (A \setminus B) \cup (B \setminus A)$ | both "moons" |
| if $A$ happens, $B$ happens | $A \subseteq B$ | $A$ sits inside $B$ |
| $A$ and $B$ can't both happen | $A \cap B = \emptyset$ (**disjoint**, or mutually exclusive) | no overlap |
| impossible / certain | $\emptyset$ / $\Omega$ | nothing / everything |
| at least one of $A_1, A_2, \dots$ | $\bigcup_i A_i$ | |
| all of $A_1, A_2, \dots$ | $\bigcap_i A_i$ | |

> In maths, **"or" is always inclusive**: "$A$ or $B$" includes the case where both happen.

A **partition** of $\Omega$ is a collection of disjoint events whose union is all of $\Omega$, so
exactly one of them happens. Example: "the die shows 1 or 2", "3 or 4", "5 or 6".

## 4. Worked example: two dice

$\Omega = \{(i, j) : i, j \in \{1, \dots, 6\}\}$, 36 equally likely ordered pairs.
Let $A$ = "sum is 7" and $B$ = "first die is even".

- $A = \{(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)\}$, 6 outcomes.
- $B$ = all pairs with $i \in \{2,4,6\}$, 18 outcomes.
- $A \cap B = \{(2,5),(4,3),(6,1)\}$, 3 outcomes.
- $A \cup B$: $6 + 18 - 3 = 21$ outcomes. *(Why subtract 3? Those outcomes are in both lists. We'd count them twice.)*
- $(A \cup B)^c$: $36 - 21 = 15$ outcomes: "the sum isn't 7 **and** the first die is odd".

That last line is a **De Morgan law** in action: "not (A or B)" = "(not A) and (not B)".
In the widget, put $(A \cup B)^c$ on the left and $A^c \cap B^c$ on the right. Same 15 squares.

Since all 36 outcomes are equally likely, $P(A) = 6/36 = 1/6$ and $P(A \cup B) = 21/36 = 7/12$.

## 5. Faded example: three coins

$\Omega$ = the 8 sequences from the hook. Let $H_1$ = "first flip is heads", $E$ = "exactly two heads", $L$ = "at least two heads".

1. $E = \{\text{HHT}, \text{HTH}, \text{THH}\}$ and $L = E \cup \{\underline{\qquad}\}$.
2. $E \subseteq L$ because $\underline{\qquad\qquad}$.
3. $H_1 \cap E = \{\underline{\qquad\qquad}\}$.
4. $L^c$ in words: $\underline{\qquad\qquad}$. As a set: $\{\underline{\qquad\qquad}\}$.

<details><summary>Check</summary>

1. $\{\text{HHH}\}$. 2. Exactly two heads is in particular at least two. 3. $\{\text{HHT}, \text{HTH}\}$.
4. "At most one head" $= \{\text{HTT}, \text{THT}, \text{TTH}, \text{TTT}\}$. That's 4 of 8, so $P(L) = 1/2$, which is also clear by symmetry between heads and tails.
</details>

## 6. Quick checks

1. Rolling one die, are "even" and "at least 4" disjoint?
2. Write "neither A nor B" in set notation, two ways.
3. For the three-coin hook, give a sample space with **two** outcomes. Is it useful?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. 4 and 6 are in both.
2. $(A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c$.
3. For example $\{\text{at least two heads}, \text{at most one head}\}$. It's a valid sample space, but it can't answer "exactly two heads?" Coarse sample spaces answer fewer questions.
</details>

## 7. Theory

### De Morgan's laws

**Theorem.** For any events $A$, $B$ (and likewise for any collection $A_1, A_2, \dots$):

$$
(A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c, \qquad (A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c.
$$

*Proof (element chasing).* $\omega \in (A \cup B)^c$
$\iff$ $\omega$ is not in $A \cup B$
$\iff$ $\omega$ is not in $A$ **and** not in $B$
$\iff$ $\omega \in A^c \cap B^c$.
The second law follows by applying the first to $A^c$ and $B^c$ and taking complements. ∎

In words: **to negate, swap "and"/"or" and negate each piece.** "Not (rich and famous)" means "not rich, or not famous".

### Distributive laws

$$
A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C), \qquad A \cup (B \cap C) = (A \cup B) \cap (A \cup C).
$$

These are like $a(b+c) = ab + ac$, except here it works both ways round. (Exercise 1.1.11.)

### Splitting an event with a partition

For any events $A, B$:

$$
A = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap B^c), \quad \text{and the two pieces are disjoint}.
$$

More generally, if $B_1, \dots, B_n$ partition $\Omega$, then $A = \bigcup_i (A \cap B_i)$ with disjoint pieces.
This innocent identity becomes the **law of total probability** in Chapter 2.2, one of the most-used tools in the subject.

### Indicators: events as 0/1 numbers

The **indicator** of $A$ is the function $\mathbb{1}_A(\omega) = 1$ if $\omega \in A$, and $0$ otherwise.
Set operations become arithmetic:

$$
\mathbb{1}_{A^c} = 1 - \mathbb{1}_A, \qquad
\mathbb{1}_{A \cap B} = \mathbb{1}_A \mathbb{1}_B, \qquad
\mathbb{1}_{A \cup B} = \mathbb{1}_A + \mathbb{1}_B - \mathbb{1}_A \mathbb{1}_B.
$$

Check the last one in each of the four cases (in both, only $A$, only $B$, neither). Indicators turn
proofs about sets into algebra. They are the engine of inclusion–exclusion (1.5) and of
expectation tricks (3.3).

### Infinitely many events (a glimpse of depth)

Flip a coin forever, and let $H_n$ = "flip $n$ is heads".

- "Heads eventually" $= \bigcup_{n \ge 1} H_n$.
- "Heads on every flip" $= \bigcap_{n \ge 1} H_n$.
- "**Infinitely many** heads" $= \bigcap_{n \ge 1} \bigcup_{m \ge n} H_m$: for every $n$, there's a head at some $m \ge n$.

That last construction is called $\limsup H_n$. It is the main character of the Borel–Cantelli
lemmas (Chapter 8.5). Countable unions and intersections are why the axioms (1.2) need to handle
infinitely many events.

### Choosing a sample space

The same experiment has many valid sample spaces. Two guidelines:

1. **Fine enough to answer the question.** "Number of heads" can't tell you whether the first flip was heads.
2. **Equally likely if you plan to count.** Ordered sequences of coins or dice are. Totals and patterns usually aren't (0.2).

A coarser sample space isn't *wrong*. Student A's $\{0,1,2,3\}$ is fine, provided you assign
probabilities $\frac18, \frac38, \frac38, \frac18$ instead of $\frac14$ each.

## 8. Pitfalls

- **"Disjoint" is not "independent".** Disjoint events are extremely *dependent*: if one happens, the other certainly doesn't. (More in 2.3.)
- **Exclusive "or".** "A or B" includes both. If you mean "exactly one", say $A \triangle B$.
- **Outcome vs event.** The outcome is $6$. The event is $\{6\}$. $P$ is applied to events: $P(\{6\})$, which everybody writes as $P(6)$ anyway.
- **Unequal outcomes counted as equal.** Student A's mistake, d'Alembert's mistake, Leibniz's mistake.
