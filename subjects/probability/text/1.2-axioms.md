# 1.2 The axioms of probability

In 0.1, three philosophies argued about what probability *means*. Kolmogorov's 1933 move was to
stop arguing: write down the **rules** every sensible notion of probability obeys, and derive
everything from those. There are three rules. From them we'll prove the complement rule,
inclusion–exclusion, the union bound, and a surprise about "random integers".

## 1. Hook: is this forecast even possible?

A weather app says:

- Chance of rain **Saturday**: 60%
- Chance of rain **Sunday**: 60%
- Chance of rain on **both** days: 10%

Nothing looks wrong. Every number is between 0 and 1. But this forecast is **impossible**:
no world, no weather model and no amount of luck could make all three true. By the end of the
chapter you'll be able to prove it in one line, and say how small "both days" is allowed to be.

## 2. Intuition: probability behaves like area

Picture $\Omega$ as a square with total area 1. Events are regions, and $P(A)$ is the area of $A$.
Area already obeys the rules we want:

- areas are never negative;
- the whole square has area 1;
- if two regions don't overlap, the area of both together is the sum of their areas.

> 🎛 **Play:** [area = probability](../widgets/venn-area.html). Drag two circles around the
> square. Watch $P(A) + P(B)$ overshoot $P(A \cup B)$ by exactly the purple overlap. Throw darts
> to *estimate* the area by frequency. Then add a third circle.

Compare with 0.1: these are the same three properties that relative frequencies had. Area,
frequency and coherent belief all obey them. So we make them the axioms.

## 3. Definition: Kolmogorov's axioms

A **probability measure** on a sample space $\Omega$ is a function $P$ that assigns a number to each event, such that:

1. **Non-negativity:** $P(A) \ge 0$ for every event $A$.
2. **Normalisation:** $P(\Omega) = 1$.
3. **Countable additivity:** if $A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots$ are pairwise disjoint ($A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ for $i \ne j$), then

$$
P\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\Big) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i).
$$

The triple $(\Omega, \text{events}, P)$ is a **probability space**. That's it. Everything in this
book, up to and including martingales, is a consequence of these three lines.

> **Small print, for later.** For uncountable $\Omega$ (like the unit square), it turns out we
> *can't* assign a probability to every subset without contradiction. We have to restrict to a
> well-behaved family of events called a **σ-algebra**. Every set you'll ever meet naturally is in
> it. Chapter 8.1–8.2 tells the story. Until then, "event" means "any reasonable subset".

### Two ways to build a probability space

**Finite, equally likely (the classical case).** If $\Omega$ is finite and all outcomes are equally likely,

$$
P(A) = \frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}.
$$

This satisfies the axioms (check: sizes are $\ge 0$, $|\Omega|/|\Omega| = 1$, and the sizes of disjoint sets add).
So the classical view is one special case of the axioms. Chapter 1.3 is about computing $|A|$.

**Countable, with weights.** If $\Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2, \dots\}$, pick weights $p_i \ge 0$ with $\sum_i p_i = 1$, and set
$P(A) = \sum_{\omega_i \in A} p_i$. This satisfies the axioms, and every probability on a countable set looks like this.

## 4. Worked examples

### (a) The forecast

Let $A$ = rain Saturday and $B$ = rain Sunday. By inclusion–exclusion (proved in §7),

$$
P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) = 0.6 + 0.6 - 0.1 = 1.1.
$$

A probability greater than 1 is impossible (also §7), so the forecast is inconsistent. ∎

How small *can* "both days" be? We need $P(A \cup B) \le 1$:

$$
P(A \cap B) \ge P(A) + P(B) - 1 = 0.2.
$$

So if each day is 60%, then **at least 20%** for both days is forced. (This is **Bonferroni's inequality**.)
The largest possible value is 0.6, reached when it rains Sunday exactly when it rains Saturday.

> *Why must the days overlap?* Two regions of area 0.6 inside a square of area 1 can't both fit
> without overlapping by at least 0.2. Try it in the widget.

### (b) First head on an even flip

Flip a fair coin until the first head. $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ (the flip on which the first head appears), with
$p_n = P(\text{first head on flip } n) = (1/2)^n$, because that needs $n-1$ tails and then a head.

*Check it's a valid probability:* $\sum_{n \ge 1} 2^{-n} = \frac12 + \frac14 + \frac18 + \cdots = 1$. ✓

The event "first head on an even flip" is $\{2, 4, 6, \dots\}$. By countable additivity,

$$
P(\text{even}) = \sum_{k \ge 1} 2^{-2k} = \frac14 + \frac1{16} + \cdots = \frac{1/4}{1 - 1/4} = \frac13.
$$

So odd is twice as likely as even. That makes sense: flip 1 alone already contributes ½ to "odd".

## 5. Faded example

$P(A) = 0.5$, $P(B) = 0.4$, $P(A \cap B) = 0.2$. Fill in:

1. $P(A \cup B) = 0.5 + 0.4 - \underline{\quad} = \underline{\quad}$
2. $P(\text{neither}) = 1 - P(A \cup B) = \underline{\quad}$
3. $P(A \text{ but not } B) = P(A) - P(A \cap B) = \underline{\quad}$
4. $P(\text{exactly one}) = P(A \setminus B) + P(B \setminus A) = \underline{\quad} + \underline{\quad} = \underline{\quad}$

## 6. Quick checks

1. Can disjoint events have $P(A) = 0.7$ and $P(B) = 0.4$?
2. If $A \subseteq B$, which is bigger, $P(A)$ or $P(B)$?
3. A loaded die has $P(k)$ proportional to $k$. What is $P(6)$?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. Disjoint means $P(A \cup B) = 1.1 > 1$.
2. $P(B)$ (monotonicity).
3. The weights $1, \dots, 6$ sum to 21, so $P(6) = 6/21 = 2/7$.
</details>

## 7. Theory: consequences of the axioms

Each result is proved from the three axioms and earlier results only. These proofs are the
first "real" mathematics in the book, so read them slowly.

**Theorem 1 ($P(\emptyset) = 0$).**
*Proof.* Take $A_1 = \Omega$ and $A_2 = A_3 = \cdots = \emptyset$. These are pairwise disjoint, and their union is $\Omega$.
Axiom 3 gives $P(\Omega) = P(\Omega) + \sum_{i \ge 2} P(\emptyset)$. So $\sum_{i\ge2} P(\emptyset) = 0$, and since each term is $\ge 0$, $P(\emptyset) = 0$. ∎

**Theorem 2 (finite additivity).** If $A_1, \dots, A_n$ are disjoint, then $P(A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(A_i)$.
*Proof.* Pad the list with $A_{n+1} = A_{n+2} = \cdots = \emptyset$ and use Axiom 3 and Theorem 1. ∎

**Theorem 3 (complement rule).** $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$.
*Proof.* $A$ and $A^c$ are disjoint with union $\Omega$, so $P(A) + P(A^c) = 1$. ∎
> The most useful line in probability. "At least one" questions are almost always easier as $1 - P(\text{none})$.

**Theorem 4 (monotonicity).** If $A \subseteq B$, then $P(A) \le P(B)$. In particular, $P(A) \le 1$.
*Proof.* $B = A \cup (B \setminus A)$, with the pieces disjoint. So $P(B) = P(A) + P(B \setminus A) \ge P(A)$. Take $B = \Omega$ for the second claim. ∎

**Theorem 5 (inclusion–exclusion for two events).** $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$.
*Proof.* Split into three disjoint pieces: $A \setminus B$, $A \cap B$ and $B \setminus A$. Then
$P(A) = P(A \setminus B) + P(A \cap B)$ and $P(B) = P(B \setminus A) + P(A \cap B)$, while
$P(A \cup B) = P(A \setminus B) + P(A \cap B) + P(B \setminus A)$. Substitute the first two into the third. ∎

**Theorem 6 (union bound, or Boole's inequality).** $P\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right) \le \sum_i P(A_i)$, for finitely or countably many events.
*Proof.* Make the events disjoint: $B_1 = A_1$, $B_2 = A_2 \setminus A_1$, $B_3 = A_3 \setminus (A_1 \cup A_2)$, and so on.
Then $\bigcup B_i = \bigcup A_i$, the $B_i$ are disjoint, and $B_i \subseteq A_i$. So $P(\bigcup A_i) = \sum P(B_i) \le \sum P(A_i)$. ∎
> The union bound is crude but never wrong, and it works with *no* information about how the events overlap.
> It's the workhorse of computer science and of the probabilistic method.

**Theorem 7 (Bonferroni).** $P(A \cap B) \ge P(A) + P(B) - 1$. More generally, $P\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^n A_i\right) \ge 1 - \sum_{i=1}^n P(A_i^c)$.
*Proof.* Apply the union bound to the complements, then use De Morgan: $P\left(\bigcup A_i^c\right) \le \sum P(A_i^c)$. ∎

### Going deeper: continuity of probability

**Theorem 8.** If $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \cdots$ (a growing sequence of events), then

$$
P\Big(\bigcup_n A_n\Big) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n).
$$

*Proof.* Slice the growing events into disjoint "rings": $R_1 = A_1$ and $R_n = A_n \setminus A_{n-1}$. Then $A_n = R_1 \cup \cdots \cup R_n$
and $\bigcup_n A_n = \bigcup_n R_n$. By countable additivity,
$P(\bigcup A_n) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty P(R_n) = \lim_{N\to\infty} \sum_{n=1}^N P(R_n) = \lim_N P(A_N)$. ∎

This is exactly why Axiom 3 asks for *countable* additivity and not just finite additivity: it lets
probabilities pass through limits. That's essential for Parts 6–8. Example: $P(\text{a head eventually}) = \lim_n P(\text{a head within } n \text{ flips}) = \lim_n (1 - 2^{-n}) = 1$.

### There is no "random positive integer"

Can we pick a positive integer "uniformly at random", with every $n$ equally likely? Suppose
$P(\{n\}) = c$ for every $n$. Countable additivity gives
$1 = P(\Omega) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty c$, which is $0$ if $c = 0$ and $\infty$ if $c > 0$. **Neither is 1.** So no such probability exists.
Questions like "the probability that a random integer is even" need a different meaning (a limit of
$\frac1N |\{n \le N : n \text{ even}\}|$, called natural density). That isn't a probability measure.

## 8. Pitfalls

- **Adding probabilities of overlapping events.** $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$ *only* when $A$ and $B$ are disjoint. The overlap gets counted twice otherwise. The hook's forecast fails exactly this way.
- **"Probability 0 means impossible."** Not for infinite sample spaces. A dart hitting the exact centre of the square has probability 0 (a point has no area), but it's a possible outcome. Likewise "probability 1" means "almost surely", not "certainly".
- **Uniform on infinite sets.** "Pick a random integer" or "a random real number" has no meaning without saying more. "Uniform on [0, 1]" is fine. "Uniform on all reals" isn't.
