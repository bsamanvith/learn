# 1.3 Naive probability and counting

When all outcomes are equally likely, probability is counting: $P(A) = |A|/|\Omega|$. That sounds
easy. It isn't, because the numbers get astronomically large almost immediately, and because there are
many plausible-looking ways to count the same thing wrong. This chapter is a toolkit of four
counting formulas, plus one trick: **count in an order, then divide out the order you didn't mean.**

## 1. Hook: the birthday problem

How many people must be in a room before it's more likely than not that two share a birthday?

Most people guess around 180 (half of 365). The answer is **23**. At 50 people it's 97%, and at
70 it's 99.9%.

> 🎛 **Play:** [birthday widget](../widgets/birthday.html). Slide the number of people and watch the
> probability shoot up. Fill random rooms and spot the collisions on a calendar.

Why so few? With 23 people there are $23 \times 22 / 2 = 253$ **pairs**, and every pair is a chance for a
match. By the end of this section you'll compute the exact answer yourself.

## 2. Intuition: every choice multiplies

Picture a tree. You pick a shirt (3 options), then trousers (4 options), then shoes (2 options). Each
shirt branches into 4 trousers, and each of those branches into 2 shoes. The number of leaves, which is
the number of outfits, is $3 \times 4 \times 2 = 24$.

**Multiplication rule.** If a process happens in $k$ steps, with $n_1$ options at step 1, $n_2$ at step 2
(whatever happened at step 1), and so on, then there are $n_1 n_2 \cdots n_k$ possible outcomes.

The fine print matters: the *number* of options at each step must not depend on earlier choices,
even if *which* options are available does. Choosing a president and then a vice-president from 10
people gives $10 \times 9$. The VP options change depending on who became president, but there are always 9 of them.

## 3. Definitions: the four ways to choose

Pick $k$ objects from $n$. Two questions decide the formula. **Does order matter?** **Can you pick the same object twice?**

| | **Order matters** (sequences) | **Order doesn't matter** (sets) |
|---|---|---|
| **With replacement** | $n^k$ | $\displaystyle\binom{n+k-1}{k}$ *(stars and bars; outcomes not equally likely!)* |
| **Without replacement** | $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) = \dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}$ | $\displaystyle\binom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$ |

Examples with $n = 10$ digits and $k = 3$:
- **Ordered, with replacement:** 3-digit PIN codes: $10^3 = 1000$.
- **Ordered, without replacement:** gold, silver and bronze among 10 runners: $10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 = 720$.
- **Unordered, without replacement:** a 3-person committee from 10: $\binom{10}{3} = 120$.
- **Unordered, with replacement:** buying 3 scoops from 10 flavours (repeats allowed): $\binom{12}{3} = 220$.

Notation: $n! = n(n-1)\cdots 2 \cdot 1$ ("$n$ factorial"), with $0! = 1$. $\binom{n}{k}$ is read "$n$ choose $k$".

### Overcounting, then dividing

**The most useful idea in counting:** if each thing you want gets counted exactly $m$ times by an easier
method, count the easy way and divide by $m$.

- **Committees.** Ordered picks of 3 from 10: $720$. Each committee $\{A,B,C\}$ appears $3! = 6$ times (ABC, ACB, BAC, …).
  So there are $720 / 6 = 120$ committees. That's where $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!\,k!}$ comes from.
- **Anagrams.** MISSISSIPPI has 11 letters: M×1, I×4, S×4, P×2. Pretend all 11 are different: $11!$ arrangements.
  Swapping identical letters gives the same word, so divide by $4!\,4!\,2!$:
  $\dfrac{11!}{1!\,4!\,4!\,2!} = 34{,}650$. This is a **multinomial coefficient**.

## 4. Worked examples

### (a) The birthday problem, exactly

Assume 365 equally likely birthdays, independent across people. Count **ordered** lists of birthdays, one per person.

- $|\Omega| = 365^n$ (ordered, with replacement).
- "No match" means all $n$ birthdays differ: $365 \cdot 364 \cdots (365 - n + 1)$ (ordered, without replacement).

$$
P(\text{at least one match}) = 1 - \frac{365 \cdot 364 \cdots (365-n+1)}{365^n}
= 1 - \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1 - \frac{k}{365}\right).
$$

At $n = 23$ this is $0.507$. *(Why the complement? "At least one match" includes one pair, two pairs, a triple, and so on, which is a mess.
"No match" is one clean count.)*

### (b) A full house in poker

Five cards from 52. Unordered, without replacement: $|\Omega| = \binom{52}{5} = 2{,}598{,}960$ equally likely hands.

A full house is three of one rank and two of another, like K K K 7 7. Build it step by step:

1. Choose the rank for the triple: 13 ways.
2. Choose 3 of its 4 suits: $\binom43 = 4$.
3. Choose a *different* rank for the pair: 12 ways.
4. Choose 2 of its 4 suits: $\binom42 = 6$.

$$
P(\text{full house}) = \frac{13 \cdot 4 \cdot 12 \cdot 6}{2{,}598{,}960} = \frac{3744}{2{,}598{,}960} \approx 0.00144,
$$

about 1 hand in 694.

> *Why is "13 then 12" right here, when it's wrong for two pair (below)?* The triple and the pair play
> **different roles**. KKK77 and 777KK are different hands, and the step-by-step count produces each hand exactly once.

### (c) De Méré's first bet

At least one 6 in four rolls. Ordered with replacement: $|\Omega| = 6^4$. "No 6" leaves $5^4$ outcomes. So
$P = 1 - (5/6)^4 \approx 0.518$. That's slightly better than even money, and de Méré made money on it.

## 5. Faded example: two pair

A hand like Q Q 4 4 9: two different pairs plus a fifth card of a third rank.

1. Choose the two ranks for the pairs. The two pairs play the **same role**, so the order of choosing them doesn't matter: $\binom{13}{\underline{\ \ }} = \underline{\quad}$.
2. Choose suits for each pair: $\binom{4}{2}^{\underline{\ \ }} = \underline{\quad}$.
3. Choose the fifth card from a rank that is neither pair: $\underline{\quad}$ cards.
4. $P(\text{two pair}) = \underline{\quad}/2{,}598{,}960 \approx \underline{\quad}$.

<details><summary>Check</summary>

1. $\binom{13}{2} = 78$. 2. $6^2 = 36$. 3. $52 - 8 = 44$. 4. $78 \cdot 36 \cdot 44 = 123{,}552$, giving $\approx 0.0475$ (about 1 in 21).

**Tempting wrong answer:** $13 \cdot 6 \cdot 12 \cdot 6 \cdot 44$, copying the full-house method. That counts QQ44 9 once as "queens first" and once as "fours first",
so it's exactly **twice** too big. Steps with interchangeable roles must use $\binom{}{}$, not ordered choices.
</details>

## 6. Quick checks

1. How many ways can 5 people line up?
2. How many 5-card hands contain no hearts?
3. A 4-digit PIN with random digits: what's the chance all its digits are different?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. $5! = 120$. 2. $\binom{39}{5} = 575{,}757$. 3. $\frac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7}{10^4} = 0.504$.
</details>

## 7. Theory

### Why the formulas are true

**Ordered with replacement ($n^k$):** $k$ steps with $n$ options each, by the multiplication rule.

**Ordered without replacement:** $n$ options for the first pick, $n - 1$ for the second, …, $n - k + 1$ for the $k$-th.

**Unordered without replacement:** each $k$-element set corresponds to exactly $k!$ orderings, so
$\binom{n}{k} \cdot k! = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$. Divide by $k!$.

**Unordered with replacement (stars and bars).** Choosing $k$ items from $n$ types, with repeats, is the same
as choosing how many of each type: $x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n = k$ with each $x_i \ge 0$. Write $k$ stars and separate them into
$n$ groups with $n - 1$ bars. For example, 3 scoops from 4 flavours, "2 vanilla, 0 chocolate, 1 mint, 0 lemon", is `★★||★|`.
Every arrangement of $k$ stars and $n-1$ bars in a row of $k + n - 1$ symbols is one choice, and vice versa.
So the count is $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$, the number of ways to choose which positions hold stars.

### The birthday approximation

Using $1 - x \approx e^{-x}$ for small $x$:

$$
P(\text{no match}) = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1 - \frac{k}{d}\right) \approx \exp\left(-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{k}{d}\right) = \exp\left(-\frac{n(n-1)}{2d}\right).
$$

The exponent is (number of pairs) / (number of days). Setting $P = \frac12$ gives $n(n-1) \approx 2d\ln 2$, so
$n \approx \sqrt{2d \ln 2} \approx 1.18\sqrt d$. For $d = 365$ that's about 22.99. **Collisions show up at around the square root of the number of possibilities.**
This "birthday bound" is why a hash function with $2^{128}$ outputs only gives about $2^{64}$ worth of collision resistance.

### Stirling's approximation

Factorials explode. $52! \approx 8 \times 10^{67}$, more than the number of atoms in the Earth. For estimates, use

$$
n! \approx \sqrt{2\pi n}\,\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n.
$$

It's 1.7% off at $n = 5$, 0.8% at $n = 10$, and 0.16% at $n = 52$. The relative error shrinks like $1/(12n)$. We'll prove it, using the CLT, in Part 6.

## 8. Pitfalls

1. **Mixing conventions.** If the denominator counts ordered hands, the numerator must count ordered hands too. Pick one convention and use it throughout.
2. **Overcounting interchangeable roles.** Two pair (above). A test: does your method produce the same hand twice? Take a specific hand and trace how your count generates it.
3. **Stars and bars outcomes are not equally likely.** Throw 2 *distinguishable* balls into 2 boxes at random. The configurations (2,0), (1,1) and (0,2) are **not** each ⅓. The (1,1) case happens two ways (ball A left and B right, or the reverse), so it has probability ½.
   (Strangely, some quantum particles, bosons, really do follow equally likely stars-and-bars statistics. That's **Bose–Einstein** statistics. Coins and dice don't.)
4. **Forgetting the complement.** "At least one" problems almost always go through $1 - P(\text{none})$.

- **← 1.2:** naive probability $|A|/|\Omega|$ is the equally likely case of the axioms.
