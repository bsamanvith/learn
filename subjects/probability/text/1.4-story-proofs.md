# 1.4 Story proofs

Some identities are easy to *check* but feel like magic. Grinding through factorials
confirms them without explaining them. A **story proof** explains them: show that both sides
count the **same collection of things**, counted in two different ways. Once you see the story,
you can't unsee it, and you can usually rebuild the identity from memory years later.

## 1. Hook: why is the 6th row of Pascal's triangle so neat?

```
                1
              1   1
            1   2   1
          1   3   3   1
        1   4   6   4   1
      1   5  10  10   5   1
    1   6  15  20  15   6   1
```

Look at the bottom row:
- It's **symmetric**: 1, 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1.
- It **sums to 64** $= 2^6$.
- The **alternating sum** $1 - 6 + 15 - 20 + 15 - 6 + 1$ is **0**.
- Each number is the **sum of the two above it**.
- The **squares** sum to $1 + 36 + 225 + 400 + 225 + 36 + 1 = 924 = \binom{12}{6}$.

Every one of these facts has a one-sentence story. By the end of this chapter you'll know them all.

## 2. Intuition: count walks on a grid

> 🎛 **Play:** [lattice paths](../widgets/lattice-paths.html). Walk from the bottom-left corner to the
> top-right, one step right or up at a time. Each dot shows how many routes reach it.

A route with $a$ right-steps and $b$ up-steps is a word like `RRURUR…` made of $a$ R's and $b$ U's.
Choosing where the R's go determines the route, so there are $\binom{a+b}{a}$ routes. The same
number counts committees, subsets and coin-flip sequences. In the widget, notice two things:

1. **Every dot's number is the sum of the dot to its left and the dot below it.** You arrive from one or the other. That's Pascal's rule.
2. **Every route crosses the purple diagonal at exactly one dot.** Sort routes by where they cross and you count all of them a second way. That's Vandermonde's identity.

## 3. Definition: a story proof

> A **story proof** (or **double-counting**, or **combinatorial** proof) of an identity $L = R$ describes
> one set $S$, then shows that counting $S$ one way gives $L$ and counting it another way gives $R$.

A close cousin is the **bijective proof**: show $|S| = |T|$ by matching every element of $S$ with exactly one element of $T$ and vice versa.

Here's the template:
1. **Interpret** one side as counting something concrete: committees, paths, words, subsets.
2. **Recount** the same things by splitting into cases or by building them in a different order.
3. **Check** that every object is counted exactly once on each side. No overlaps, nothing missed.

## 4. Worked examples

### (a) Symmetry: $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$

**Story.** Choosing the $k$ people who are **on** a committee is the same as choosing the $n - k$ people who are **off** it. ∎

### (b) Pascal's rule: $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k}$

**Story.** Count $k$-person committees from $n$ people, one of whom is Fred.
- Committees **with** Fred: choose the other $k - 1$ from the remaining $n - 1$, giving $\binom{n-1}{k-1}$.
- Committees **without** Fred: choose all $k$ from the other $n - 1$, giving $\binom{n-1}{k}$.

Every committee either contains Fred or doesn't, so the two cases add up to $\binom nk$. ∎

*(Compare the algebra: $\frac{(n-1)!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!} + \frac{(n-1)!}{k!(n-k-1)!}$, common denominator, simplify… correct, and it tells you nothing.)*

### (c) Vandermonde's identity: $\displaystyle\binom{m+n}{k} = \sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{m}{j}\binom{n}{k-j}$

**Story.** A club has $m$ maths students and $n$ physics students. Choose a $k$-person committee.
- Directly: $\binom{m+n}{k}$.
- By cases on $j$, the number of maths students on the committee: choose them ($\binom mj$), then $k - j$ physics students ($\binom{n}{k-j}$). The cases $j = 0, 1, \dots, k$ don't overlap and cover everything. ∎

In the widget, the "maths students" are the first $t$ steps of the route and the "physics students" are the last $a + b - t$ steps.

### (d) Team captain: $k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$

**Story.** Count teams of $k$ (from $n$ people) **with a captain**.
- Choose the team, then its captain: $\binom nk \cdot k$.
- Choose the captain first ($n$ ways), then the other $k - 1$ members from the remaining $n - 1$: $n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$. ∎

(You already did this with $n = 8$, $k = 3$ in Exercise 1.3.3.)

## 5. Faded example: the hockey stick

**Claim:** $\displaystyle\sum_{j=k}^{n}\binom{j}{k} = \binom{n+1}{k+1}$. On Pascal's triangle this is a diagonal run of numbers adding up to the number just below and beside its end, like a hockey stick.

**Story.** Count the $(k+1)$-element subsets of $\{1, 2, \dots, n+1\}$.
- Directly: $\binom{n+1}{\underline{\quad}}$.
- By cases on the **largest** element, call it $j + 1$. The other $k$ elements must be chosen from $\{1, \dots, \underline{\quad}\}$, which can be done in $\binom{\underline{\ \ }}{k}$ ways.
- The largest element can be anything from $\underline{\quad}$ (the subset needs $k$ elements below it) up to $n + 1$. So $j$ runs from $\underline{\quad}$ to $n$.

<details><summary>Check</summary>

$\binom{n+1}{k+1}$; the others come from $\{1, \dots, j\}$, giving $\binom{j}{k}$; the largest is at least $k + 1$, so $j$ runs from $k$ to $n$. ∎

With $k = 1$: $\sum_{j=1}^{n} j = \binom{n+1}{2} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$. That's the famous "add 1 to 100" formula, as a story.
</details>

## 6. Quick checks

1. Give a one-line story for $\binom n2 = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$.
2. How many routes go from $(0,0)$ to $(5,3)$? How many of those pass through $(2,1)$?
3. What's the coefficient of $x^3 y^5$ in $(x+y)^8$?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Handshakes: each of $n$ people shakes $n - 1$ hands, and each handshake gets counted by both people involved, so divide by 2.
2. $\binom83 = 56$. Through $(2,1)$: $\binom31 \cdot \binom{5}{3} = 3 \cdot 10 = 30$ (get there, then finish from there).
3. $\binom83 = 56$.
</details>

## 7. Theory

### The binomial theorem

$$
(x + y)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k} x^k y^{n-k}.
$$

**Story proof.** $(x+y)^n = (x+y)(x+y)\cdots(x+y)$. Expanding means choosing either $x$ or $y$ from each of the $n$ brackets
and multiplying. You get $x^k y^{n-k}$ whenever you choose $x$ from exactly $k$ brackets, and there are $\binom nk$ ways to pick which ones. ∎

Two special cases do a lot of work:

- $x = y = 1$: $\displaystyle\sum_k \binom nk = 2^n$. **Story:** each element of an $n$-set is either in a subset or not, giving $2^n$ subsets, grouped by size.
- $x = -1, y = 1$ (with $n \ge 1$): $\displaystyle\sum_k (-1)^k \binom nk = 0$. **Story:** an $n$-set has as many even-sized subsets as odd-sized ones.
  Pair each subset with the subset you get by toggling element 1 in or out. Toggling changes the size by exactly 1, so it swaps even and odd sizes, and the pairing is perfect.

That alternating sum is the secret engine of inclusion–exclusion in the next chapter.

### Sum of squares

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}^2 = \binom{2n}{n}.
$$

**Story.** This is Vandermonde with $m = n$ and $k = n$, after using symmetry: $\binom nk^2 = \binom nk \binom{n}{n-k}$.
Choosing $n$ people from $n$ maths students and $n$ physics students, by cases on how many maths students are chosen.

### Multinomial coefficients

The number of ways to split $n$ labelled objects into groups of sizes $k_1, \dots, k_r$ (with $\sum k_i = n$) is

$$
\binom{n}{k_1, k_2, \dots, k_r} = \frac{n!}{k_1!\,k_2!\cdots k_r!},
$$

and $(x_1 + \cdots + x_r)^n = \sum \binom{n}{k_1, \dots, k_r} x_1^{k_1}\cdots x_r^{k_r}$, by the same choose-from-each-bracket story.
(MISSISSIPPI from 1.3 is the split of 11 positions into groups of sizes 1, 4, 4, 2.)

### Why probabilists care

- $\binom nk / 2^n$ is the probability of exactly $k$ heads in $n$ fair flips. The row sum $2^n$ is exactly the statement that these probabilities add to 1.
- Vandermonde, divided by $\binom{m+n}{k}$, says the **hypergeometric** probabilities add to 1 (Chapter 3.2).
- The team-captain identity gives the average number of heads in $n$ flips: $\sum_k k\binom nk / 2^n = n 2^{n-1}/2^n = n/2$ (Exercise 1.4.6). This is our first **expectation** (Chapter 3.3).

## 8. Pitfalls

- **A story that overcounts isn't a proof.** "Choose a captain, then choose the rest of the team from everyone" counts the captain twice if they get picked again. Always ask: could the same object be produced twice?
- **Mismatched objects.** Both sides must count *exactly the same* set. Check with tiny cases ($n = 2$ or $3$) by listing.
- **Off-by-one ranges.** In the hockey stick, the smallest possible maximum is $k + 1$, not 1. Boundary cases are where stories break.
